Suppose you had to build a transformer that denoises video — you would be forced, step by step, into exactly this design: compress pixels into grids of 16-number cells, cut the grids into tiles lined up as a list of 5120-wide tokens, denoise that list with a stack of shape-preserving blocks at every noise level, and generate chunk by chunk — each chunk reading only finished ones — which is what lets a video model close a robot’s control loop.

Invent it yourself — and a map of this page

Start from what you already own: a language model. It reads a list of tokens and predicts the next one. Now I hand you a robot and a camera and say: make this thing watch the world and act. What breaks? Three things — and each fix turns out to be a rename, not an invention. Words become latent patches (compressed pieces of video); next-token prediction becomes denoising (predict the flow-matching velocity for every noisy token — see Flow Matching); and the decoder-only causal mask becomes block-causal over chunks: a chunk being denoised may read every finished chunk, never the future. Everything else — attention, MLPs, residuals, KV caching — you keep untouched; it is the same machinery LLMs use.

We will make those three moves in order, and I will try to convince you each one is forced. Part 1 builds the object the model actually reads, following 1.6 s of real video from pixels to tokens with every shape shown. Part 2 makes the video encoder causal in time. Part 3 opens one DiT block with dimensions on every wire. Part 4 chains chunks together with masks and a cache. Part 5 shows who actually does the denoising. Every term is defined at first use and collected in the Glossary.

Part 1 · What can a transformer eat? Follow 1.6 seconds of video

Here is your first problem, and it is not optional. A transformer cannot eat an image, a video, or a “16 × 60 × 104” anything. It eats exactly one kind of object — a list of same-length vectors (the tokens) — and nothing a camera hands you looks like that. So before any architecture, keep asking the only honest question: this shape becomes what? You know where the answer must end — a list of 5120-long vectors — so let us watch it happen, holding on to one concrete object the whole way: 1.6 seconds of one 832 × 480 camera view.

Step 1 — space: pixel blocks become cells. Count what you are holding first: one frame is 480 × 832 pixels × 3 colors — about 1.2 M numbers — and attention over that is hopeless. You are forced to compress before you tokenize. So the VAE encoder slides over each frame and replaces every 8 × 8 block of pixels (8 · 8 · 3 = 192 raw numbers) with one cell: 16 learned numbers that summarize the block’s content — edges, color, texture — instead of storing every pixel. A 480 × 832 frame therefore shrinks to a 60 × 104 grid of cells (480 ÷ 8 and 832 ÷ 8). And that is all the phrase “a small grid of vectors” means, and all the shape notation means: 16 × 60 × 104 = (numbers per cell) × (grid rows) × (grid columns).

Step 2 — time: frames merge into latent frames. Video is redundant in time too — frame 5 mostly repeats frame 4 — so the same encoder compresses along time as well: each group of 4 consecutive frames merges into one latent frame. (How it manages that without peeking at the future is a genuine problem; it gets the whole of Part 2.) Our 8 raw frames become K = 2 latent frames:

Step 3 — format: the grid becomes a list. You have compressed — but look at what you hold: a 16 × 60 × 104 slab, and the transformer still refuses it, because a slab is not a list of same-length vectors. Only one move is left: cut. Cut the 60 × 104 grid into 2 × 2 tiles — the notation “patch 1 × 2 × 2” just names the tile’s size as (time × height × width): 1 latent frame thick, 2 cells tall, 2 cells wide. That yields 30 × 52 = 1,560 tiles per latent frame. Each tile contains 4 cells × 16 numbers = 64 numbers; flatten them into one row, multiply by a single learned 5120 × 64 matrix (the patch embedding), and you have one token of 5120 numbers:

100%

Step 4 — the company the video keeps. Now notice what the list format bought you: anything you can turn into a 5120-wide row rides in the same list, no special cases. So the chunk’s 3,120 video tokens are joined by 48 action tokens — the action encoder maps each of the chunk’s 48 motor actions (a vector of normalized joint deltas) to its own 5120-wide token, so actions are simply 48 more rows — plus the instruction’s text tokens, a proprioception token from the state encoder, and the cached tokens of past chunks. Here is the full ledger of what became what — check every row:

stageshapehow many numbers
raw chunk (8 frames)3 × 8 × 480 × 8329.58 M
latent chunk (K = 2)2 × (16 × 60 × 104)199,680 (≈ 48× smaller)

Read the last two rows again, slowly — they dissolve the “becomes what” confusion in one glance, and they surprise most people. Patchify compresses nothing: 3,120 × 64 is the same 199,680 numbers as the latent chunk, just regrouped into a list — do the multiplication yourself. And the embedding then expands each 64-vector into the model’s 5120-wide working space, which looks wasteful until you say each stage’s job out loud: compression was the VAE’s job; patchify changes the format; the embedding spends size to buy a shape attention can operate on.

Part 2 · Now forbid the future — and win an exact anchor

Part 1 left you a debt: the encoder merges every 4 frames into 1, and your robot has to encode video as it streams in. Reach for the obvious tool and see where it fails. A 3-D convolution is an ordinary convolution whose kernel spans time as well as space, (kt,kh,kw)(k_t, k_h, k_w)(kt​,kh​,kw​). Now ask where the temporal window sits: centered (the default), the output at time τ\tauτ reads τ−1,τ,τ+1\tau - 1, \tau, \tau + 1τ−1,τ,τ+1 — it reads the future, and a robot at time τ\tauτ does not have the future. So you must break the symmetry, and the fix is one shift, not new machinery:

Definition — Causal temporal convolution

A convolution over time whose output at step τ\tauτ depends only on inputs at steps ≤τ\le \tau≤τ. Implementation: pad kt−1k_t - 1kt​−1 frames on the past side and none on the future side, so the kernel window ends at the present. Stacking layers grows the receptive field backward only (the WaveNet construction, applied here to video).

Stare at Fig. DIT-3 until two things pop out, because both get used later. First, the ÷4 you took on faith in Step 2 was hiding in plain sight: strided causal stages do the temporal downsampling — each stride-2 stage halves the frame count, and two stages give the ÷4. Second, you got something for free: follow frame 1’s pathway and see that it reaches its latent through padding only, so the anchor — frame 1’s latent, computed from frame 1 alone — is exact. That is what lets an image-to-video model condition on a real frame exactly, and lets the encoder run on a growing stream without ever revisiting what it already encoded. The anchor also settles an arithmetic that would otherwise nag you as off by one: the 8 chunked latent frames of DreamZero’s context each summarize 4 raw frames, and the anchor rides alongside them as the conditioning frame — 1 + 8 × 4 = 33 raw frames ↔ 8 chunked latent frames. (Honesty about sources: the paper states only the 8 ↔ 33 correspondence; the split is the Wan-family causal-VAE design.)

Now hold up one chunk and read it the way the robot does: three clocks reading the same 1.6 s of wall time. And catch the trap before you fall in — you might guess the model emits actions one at a time, LLM-style. It does not: the 48 actions are denoised together, in one pass, then streamed one per 33 ms by the 30 Hz controller while the GPU already denoises the next chunk. Part 3 · Build one block — with every shape on the wire

Chop, embed, list — now you must build the machine that reads the list, and the temptation is to invent something exotic. Resist it. A DiT is N=40N = 40N=40 copies of one block, and one block is easier than it looks: it is the same wrapper applied twice — normalize, re-tune for the noise level, operate, gate, add the untouched copy back — differing only in the operation wrapped. Sub-block 1 wraps attention, the single place where the nnn tokens exchange information (its weights are n×nn \times nn×n per head); sub-block 2 wraps the MLP, which transforms each token alone. As you read the drawing, check the invariant on every wire: the whole stack is shape-preserving — a list of nnn tokens of width 5120 goes in, the same shape comes out, forty times over. Everything else in the drawing is plumbing for stability (norms, residuals) or for conditioning (the right-hand column — which is where the real question hides):

So far you have built a plain transformer; here is what makes it a diffusion transformer. One network must act as a whole family of denoisers — coarse structure-guessing at high noise, fine sharpening at low noise — with the timestep selecting the family member. So one scalar needs the leverage to re-tune what every block computes. That is the right-hand column’s entire job:

Definition — Adaptive layer norm (adaLN / adaLN-Zero)

With conditioning vector ccc (here: the timestep embedding), an MLP produces per-channel modulation signals, and each sub-block computes

where SubBlock is self-attention or the MLP. In adaLN-Zero the gates alpha\\alphaalpha are initialized to zero, so every block starts as the identity and training grows the computation gradually.

Why scaling-and-shifting, of all things?

Do not take adaLN on authority — try to invent the injection yourself and watch the obvious candidates die. As an extra token? ttt is global — every token in the chunk shares the same value — so attention would spend capacity re-fetching one constant fact, block after block. Concatenated once at the input? Forty blocks later the signal has washed out, and the late blocks are precisely the ones whose behavior must differ most across noise levels. Which pushes you to feature-wise modulation at every block (the FiLM pattern, which adaLN instantiates): a per-channel scale can amplify, suppress, or flip a feature — it reconfigures each block’s computation — for a few thousand numbers per block, applied uniformly to all tokens, exactly matching the global nature of the information. Where to inject it? The normalization layer is the natural site, because LN has just stripped each token’s scale and shift; re-introducing them as functions of the conditioning costs nothing structurally. One worry remains — forty freshly re-tunable blocks sound unstable — and the zero-initialized gates answer it: every residual branch starts closed, each block begins as the identity, and training opens the gates gradually — which is what makes a 40-block denoiser stable. And none of this is taste: the DiT paper ablated the alternatives (in-context token, cross-attention) and adaLN-Zero won on both quality and compute (Peebles & Xie 2023).

Why sinusoidal features, and not the raw scalar?

Definition — Sinusoidal (Fourier) features

The scalar ttt is encoded as its readings on a bank of sinusoids at geometrically spaced frequencies,

100%

You should object here: ttt is a perfectly good number — why dress it up? Because of a failure you would meet if you tried the lazy thing: a plain MLP fed one raw scalar is biased toward overly smooth functions of it (the spectral-bias result — Fourier features are the standard cure), while a denoiser’s behavior genuinely must change quickly in places, most sharply near the ends of the schedule. The bank fixes this at every scale at once: slow waves encode early-versus-late, fast waves separate neighboring timesteps, nearby ttt always map to nearby vectors (no binning cliffs), and the code has constant norm with rich structure the per-block heads can slice by frequency. One scalar in, a whole spectrum of handles out.

Part 4 · Now make it act: chunks, masks, cache

Take stock: you now own a machine that denoises one clip beautifully — and is useless to a robot. Find the flaw yourself. A stock video DiT is bidirectional: it emits one fixed-length clip, every frame attending to every other — no notion of “now”, so no way to act mid-clip, and fixed windows force frame subsampling that breaks the video-time ↔ action-time alignment. You solved exactly this problem in Part 2 for convolutions; solve it once more for attention. The causal variant generates chunk by chunk under a mask that is the video version of an LLM’s causal mask:

Definition — Block-causal attention mask

Group the sequence into chunks 1,…,M1, \dots, M1,…,M. A query token in chunk iii may attend to a key token in chunk jjj iff j<ij < ij<i (finished, clean context) or j=ij = ij=i (its own window, bidirectional). Conditioning tokens (text, proprioception) are attendable from every chunk.

Finished chunks persist as a KV cache, so context grows almost for free (up to 4 chunks = 6.6 s here). Explore the masks — and the one move unique to a robot video model:

Hover a row to read its receptive field. Per the released code, the state token rides after the action tokens as the register tail, while text and the first-frame CLIP embedding enter each block via cross-attention — outside this grid. The grid shows Fig. 14 frame/action structure.

One honest crack remains — find it before it finds you. Training uses teacher forcing: previous chunks in context are clean ground truth. At inference a pure video generator must feed on its own imperfect outputs, and the mismatch compounds — autoregressive drift. Now ask what a robot has that an offline generator never will: a world that answers back. That is the exit. After each chunk executes, the real camera frames overwrite the model’s predicted frames in the KV cache, restoring teacher-forcing conditions every 1.6 s (toggle it in Fig. DIT-2’s inference tab). And mark the difference in kind: offline cousins — noise injection on context, diffusion forcing — only soften the mismatch; observation overwrite deletes it, at the price of needing an environment that answers back.

Part 5 · So who actually denoises? The loop

Last question, and it is a trick: which part of the stack generates the video? None of it — no block “generates” anything. Run the stack once and look at what actually comes out: one forward pass reads the noisy chunk and outputs a velocity per token — the flow-matching arrow saying “move this way”. An arrow is not a video. So generation must live outside the network, in the outer loop that alternates network calls with small updates:

100%

If you arrive from language models, two inversions are worth pinning down. An LLM’s output at position iii is the next token — the sequence grows by one and the model’s own output becomes its next input. Here the noisy chunk’s positions never move: the output at each noisy position is a velocity for that same position; the scheduler applies it to the underlying raw latents/actions (re-embedded before the next call), refining the same positions in place as ttt climbs. The sequence grows only at chunk granularity — and what gets appended as “past” is the real observation, not the model’s output.

And the way out you already know, because it is Part 1 run backwards: after the loop exits at t=1t = 1t=1, a final linear layer maps each video token 5120 → 64, those 64 numbers fold back into the token’s 2 × 2 tile (un-patchify — Fig. DIT-8 run right-to-left), reassembling the 16 × 60 × 104 latent frames for the VAE decoder, while the action tokens pass through the action decoder into the 48 executable joint deltas. Pixels went in, joint deltas come out — and every step in between is one you could now re-derive yourself.

Why does chunk-causal generation matter for a robot, beyond latency?
Alignment and statefulness. Bidirectional models need fixed-length windows, which forces video subsampling that distorts the correspondence between frame time and action time; causal chunks preserve native rates. And the KV cache is a visual memory: each new chunk conditions on up to 6.6 s of actual history — at inference, real observed history, thanks to the overwrite.
Where exactly does the LLM analogy break?
Two places. Tokens within a chunk are denoised jointly with bidirectional attention among themselves (an LLM is causal token-by-token), and the model predicts continuous velocities for latent patches rather than a softmax over a vocabulary. The causal structure lives at chunk granularity, not token granularity.
Why do 8 latent frames correspond to 33 raw frames rather than 32?
Because the VAE is causal with an exact anchor: the first raw frame is encoded alone as the conditioning frame, and each of the 8 chunked latent frames summarizes 4 raw frames — 1 + 8 × 4 = 33, with the anchor riding alongside the 8 rather than being one of them. The anchor is what makes image-to-video conditioning exact and streaming encoding possible; a plain non-causal 4× VAE would give the rounder 32.
Why is t injected at all 40 blocks via scale-and-shift, rather than fed once as an input or a token?
Because t must reconfigure the whole family of computations: early blocks and late blocks both change behavior with noise level, and a signal injected once at the bottom washes out by the top. As a token it would waste attention on a value every token already shares. Per-block FiLM-style scales and shifts can amplify, suppress or flip each feature channel — reconfiguring every block for a few thousand parameters each — and the zero-initialized gates make every block start as the identity, which stabilizes training. The DiT ablation found exactly this (adaLN-Zero) best on quality and compute.
