<TLDR>Flow matching trains a network to predict the velocity of a straight-line journey from noise to
data; generating a sample is then just integrating that velocity field. It is the objective behind
today's large video/image models (SD3, Flux, Wan) — and behind DreamZero's joint video+action
denoising.</TLDR>

## Intuition

A generative model must answer: *given pure randomness, produce something that looks like the data.*
Flow matching's answer is transport. Imagine every possible data sample connected to a random noise
sample by a straight line. If a network could tell you, at any intermediate point, "which way is
home and how fast", you could start anywhere in the noise and simply walk to a data sample. Training
teaches exactly that one **job**: look at a partially noisy point and the time <M tex="t" />, output
the direction-and-speed of the line passing through it. (The word is "job", not "skill" — in
robotics papers *skill* means a motion primitive; here nothing robotic is meant.)

## The exact definition

Fix two distributions in the same space: noise <M tex="p_0 = \mathcal{N}(0, I)" /> and the data
distribution <M tex="p_1" />. Choose a **probability path** <M tex="p_t" /> that morphs one into the
other as <M tex="t" /> runs 0 → 1. A time-dependent **velocity field** <M tex="u(x, t)" />
*generates* that path if moving every point by the ODE <M tex="\mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}t = u(x, t)" />
carries <M tex="p_0" /> into exactly <M tex="p_t" /> at every time. Flow matching = fit a network
<M tex="u_\theta" /> to such a field by plain regression.

The marginal field is intractable, so **conditional flow matching** (the trick that makes it work)
regresses against the velocity of the path *conditioned on a sampled pair*: draw a data point
<M tex="x_1" />, a noise point <M tex="x_0" />, a time <M tex="t" />, form the interpolant, and
regress on that pair's own velocity. The theorem making this legal (Lipman et al. 2022): the
conditional objective has the **same gradients** as the intractable marginal one, so its minimizer
is the true marginal field. With the straight-line (rectified-flow) path used in practice:

<MathBlock>{`x_t = t\\,x_1 + (1-t)\\,x_0, \\qquad \\mathcal{L} = \\mathbb{E}_{x_0, x_1, t}\\, \\bigl\\| u_\\theta(x_t, t) - (x_1 - x_0) \\bigr\\|^2`}</MathBlock>

Convention warning: this page (and DreamZero's Eq. 2) puts noise at <M tex="t = 0" /> and data at
<M tex="t = 1" />; many diffusion papers run the axis the other way. Always check before comparing
equations across papers.

## What "velocity" means here — and what it does not

The velocity is the time-derivative of a point's position along the chosen path,
<M tex="\mathrm{d}x_t/\mathrm{d}t" /> — "which direction and how fast this point should move
through the space at time <M tex="t" />". The space is the model's sample space (image latents,
video latents, normalized action chunks). It is **not** a physical velocity: in DreamZero the
flow-matching velocity for action tokens is a derivative in normalized-action space along the
denoising path — it is not the robot's joint velocity, and the executed actions are joint-position
deltas, not speeds.

**Why is it constant along a straight line?** Differentiate the interpolant once:

<MathBlock>{`\\frac{\\mathrm{d}x_t}{\\mathrm{d}t} = \\frac{\\mathrm{d}}{\\mathrm{d}t}\\bigl[\\, t\\,x_1 + (1-t)\\,x_0 \\,\\bigr] = x_1 - x_0`}</MathBlock>

The result contains no <M tex="t" />: position depends on <M tex="t" /> linearly, so its derivative
is the same everywhere on the segment. That constancy is the pedagogical gift of the straight path —
the regression target for a given pair does not change as the point travels, which makes the
learning problem as clean as regression gets.

## Why predict the velocity (and not the clean sample, or the noise)?

Given <M tex="x_t" /> and <M tex="t" />, the three classic targets are affinely interchangeable —
predict any one and the others follow:

<MathBlock>{`x_1 = x_t + (1-t)\\,v, \\qquad x_0 = x_t - t\\,v, \\qquad v = x_1 - x_0`}</MathBlock>

So velocity-prediction is a *parameterization choice*, not extra power. It is the preferred one
because (1) the sampler integrates a velocity field, so the network outputs exactly what the ODE
solver consumes; (2) its scale is well-behaved across all <M tex="t" /> — noise-prediction targets
become ill-conditioned near the data end and clean-sample-prediction becomes trivial there, while
the constant <M tex="x_1 - x_0" /> keeps the loss informative everywhere; and (3) one network
evaluated at any <M tex="(x, t)" /> defines the entire transport at once — a single amortized field
rather than a chain of step-specific denoisers.

## History in five steps

1. **Continuous normalizing flows** (Chen et al. 2018, FFJORD 2019): generate by integrating a
   learned ODE — elegant, but training required simulating the ODE. Too slow to scale.
2. **Score-based diffusion / DDPM** (Song & Ermon 2019; Ho et al. 2020): corrupt data with a fixed
   *stochastic* noising process and learn to reverse it step by step. Simulation-free training —
   this is what scaled first — but sampling wanted many steps along a curved path.
3. **Probability-flow ODE** (Song et al. 2021): every diffusion SDE has a deterministic ODE with the
   same marginals — the bridge showing diffusion and flows are two views of one thing.
4. **Flow matching / rectified flow / stochastic interpolants** (Lipman et al.; Liu et al.; Albergo
   & Vanden-Eijnden — all 2022, independently): train the ODE directly, simulation-free, with freely
   chosen paths — including straight ones.
5. **Adoption** (2024–): Stable Diffusion 3, Flux, Movie Gen, and Wan2.1 — DreamZero's backbone —
   all train with flow-matching objectives.

## Benefits — and the honest drawbacks

Benefits: a stable MSE loss (no adversary, no likelihood machinery); simulation-free training of a
continuous flow; near-straight paths that tolerate few, large integration steps (DreamZero: 16
denoising steps, cut to ~4 by caching directionally-consistent velocities, and to 1 video step in
its Flash variant); and one field usable at every noise level.

Drawbacks of replacing the curved stochastic path with a straight deterministic one:

- **Individually straight, collectively curved.** Conditional paths cross (visible in Fig. FM-1 —
  lines intersect), and the learned *marginal* field is an average over crossings, so actual sampled
  trajectories still bend. True one-step generation needs extra work (reflow, distillation).
- **No stochastic self-correction.** An SDE sampler re-injects noise each step, which can act as a
  corrector that "forgets" small integration errors; a deterministic ODE accumulates them. Some
  quality regimes still favor stochastic samplers.
- **Determinism means diversity comes only from the seed.** Same noise in, same sample out — fine in
  practice, but worth knowing.
- **It is largely diffusion, reparameterized.** Under the hood, straight-path flow matching is
  equivalent to a specific weighting/parameterization of denoising objectives (the Kingma & Gao
  view), so the schedule and loss weight <M tex="w(t)" /> still matter — the gains are practical
  (stability, step count), not a new capability class.

## In this library

DreamZero applies exactly this objective jointly to video latents and action latents, with one
shared <M tex="t" /> per chunk so both modalities denoise toward the same imagined future — and its
Flash variant deliberately *breaks* the shared schedule (video <M tex="t \sim \mathrm{Beta}(7,1)" />,
action uniform) to buy 1-step video inference at a measured quality cost. See the paper page for the
chunked, causal machinery around the objective ([Causal DiT](../causal-dit/)).
