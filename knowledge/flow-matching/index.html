Flow matching trains a network to predict the velocity of a straight-line journey from noise to data; generating a sample is then just integrating that velocity field. It is the objective behind today’s large video/image models (SD3, Flux, Wan) — and behind DreamZero’s joint video+action denoising.

Intuition

A generative model must answer: given pure randomness, produce something that looks like the data. Flow matching’s answer is transport. Imagine every possible data sample connected to a random noise sample by a straight line. If a network could tell you, at any intermediate point, “which way is home and how fast”, you could start anywhere in the noise and simply walk to a data sample. Training teaches exactly that one job: look at a partially noisy point and the time ttt, output the direction-and-speed of the line passing through it. (The word is “job”, not “skill” — in robotics papers skill means a motion primitive; here nothing robotic is meant.)

First, the vocabulary: field, probability path, marginal

Three words do the heavy lifting in every flow-matching paper, and none of them is exotic.

A field assigns a value to every point of space. A temperature map is a scalar field — one number per location. A wind map is a vector field — one arrow per location. That is all “field” means here: not a trajectory, not a path, but a function you can query anywhere. (And not the algebra sense: the “field” of group–ring–field is a number system closed under the four operations — German distinguishes Feld, this one, from Körper, that one; English collapsed both. The only contact point: velocity vectors live in a vector space, which is defined over the algebraic field ℝ of scalars.) A velocity field u(x,t)u(x, t)u(x,t) is a wind map that may change over time: feed it any location xxx and any time ttt, it returns the one arrow at that place and moment.

A probability path ptp_tpt​ is a movie of distributions: frame 0 is the noise distribution, frame 1 is the data distribution, and the frames in between morph one into the other continuously (Fig. FM-4 shows one as a filmstrip). The straight-line interpolant picks one specific movie — at time ttt, the crowd of points is exactly the pairwise blends t x1+(1−t) x0t\,x_1 + (1-t)\,x_0tx1​+(1−t)x0​.

The two notions meet in one verb: a velocity field generates a probability path when blowing the crowd with that wind reproduces the movie — scatter points as frame 0, move each one by dx/dt=u(x,t)\mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}t = u(x, t)dx/dt=u(x,t), and at every ttt the crowd is distributed exactly as frame ttt. Flow matching’s whole object of desire is one such field.

The marginal field is intractable, so conditional flow matching (the trick that makes it work) regresses against the velocity of the path conditioned on a sampled pair: draw a data point x1x_1x1​, a noise point x0x_0x0​, a time ttt, form the interpolant, and regress on that pair’s own velocity. The theorem making this legal (Lipman et al. 2022): the conditional objective has the same gradients as the intractable marginal one, so its minimizer is the true marginal field.

What u_θ actually converges to: the marginal field

Read the loss closely and a puzzle appears — for a fixed pair the target x1−x0x_1 - x_0x1​−x0​ is one constant vector, so is the network supposed to output that same vector at every ttt? No, and the reason is the heart of the method. The network is a function of (x,t)(x, t)(x,t) only; it is never told which pair produced its input. The same point xxx at time ttt can be reached by many different (noise, data) pairs, each demanding a different velocity. A mean-squared-error regression fed conflicting targets converges to their average — precisely, the conditional expectation

Definition — Marginal velocity field
u⋆(x,t)  =  E[ x1−x0  ∣  xt=x ]u^{\star}(x, t) \;=\; \mathbb{E}\bigl[\, x_1 - x_0 \;\big|\; x_t = x \,\bigr]u⋆(x,t)=E[x1​−x0​​xt​=x]

This average-over-all-pairings is the marginal velocity field. It is intractable to write down — evaluating that expectation means integrating over every pairing consistent with landing at xxx at time ttt, weighted by the very data distribution you are trying to learn. But you never need to write it down: SGD on the per-pair (conditional) loss converges to it automatically, and the flow-matching theorem guarantees that integrating this mean field still transports p0p_0p0​ exactly to p1p_1p1​.

So the honest description of uθ(xt,t)u_\theta(x_t, t)uθ​(xt​,t): input — a position in sample space and a time; output — one velocity vector, the posterior-mean of x1−x0x_1 - x_0x1​−x0​ over all pairings that could have put a point there. Near t=0t = 0t=0 the input is almost pure noise and says little about the destination, so the posterior is broad and the arrow points toward a blur of plausible data. Near t=1t = 1t=1 the point has nearly arrived, the posterior collapses, and the arrow points at one specific sample. Constant along each conditional line, yes — but what is learned is a genuine function of position and time.

Two magnitudes worth keeping straight. The target’s magnitude is the full ∥x1−x0∥\lVert x_1 - x_0 \rVert∥x1​−x0​∥ at every point of its segment — not the remaining distance — because ttt runs over [0,1][0,1][0,1] and the whole journey must finish in unit time; what shrinks as ttt grows is the remaining displacement (1−t)(x1−x0)(1-t)(x_1 - x_0)(1−t)(x1​−x0​), which lands exactly on x1x_1x1​. The mean’s magnitude is generally smaller than its components’ (vectors at an angle partially cancel) — one more reason the marginal field is not any single pair’s field.

”Rectified flow”, named

Definition — Rectified flow

Flow matching with the linear interpolant xt=t x1+(1−t) x0x_t = t\,x_1 + (1-t)\,x_0xt​=tx1​+(1−t)x0​ and per-pair target x1−x0x_1 - x_0x1​−x0​; optionally iterated by reflow, which re-pairs generated samples with their own noise seeds and retrains.

Rectified flow is the name of exactly this linear-interpolant instance — straight conditional paths, constant per-pair velocity — introduced by Liu et al. (2022) alongside an optional iterate called reflow: sample with the trained field, re-pair each output with the noise seed that produced it, retrain on those non-crossing pairs. Each round straightens the marginal trajectories (Fig. FM-2’s curving diminishes), which is the principled route to one-step generation. “Flow matching with the linear path”, “rectified flow”, and “stochastic interpolants with linear interpolant” name the same object from three simultaneous 2022 papers.

What “velocity” means here — and what it does not

The velocity is the time-derivative of a point’s position along the chosen path, dxt/dt\mathrm{d}x_t/\mathrm{d}tdxt​/dt — “which direction and how fast this point should move through the space at time ttt”. The space is the model’s sample space (image latents, video latents, normalized action chunks). It is not a physical velocity: in DreamZero the flow-matching velocity for action tokens is a derivative in normalized-action space along the denoising path — it is not the robot’s joint velocity, and the executed actions are joint-position deltas, not speeds.

Why is it constant along a straight line? Differentiate the interpolant once:

dxtdt=ddt[ t x1+(1−t) x0 ]=x1−x0\frac{\mathrm{d}x_t}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigl[\, t\,x_1 + (1-t)\,x_0 \,\bigr] = x_1 - x_0dtdxt​​=dtd​[tx1​+(1−t)x0​]=x1​−x0​

The result contains no ttt: position depends on ttt linearly, so its derivative is the same everywhere on the segment. That constancy is the pedagogical gift of the straight path — the regression target for a given pair does not change as the point travels, which makes the learning problem as clean as regression gets.

Why predict the velocity (and not the clean sample, or the noise)?

Given xtx_txt​ and ttt, the three classic targets are affinely interchangeable — predict any one and the others follow:

x1=xt+(1−t) v,x0=xt−t v,v=x1−x0x_1 = x_t + (1-t)\,v, \qquad x_0 = x_t - t\,v, \qquad v = x_1 - x_0x1​=xt​+(1−t)v,x0​=xt​−tv,v=x1​−x0​

So velocity-prediction is a parameterization choice, not extra power. It is the preferred one because (1) the sampler integrates a velocity field, so the network outputs exactly what the ODE solver consumes; (2) its scale is well-behaved across all ttt — noise-prediction targets become ill-conditioned near the data end and clean-sample-prediction becomes trivial there, while the constant x1−x0x_1 - x_0x1​−x0​ keeps the loss informative everywhere; and (3) one network evaluated at any (x,t)(x, t)(x,t) defines the entire transport at once — a single amortized field rather than a chain of step-specific denoisers.

History in five steps

  1. Continuous normalizing flows (Chen et al. 2018, FFJORD 2019): generate by integrating a learned ODE — elegant, but training required simulating the ODE. Too slow to scale.
  2. Score-based diffusion / DDPM (Song & Ermon 2019; Ho et al. 2020): corrupt data with a fixed stochastic noising process and learn to reverse it step by step. Simulation-free training — this is what scaled first — but sampling wanted many steps along a curved path.
  3. Probability-flow ODE (Song et al. 2021): every diffusion SDE has a deterministic ODE with the same marginals — the bridge showing diffusion and flows are two views of one thing.
  4. Flow matching / rectified flow / stochastic interpolants (Lipman et al.; Liu et al.; Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden — all 2022, independently): train the ODE directly, simulation-free, with freely chosen paths — including straight ones.
  5. Adoption (2024–): Stable Diffusion 3, Flux, Movie Gen, and Wan2.1 — DreamZero’s backbone — all train with flow-matching objectives.

Benefits — and the honest drawbacks

Benefits: a stable MSE loss (no adversary, no likelihood machinery); simulation-free training of a continuous flow; near-straight paths that tolerate few, large integration steps (DreamZero: 16 denoising steps, cut to ~4 by caching directionally-consistent velocities, and to 1 video step in its Flash variant); and one field usable at every noise level.

Drawbacks of replacing the curved stochastic path with a straight deterministic one:

  • Individually straight, collectively curved. Conditional paths cross (visible in Fig. FM-1 — lines intersect), and the learned marginal field is an average over crossings (Fig. FM-2), so actual sampled trajectories still bend — watch Fig. FM-3’s accent curves do it. True one-step generation needs extra work (reflow, distillation).
  • No stochastic self-correction. An SDE sampler re-injects noise each step, which can act as a corrector that “forgets” small integration errors; a deterministic ODE accumulates them. Some quality regimes still favor stochastic samplers.
  • Determinism means diversity comes only from the seed. Same noise in, same sample out — fine in practice, but worth knowing.
  • It is largely diffusion, reparameterized. Under the hood, straight-path flow matching is equivalent to a specific weighting/parameterization of denoising objectives (the Kingma & Gao view), so the schedule and loss weight w(t)w(t)w(t) still matter — the gains are practical (stability, step count), not a new capability class.

A friend says flow matching learns to move points along straight lines. What is the precise correction?
Each *conditional* pair defines a straight regression target, and that target's velocity is constant along its segment. But the learned field is the marginal average over many crossing pairs, so the trajectories a sampler actually follows are generally curved. Straightness is a property of the training target, not of the sampled path.
Why does predicting velocity beat predicting the clean sample directly, if the two are affinely equivalent?
Equivalent in information, not in conditioning: near t = 1 the clean-sample target degenerates (the input almost equals the answer) and noise-prediction blows up in scale, while the velocity target x1 − x0 keeps constant scale for every t. And the sampler consumes a velocity anyway — predicting it removes a conversion.
