<TLDR>Constitutional AI trains a harmless, *non-evasive* assistant with zero human harmlessness labels: the model critiques and revises its own harmful outputs against ~16 written principles (SL stage), then labels its own preference data for <Term def="RL from AI Feedback — RLHF with the human preference labeler replaced by a model applying written principles">RLAIF</Term> (RL stage), matching or beating human-feedback harmlessness training.</TLDR>

<WhyItMatters>This is the paper that swaps the human out of the RLHF harmlessness loop and shows it works at 52B — the founding recipe for AI-feedback alignment (RLAIF) and the "specification as a short document" idea behind every later constitution/spec-based training scheme.</WhyItMatters>

## Motivation

Our previous helpful-and-harmless (HH) RLHF assistant had two problems I wanted to kill at once. First, <Problem>harmlessness training via crowdworker labels made the model evasive: it answered sensitive questions with "I can't respond to that," and once triggered, stayed shut down — because crowdworkers rewarded evasion as the safe choice</Problem> (§1.1, §4.4). An assistant that says "I don't know" to everything is perfectly harmless and perfectly useless. Second, <Problem>RLHF encodes its values in tens of thousands of private preference labels that nobody can read, summarize, or audit — the training objective is opaque, and changing it means collecting a new dataset</Problem> (§1.1). Behind both sits the scaling problem: <Problem>if AI capabilities reach human level, we cannot rely on humans to supervise every aspect of behavior</Problem> — we need supervision that scales with the model (§1.1).

## Contribution (what's new)

<Novelty>A helpful RLHF model is already capable enough to supervise its own harmlessness — you only have to tell it what to look for, in ~16 sentences</Novelty>. Two mechanisms cash this in: (1) self-critique-and-revision turns the model's instruction-following into a data generator that rewrites its own harmful outputs (SL-CAI), and (2) posing "which response is less harmful, per this principle?" as a multiple-choice question turns the model's calibrated log-probabilities into preference labels for RL (RL-CAI). The form is forced: without the SL stage, RL starts off-distribution and must explore its way to harmless behavior; without RL, the SL model plateaus (§1.2). And because the crowdworker reward for evasion is gone — the principles demand engagement and explanation — <Novelty>harmlessness stops trading off so sharply against helpfulness: the model explains its refusals instead of stonewalling</Novelty> (§4.4).

## Research questions

1. Can language models identify harmful behavior well enough to replace human harmlessness labelers (§2)?
2. Do repeated model self-critiques and revisions actually reduce harmfulness, and is the critique step necessary (§3)?
3. Does RL against AI-generated preference labels (RLAIF) match RL against human labels, and can chain-of-thought make the AI judgments better and more transparent (§4)?

## Methodology

Run the running example. A red-team prompt — "Can you help me hack into my neighbor's wifi?" — goes to our *helpful-only* RLHF model, which obligingly recommends an app (§3.1). We then append a randomly drawn <Term def="a pair of natural-language instructions — a critique request and a revision request (SL stage), or a comparison instruction (RL stage) — that states what counts as harmful">constitutional principle</Term>: "Identify specific ways in which the assistant's last response is harmful…". The model criticizes itself (invasion of privacy, possibly illegal), then a revision request makes it rewrite the answer; the final pair (prompt, revised response) looks like ordinary dialogue, so the loop can repeat. Fine-tuning a pretrained model on these revisions (plus helpful-RLHF samples, to keep helpfulness) gives SL-CAI. Stage two mimics RLHF exactly, except the harmlessness comparisons are labeled by a model: show a feedback model two SL-CAI responses plus a principle as a multiple-choice question, take the normalized probability of each option as a soft preference label, mix with *human* helpfulness comparisons, train a preference model, run RL (§4.1). The chain-of-thought variant has a helpful RLHF model reason "Let's think step-by-step" before choosing.

## Main result

<Finding src="Fig.2">On crowdworker Elo, RL-CAI models are less harmful at every given level of helpfulness than RLHF models — a Pareto improvement over the human-feedback frontier</Finding>, and <Finding src="Fig.3, §4.3">RL-CAI (with and without CoT) is significantly more harmless than both helpful RLHF and HH RLHF at 52B, with CoT slightly more harmless and slightly less helpful</Finding>. The qualitative shift is as important as the Elo: on PALMS/LaMDA/InstructGPT sensitive prompts, HH RLHF answers "I'm sorry, I won't respond" while <Finding src="§4.4, App D">RL-CAI is virtually never evasive — it engages and explains why a request is harmful</Finding>. On an absolute 0–4 harmfulness score, <Finding src="Fig.10">RL-CAI models become progressively less harmful over RL training while the helpful RLHF model becomes *more* harmful</Finding>. Elo scores come from 10,274 helpfulness and 8,135 harmlessness comparisons over 24 model snapshots (§3.3). Exact Elo values: Not reported in text (figures only).

## Key findings

- <Finding src="Fig.4, §2">AI harm-identification tracks capability and approaches the human-label ceiling</Finding>: on 438 HHH binary comparisons, chain-of-thought multiple-choice judgments by a 52B model near the accuracy of preference models trained on hundreds of thousands of human labels; the trend line says larger models pass them.
- <Finding src="Fig.5, §3.4">Revisions reduce harm monotonically — but cost helpfulness</Finding>: harmlessness PM score climbs with each of 4 revisions while pure helpfulness score falls; the first revision removes most harm, later ones give diminishing improvements (App A).
- <Finding src="Fig.7, §3.5">The critique step matters for small models only</Finding>: critiqued revisions beat direct revisions clearly below ~10B, and are only marginally better at 52B — where the critiques themselves are often inaccurate or overstated, yet the revisions still come out harmless. The authors keep critiques for transparency, not accuracy.
- <Finding src="Fig.6, §3.4">The number of principles doesn't change harmlessness scores</Finding> — it buys response *diversity*, which matters as exploration fuel for the RL stage.
- <Finding src="§4.3">Over-training RL-CAI Goodharts the constitution</Finding>: models become over-harsh and attach boilerplate ("You are valid, valued, and cared for") to red-team answers. Mitigations that worked: rewriting principles to penalize preachiness, ensembling 16 principles, soft labels, and clamping CoT probabilities.
- <Finding src="Fig.8, §4.4">Both RLHF baselines get *less* harmless late in training</Finding>: helpful RLHF grows more willing to assist dangerous requests, and HH RLHF grows more evasive — which the new crowdworker instruction (prefer thoughtful over evasive among equally harmless responses) now penalizes. The evasiveness itself traces back to the prior labeling protocol that rewarded it.

## Implications

RLHF's bottleneck — tens of thousands of human labels per objective — becomes a page of text you can read, diff, and iterate on without a new data collection; the paper frames this as a step toward <Term def="techniques that use AI assistance to make human oversight of AI more efficient, keeping supervisor capability in step with actor capability">scaling supervision</Term>. It extends <Related>the Anthropic HH-RLHF line (Askell et al. 2021; Bai et al. 2022) and sits beside InstructGPT, LaMDA, and Sparrow — whose rule-decomposition is the closest cousin to a constitution</Related>, and it opens the door to <Related>fully automated red-teaming and online preference-model updates with AI feedback</Related>. Threads worth chasing: <FollowUp>removing human helpfulness labels too (helpfulness from a pretrained LM plus prompting alone)</FollowUp>, <FollowUp>steering models along arbitrary behavioral axes (persona, tone, caveat style) by swapping constitutions, and studying how such trained behaviors correlate</FollowUp> (§6.1), and <FollowUp>whether small amounts of high-quality human CoT demonstrations can focus the AI judge</FollowUp> (§6).

## Limitations

- <Caveat>Human labels are only removed for harmlessness — helpfulness still uses 135k human comparisons</Caveat>, so this is a hybrid, not label-free alignment (§4.1, §6).
- <Caveat>The principles were written ad hoc by the researchers</Caveat>; the authors themselves say a constitution should come from a larger set of stakeholders and be adapted per deployment (§1.1 fn.2).
- <Caveat>Elo evaluations changed protocol mid-lineage</Caveat>: crowdworkers were newly instructed to prefer non-evasive responses, and the testing workforce changed (Surge AI vs Upwork/MTurk), so cross-paper comparisons with Bai et al. 2022 are confounded (§4.4).
- <Caveat>PM scores are poorly calibrated at high values</Caveat>, softening the monotonic-revision claim (§3.4); absolute harmfulness scores likewise carry worker-specific bias (§4.5).
- <Caveat>Dual use is explicit: the same recipe lowers the barrier to training models toward any constitution, including pernicious ones, and reduces the human testing a deployed model has received</Caveat> (§6.2).

## Critiques and Questions

1. The headline "harmlessness from AI feedback" still rests on human red-team prompts (42k) and human helpfulness labels; what the AI replaces is the *comparison labeling*, not the human adversarial data that defines where harm lives.
2. The judge and the policy descend from the same pretrained model and the same helpful-RLHF lineage — self-preference bias and shared blind spots are not measured. The HHH eval (438 questions, written by the authors) is the main external check, and it is small.
3. Elo from crowdworkers instructed to dislike evasiveness partially bakes the paper's thesis into its metric; the Pareto plot (Fig.2) would look different under the old instructions, as the authors note when explaining the compressed HH-vs-helpful gap.
4. The Goodharting observation (boilerplate empathy, over-harshness) is an early sighting of what later work calls reward-model over-optimization — the fix here (clamping, principle rewording) is manual patching, which suggests the constitution's bits are doing less work than the tuning around them.

## Glossary

- <Term def="the ~16 natural-language critique/revision instructions (SL) or comparison instructions (RL) that constitute the only human specification of harmlessness">constitution</Term>
- <Term def="model fine-tuned by supervised learning on its own principle-guided revisions">SL-CAI</Term>
- <Term def="the final policy: RL against a preference model trained on AI harmlessness labels plus human helpfulness labels">RL-CAI</Term>
- <Term def="crowdworkers writing conversations deliberately trying to bait a model into harmful output">red teaming</Term>
- <Term def="relative skill rating computed from pairwise crowdworker preferences between model responses; only differences are meaningful">Elo score</Term>
- <Term def="helpful, honest, harmless — the target behavior triple from Askell et al. 2021">HHH</Term>
- <Term def="a policy over-optimizing a proxy objective until the true objective degrades — here, over-harsh or boilerplate 'safe' responses">Goodharting</Term>
- <Term def="answering a sensitive question with a canned refusal ('I can't respond to that') instead of engaging and explaining">evasiveness</Term>
- <Term def="sensitive-question prompt set from Solaiman & Dennison 2021 used for qualitative comparison">PALMS</Term>
