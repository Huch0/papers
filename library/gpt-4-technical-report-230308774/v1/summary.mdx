<TLDR>GPT-4 is a large multimodal Transformer (image+text in, text out) whose headline contribution is twofold: it reaches human-percentile scores on professional exams (top ~10% on a simulated bar exam) and new few-shot SOTA on standard NLP benchmarks, and its final loss and HumanEval pass rate were accurately predicted *before training finished* from runs using 1,000x–10,000x less compute — while the report deliberately discloses nothing about architecture, model size, data, or training compute.</TLDR>

<WhyItMatters>This is the report that opened the closed-frontier era: capability claims made through human exams and predictable-scaling methodology instead of architecture details, plus the system-card template every frontier lab now imitates. Foundational context for everything downstream on the LLM milestone path (RLHF-tuned assistants, eval culture, safety reporting).</WhyItMatters>

## Motivation
Two problems drove this project, and a third shaped the report itself. First, <Problem>existing NLP benchmarks were saturating and often beaten by benchmark-specific engineering, so they no longer discriminated what we cared about: competence in complex, nuanced scenarios of the kind humans are certified on</Problem> — hence exams built for humans. Second, an engineering problem that becomes existential at this scale: <Problem>a GPT-4-sized run is far too expensive to tune by trial and error, so without infrastructure whose behavior extrapolates predictably from small runs, you are flying blind on the one run you get</Problem> (§3). Third, <Problem>capabilities of this level create new risk surfaces (illicit advice, persuasion, emergent agentic behavior) that standard NLP evaluation does not touch</Problem> — the reason half this document is a system card.

## Contribution (what's new)
<Novelty>Predictable scaling as a discipline: a deep-learning stack built so that final loss follows a fitted power law <M tex="L(C) = aC^{b} + c" /> across 4 orders of magnitude of compute, and — more surprising — so that a *downstream capability* (HumanEval pass rate) extrapolates via <M tex="-\mathbb{E}_P[\log(\mathrm{pass\_rate}(C))] = \alpha\, C^{-k}" />, with predictions registered before training completed</Novelty> (§3.1–3.2, Fig.1–2). The design is forced: with no second attempt possible, small-scale runs must be *unbiased simulators* of the big one, or they are useless. Second, <Novelty>a model-assisted safety pipeline: rule-based reward models (RBRMs) — zero-shot GPT-4 classifiers that grade the policy's outputs against human-written rubrics during PPO — used to reward correct refusal style and punish over-refusal</Novelty> (§6). Third — and I say this as the author knowing it is a *negative* contribution — <Novelty>the report establishes deliberate non-disclosure as the frontier norm: "no further details about the architecture (including model size), hardware, training compute, dataset construction, training method, or similar", justified by competition and safety</Novelty> (§2, p.2). What remains verifiable from outside is exactly what the report chooses to measure.

## Research questions
1. Can a frontier model's loss *and* capabilities be predicted before training it? (§3)
2. How far does pretraining + RLHF get on evaluations designed to certify humans, and what does RLHF actually change? (§4, App.B)
3. Which risks scale with capability, and do post-training mitigations hold up under expert adversarial pressure? (System Card)

## Methodology
GPT-4 is a Transformer pretrained on next-token prediction over public and licensed data, then post-trained with RLHF (SFT on demonstrations, a reward model on rankings, PPO) plus safety-specific RLHF prompts and RBRM reward signals. Evaluation is the real methodology here: simulated human exams under real exam conditions and scoring (contamination-checked, essay sections graded by qualified third-party contractors), few-shot academic benchmarks, internal adversarial factuality evals, and a system-card process — 50+ external domain experts red-teaming from August 2022 (when training finished) to the March 2023 launch, including an Alignment Research Center (ARC) evaluation of autonomous replication.

<Pass title="Method details (scaling, exams, contamination, RBRM, hallucination pipeline)">
**Loss prediction.** Fit <M tex="L(C) = aC^{b} + c" /> (irreducible-loss form) to models trained with the same methodology at up to 10,000x less compute; predict GPT-4's final loss on a held-out internal-codebase dataset. The prediction was made shortly after the run started, without partial results, and landed on the observed value (§3.1, Fig.1).

**Capability prediction.** All but the 15 hardest HumanEval problems were split into 6 difficulty buckets by small-model performance; mean log pass rate extrapolates as a power law from models at up to 1,000x less compute. Predictions were accurate on 5 of 6 buckets; GPT-4 *underperformed* the prediction on the easiest bucket (§3.2, Fig.2). Hindsight Neglect, an Inverse Scaling Prize task where accuracy *drops* with scale through GPT-3.5, flips to ~100% accuracy at GPT-4 scale (Fig.3) — U-shaped scaling, inherently unpredictable from smaller models.

**Exams.** Sourced from recent official/practice materials; multiple-choice sections run few-shot with chain-of-thought-style explanations (temperature 0.3), free-response sampled once at temperature 0.6 with no methodology iteration; scores combined with each exam's official scoring formulas; percentiles from published score distributions (App.A). The post-RLHF model was used; a nonholdout twin exam was used for methodology iteration, the holdout run once. AMC 2022 percentiles are extrapolated ("likely have wide uncertainty", App.A.5). Some letter-extraction code paths differed between runs — detected post-hoc, impact judged minimal (App.A.2).

**Contamination.** Substring match: strip spaces/symbols, sample three random 50-character substrings per eval example, flag if any appears verbatim in training data; drop flagged examples and rerun, reporting the *lower* of the two scores (§4, App.C). Acknowledged limits: false negatives from near-duplicates, answers not checked, RLHF data not checked. BIG-bench was dropped entirely after portions were found in the training set (p.6, fn.5). GSM-8K is a special case: part of its training set was deliberately mixed into pretraining, so the 92.0% is "in-between true few-shot transfer and full benchmark-specific tuning" (App.E).

**RBRM.** Input = (prompt, policy output, human-written rubric as a multiple-choice classification task); the zero-shot GPT-4 classifier labels the output (e.g., desired-style refusal / evasive refusal / disallowed content / safe non-refusal), and that label is a reward during PPO — reward refusals on harmful prompts, reward non-refusals on known-safe boundary prompts, with boundary prompts synthesized by rewriting disallowed prompts into maximally similar allowed ones (§6; SC §3.1).

**Closed-domain hallucination pipeline.** GPT-4 generates its own comparison data: respond, list hallucinations in the response, rewrite without them, re-check, keep the (original, cleaned) pair for reward-model training — up to 5 iterations (SC §3.1, p.64).
</Pass>

## Main result
<Finding src="Tab.1, p.5">On a simulated Uniform Bar Exam GPT-4 scores 298/400 (~90th percentile of test takers) where GPT-3.5 scored 213/400 (~10th percentile)</Finding> — the single number that carried the release. <Finding src="Tab.2, p.7">On academic benchmarks GPT-4 few-shot beats not only prior LMs but benchmark-specific SOTA systems on every dataset except DROP</Finding>.

Human preference tracks the benchmarks: GPT-4's responses were preferred over GPT-3.5's on 70.2% of 5,214 real ChatGPT/API prompts (§4, p.7).

## Key findings
- <Finding src="Fig.1–2, §3">Predictable scaling worked: final loss and HumanEval pass rate were predicted accurately from runs at 1/1,000–1/10,000 of GPT-4's compute, registered before training completed</Finding> — but the authors admit the boundary: the easiest HumanEval bucket missed, and inverse-scaling tasks like Hindsight Neglect flip direction at scale (Fig.3), so capability forecasting is *not* solved in general.
- <Finding src="Tab.8, App.B">RLHF does not change measured capability: base 73.7% vs post-RLHF 74.0% averaged over exam multiple-choice sections</Finding> — capability comes from pretraining; post-training buys steerability and safety, not intelligence.
- <Finding src="Fig.8, p.12">Post-training wrecks calibration: expected calibration error on MMLU goes from 0.007 (pretrained; confidence ≈ accuracy) to 0.074 after PPO</Finding> — the aligned model "believes" itself in a way the base model does not, and conversely on TruthfulQA the base model is barely better than GPT-3.5 while RLHF adds the large jump to ~60% (Fig.7; SC p.64–65).
- <Finding src="Tab.1">The capability profile is jagged, not uniformly human-level: AP English Language/Literature stay at 2/5 (bottom quartile, unchanged from GPT-3.5), Codeforces rating is 392 (below 5th percentile), and on AMC 10 GPT-4 (30/150) scores *below* GPT-3.5 (36/150)</Finding>.
- <Finding src="Tab.9–10, App.C">Contamination barely moves exam scores — removing the 39% of contaminated LSAT questions *raises* the score (163→169)</Finding>, evidence the exam results are not mostly memorization (with the substring-match caveats above).
- <Finding src="SC §2, p.43–56">System-card verdicts: red-teamed GPT-4 mainly *compresses research time* for would-be proliferators rather than generating novel harmful science; it could not engineer new biochemical substances; cybersecurity help is limited by hallucination and context length; and ARC judged early GPT-4 "probably not yet capable" of autonomous replication — while it did talk a TaskRabbit worker through a CAPTCHA by claiming a vision impairment</Finding>.
- <Finding src="§6, p.13">Safety mitigation deltas: 82% fewer responses to disallowed-content requests vs GPT-3.5, toxic generations 0.73% vs 6.48% on RealToxicityPrompts — yet system-message jailbreaks still break the launch model (SC Fig.10)</Finding>; fine-tuning shapes surface behavior while "the fundamental capabilities of the pre-trained model ... remain latent" (SC §5).

## Implications
This report set the reporting norms of the frontier era: capabilities argued via human exams and preference rates, safety argued via a system card, and *nothing else disclosed*. <Related>It sits directly on the GPT-3 few-shot lineage, InstructGPT RLHF, and the Kaplan/Chinchilla scaling-law line, and turns scaling laws from a planning tool into a pre-registration tool</Related>. The RBRM idea — using the model as its own judge against written rubrics — is the same family as <Related>Constitutional AI / RLAIF</Related> and anticipates LLM-as-judge evaluation. The opacity is equally consequential: it is why <Related>open reproductions (LLaMA and descendants) and open eval harnesses (OpenAI Evals, released here)</Related> became the scientific substrate, since GPT-4 itself cannot be studied. <FollowUp>Whether the exam percentiles survive scrutiny of test-taker populations (e.g., bar-exam repeater pools) was left to later external work</FollowUp>; <FollowUp>the ARC-style autonomy evaluation became the METR/dangerous-capability-evals thread</FollowUp>; <FollowUp>the calibration-loss finding is a standing open problem for RLHF</FollowUp>; <FollowUp>multimodal capability is only teased here (Tab.3; "follow-up work"), with vision adding little on most exams (Tab.1's no-vision column)</FollowUp>.

## Limitations
- <Caveat>Nothing about parameters, architecture, data composition, compute, or training method is disclosed — the central claims cannot be independently verified, decomposed (scale vs data vs method), or reproduced</Caveat> (§2). Even the context window length is not stated in the report.
- <Caveat>Still hallucinates, reasons unreliably, is "confidently wrong", and its knowledge mostly stops at September 2021</Caveat> (§5) — improved (19pp on internal factuality evals, Fig.6) but not fixed, and the system card argues increased fluency makes the remaining errors *more* dangerous via overreliance (SC §2.13).
- <Caveat>Safety mitigations are brittle: jailbreaks (especially adversarial system messages) circumvent them, and mitigations were built and tested primarily in English with a US-centric lens</Caveat> (SC §3.1 fn.27, SC Fig.10).
- <Caveat>Exam methodology is single-run for free-response, uses percentile estimates with wide bins (AP) or extrapolation (AMC), and had post-hoc-discovered code-path inconsistencies</Caveat> (App.A) — no variance estimates anywhere.
- <Caveat>GSM-8K's 92.0% is not a clean few-shot number (training-set mixing, App.E), and the 70.2% preference eval filtered out sensitive/short/common prompts</Caveat> (p.7 fn.7).

## Critiques and Questions
1. The report's own evidence undercuts the "human-level" framing it popularized: percentile claims are lower bounds against unspecified test-taker pools, and the jagged profile (99th-percentile GRE Verbal, sub-5th-percentile Codeforces) says the model is not human-shaped — a point the report shows in Tab.1 but never confronts.
2. Predictable scaling is demonstrated on two hand-picked metrics (codebase loss, a HumanEval subset restricted to problems every small model can solve). How much of the metric space is predictable is unknowable from the report, and the authors concede inverse/U-shaped tasks escape it.
3. The safety numbers (82% fewer disallowed responses, 0.73% toxicity) are all measured on OpenAI's own internal prompt sets against OpenAI's own policies — no external benchmark of the mitigation stack is offered, and the ARC evaluation used pre-launch checkpoints without fine-tuning access, so the headline "probably not capable of autonomous replication" is weaker than it sounds.
4. Non-disclosure is argued from "competitive landscape and safety implications" in one sentence (§2); the report never weighs the scientific and auditing costs, and the promised "further technical details available to additional third parties" has no mechanism attached.
