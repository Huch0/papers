<TLDR>For a fixed training-compute budget, model size and training tokens should scale in **equal proportion** (double the model ⇒ double the data) — validated by Chinchilla, a 70B model trained on 1.4T tokens with Gopher's exact compute budget that beats the 4×-larger Gopher on essentially every benchmark.</TLDR>

<WhyItMatters>This is the paper that reset how the field sizes language models: "Chinchilla-optimal" replaced Kaplan scaling as the default sizing rule, and it reframed *data*, not parameters, as the binding constraint on LLM progress. Essential foundation for reading any later pretraining or scaling paper.</WhyItMatters>

## Motivation

You typically get **one shot** at training a frontier model: the budget — how many accelerators, for how long — is fixed before you start. So the allocation question "given <M tex="C" /> FLOPs, how big a model on how many tokens?" must be answered *in advance*, and <Problem>if the allocation rule is wrong, every flagship run wastes its budget</Problem>.

The rule the field was using came from <Related>Kaplan et al. (2020)</Related>: per 10× more compute, grow the model 5.5× and the data only 1.8× (§1). The field obeyed — GPT-3 (175B), Jurassic-1 (178B), Gopher (280B), MT-NLG (530B) were all trained on roughly 300B tokens regardless of size (Tab.1). We saw two structural flaws in that estimate: <Problem>Kaplan et al. reused one fixed ~130B-token learning-rate schedule for every run, so every intermediate loss overestimates what a properly-scheduled shorter run achieves — systematically undervaluing training on more data</Problem> (§2); and <Problem>their fits were dominated by very small models (mostly under 100M params), while the frontier shows curvature at scale</Problem> (§2, App.E).

## Contribution (what's new)

<Novelty>The compute-optimal frontier is symmetric: <M tex="N_{opt}\propto C^{0.5}" /> and <M tex="D_{opt}\propto C^{0.5}" /> — every doubling of model size demands a doubling of training tokens</Novelty>, which means the 2022 generation of LLMs was drastically oversized and undertrained. The measurement *has* to differ from Kaplan et al.'s: <Novelty>the cosine learning-rate schedule length must be matched to each run's token horizon</Novelty>, otherwise the schedule artifact above re-enters and biases you toward "params matter more". We estimate the frontier three independent ways, and then make the result falsifiable with one decisive bet: <Novelty>Chinchilla — the same 5.76e23 FLOPs as Gopher, spent as 70B params × 1.4T tokens instead of 280B × 300B</Novelty> (§4).

## Research questions

1. Given a fixed FLOPs budget <M tex="C" />, what <M tex="(N, D)" /> minimizes final pretraining loss <M tex="L(N, D)" /> subject to <M tex="\mathrm{FLOPs}(N,D)=C" />? (Eq.1)
2. Are current large models correctly sized for their budgets — and if not, by how much?
3. Does the loss-optimal reallocation actually win *downstream* (few-shot benchmarks), not just in training loss?

## Methodology

Train over 400 models (70M–16B params, 5B–500B tokens), then estimate the frontier three ways that share no fitting machinery. Carry one example through everything: **Gopher's budget, 5.76e23 FLOPs** — Kaplan's rule spent it as 280B params on 300B tokens; each of our estimators answers what it *should* have bought.

The parametric form is a classical risk decomposition (App.D.2):

<MathBlock>{`\\hat{L}(N, D) = E + \\frac{A}{N^{\\alpha}} + \\frac{B}{D^{\\beta}}`}</MathBlock>

<M tex="E" /> is the irreducible entropy of text, the <M tex="A/N^{\alpha}" /> term is finite-capacity error, and <M tex="B/D^{\beta}" /> is the cost of a single finite pass over the data. Fitted values: <M tex="E=1.69" />, <M tex="A=406.4" />, <M tex="B=410.7" />, <M tex="\alpha=0.34" />, <M tex="\beta=0.28" /> (Eq.10). The frontier exponents fall out in closed form as <M tex="a=\beta/(\alpha+\beta)" />, <M tex="b=\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)" /> (Eq.4). All analysis uses smoothed *training* loss as an unbiased test-loss estimate — valid only because every run sees less than one epoch (§1 fn.2).
</Pass>

<Pass title="Chinchilla training setup (§4.1)">
Same architecture and data (<Term def="DeepMind's 10TB-scale curated text corpus: MassiveWeb 45%, Books 30%, C4 10%, News 10%, GitHub 4%, Wikipedia 1% for Chinchilla">MassiveText</Term>) as Gopher, so the comparison isolates the allocation. Differences: 80 layers but d_model 8,192 vs 16,384 and 64 vs 128 heads; 4× higher max LR (1e-4); batch 1.5M→3M tokens (Tab.4); AdamW instead of Adam; a SentencePiece tokenizer without NFKC normalization (94.15% token overlap with Gopher's — helps math/chemistry text); bfloat16 compute with a float32 master copy of weights in the optimizer state. Trained on TPUv3/v4 with JAX. Data reweighted for 1.4T tokens — Wikipedia is repeated 3.40 epochs, MassiveWeb 1.24 (Tab.A1).
</Pass>

## Main result

At identical training compute, the smaller-but-longer-trained model wins nearly everywhere: <Finding src="Tab.6, p.11">MMLU 5-shot 67.6% vs Gopher's 60.0% (+7.6), beating GPT-3 (43.9%) and even the expert-forecast SOTA for June **2023** (63.4%)</Finding>, with more than 90% accuracy on 4 of 57 subjects — the first model to do so on any subject.

## Key findings

- <Finding src="Tab.2">Three independent estimators converge on equal scaling: a/b = 0.50/0.50, 0.49/0.51, 0.46/0.54 — versus Kaplan et al.'s 0.73/0.27</Finding>: the conclusion is not an artifact of one fitting method.
- <Finding src="Fig.A1, App.B">The schedule artifact is real and large: a cosine cycle that overshoots the training horizon by more than ~25% visibly degrades final loss</Finding> — this is the mechanism by which Kaplan et al.'s fixed-length schedule inflated intermediate losses and undervalued data.
- <Finding src="Tab.3">The implied undertraining is enormous: a 175B (GPT-3-sized) model is compute-optimal only with ~3.85e24 FLOPs and 3.7T tokens; a 1T-param model is not optimal below ~1e26 FLOPs (220+ Gopher budgets)</Finding> — so data collection, not model engineering, becomes the bottleneck for further scaling.
- <Finding src="Tab.A2">The equal-scaling law replicates on C4 (0.50/0.50) and GitHub code (0.53/0.47)</Finding>: within the single-epoch regime the allocation rule is dataset-independent.
- <Finding src="Fig.A4, App.D.4">A direct small-scale duel at 1e21 FLOPs confirms the fit: our predicted-optimal 2.80B model ends at lower loss than the Kaplan-predicted 4.74B model</Finding> — the same experiment that Chinchilla-vs-Gopher runs at 5.76e23.
- <Finding src="§4.2.5">TruthfulQA improves 14.1% 0-shot (43.6% vs 29.5%) purely from better pretraining</Finding> — contradicting the prior narrative that better language models are necessarily more prone to imitative falsehoods.
- <Finding src="App.E, §3.4">The FLOP-loss frontier shows concavity at high compute, and Approach 3 (which downweights small runs) predicts the smallest optimal models</Finding> — meaning even this paper likely *over*estimates optimal model size at large budgets.
- <Finding src="Fig.A6–A7, App.G">The optimizer choices matter independently: AdamW beats Adam and a float32 master-weight copy helps at every scale tested</Finding> — though AdamW's advantage only materializes in the last ~20% of the cosine cycle (fn.8).

## Implications

The sizing rule for the field changed overnight: <Related>Gopher and GPT-3-era models</Related> were retroactively revealed as ~4× oversized, and subsequent models (the LLaMA line most explicitly) trained smaller models on far more tokens — often deliberately *past* Chinchilla-optimal, since inference cost amortizes over a model's lifetime, a trade-off this paper motivates but does not formalize. The result also elevates <Related>data-side approaches like retrieval augmentation (RETRO), which effectively multiply tokens seen</Related>. Threads worth chasing: <FollowUp>what replaces the law when data runs out — the multi-epoch / data-constrained regime the paper explicitly excludes</FollowUp>, <FollowUp>inference-aware "overtraining" as an explicit objective</FollowUp>, and <FollowUp>whether the fitted exponents (α=0.34, β=0.28 — both below the theoretical 1/2) can be raised by better architectures or data quality</FollowUp>.

## Limitations

- <Caveat>Only two comparable runs exist at frontier scale (Chinchilla and Gopher) with nothing at intermediate scales</Caveat> — the extrapolation spans two orders of magnitude on faith in power laws (§5).
- <Caveat>The power-law form is assumed despite observed concavity at high budgets (App.E), so optimal sizes at large C may be overestimated</Caveat> (§5).
- <Caveat>Everything is single-epoch; the analysis is silent about repeating data</Caveat> — yet its own projections (e.g. 3.7T+ tokens for 175B, Tab.3) exceed the curated data available at the time, making the multi-epoch regime unavoidable (§5).
- <Caveat>Chinchilla is trained once (no variance estimate, model card Tab.A8) and differs from Gopher in optimizer, tokenizer, LR, and batch size — App.G shows the optimizer change alone improves loss</Caveat>, so the headline gap is allocation *plus* tuning, not allocation alone.
- <Caveat>Language-modelling comparisons (Pile, Wikitext103) are confounded by 4× more training data raising leakage risk — the paper itself redirects emphasis to MMLU/BIG-bench for this reason</Caveat> (§4.2.1).

## Critiques and Questions

1. **Internal number inconsistencies.** The abstract says MMLU 67.5% but Tab.6 says 67.6%; §3.4 prose says a 175B model needs 4.41e24 FLOPs / 4.2T tokens while Tab.3 (Approach 1) says 3.85e24 / 3.7T; §4.2.6 prose quotes Gopher NQ at 21%/28% while Tab.9 says 24.5%/28.2%. All minor, but they suggest prose written against a different fit or model revision than the tables.
2. **"Compute-optimal" optimizes the wrong objective for practice.** The frontier minimizes *pretraining loss per training FLOP*; total lifetime cost (training + inference) would push optima to even smaller models trained longer. The paper argues this qualitatively (§1, §4) but never re-derives the frontier under it.
3. How much of Chinchilla's downstream win comes from lower loss vs from *seeing 4× more facts*? Closed-book QA (Tab.9) plausibly rewards data coverage directly, independent of the loss argument — the paper does not separate the two.
4. The three approaches disagree exactly where it matters most (40B vs 63B vs 67B at the Gopher budget); choosing 70B — outside all three point estimates — was justified by "dataset and computational efficiency considerations" (§4), i.e., partially pragmatic.

## Glossary

- <Term def="Choosing model size N and training tokens D to minimize final loss subject to a fixed total training FLOPs budget C">compute-optimal</Term>
- <Term def="The approximation FLOPs ≈ 6·N·D for training a transformer with N params on D tokens (2 for forward, 4 for backward); this paper's exact accounting stays within 1.10× of it (Tab.A4)">6ND approximation</Term>
- <Term def="A curve of final loss vs model size at one fixed total-FLOP budget; its valley locates the optimal size for that budget">IsoFLOP profile</Term>
- <Term def="Learning-rate schedule following a cosine decay (here by 10×) over a set token horizon; its length must match the horizon or final loss suffers">cosine cycle</Term>
- <Term def="57-subject exam-style multiple-choice benchmark (Massive Multitask Language Understanding); human expert ceiling ~89.8%">MMLU</Term>
- <Term def="Collaborative suite of 200+ diverse language tasks (Beyond the Imitation Game); this paper evaluates 62 of them">BIG-bench</Term>
- <Term def="DeepMind's 280B-parameter LLM (Rae et al., 2021), trained on 300B MassiveText tokens — the compute-matched baseline throughout">Gopher</Term>
- <Term def="Bits-per-byte: language-modelling loss normalized per byte of raw text, comparable across tokenizers">bpb</Term>
- <Term def="Robust regression loss, quadratic near zero and linear in the tails, which prevents small-compute outlier runs from dominating the parametric fit">Huber loss</Term>
