<QA question="What exactly is inverse dynamics, and what does 'shifting action learning from dense state-action imitation to inverse dynamics' mean?" asked_by="Huch0" concepts="">
**Forward vs inverse.** Forward dynamics asks: given the current state and an action, what state comes next?
Inverse dynamics asks the reverse: given where you are and where you want to be next, which action produces
that transition? Classically this is the torque computation for a desired trajectory; in learned policies an
**inverse dynamics model (IDM)** maps a pair of observations <M tex="(o_t,\, o_{t+1})" /> to the action <M tex="a_t" /> that connects them.

**Where it appears here.** Eq. 1 (p.6) factorizes DreamZero's joint prediction into exactly two terms —
<M tex="\pi(o_{t:t+H} \mid o_{0:t},\, c,\, q_t)" /> (video prediction) times <M tex="\pi(a_{t:t+H} \mid o_{0:t+H},\, q_t)" /> (the IDM). Read the second
term closely: it conditions on observations *through the future horizon* and on proprioception <M tex="q_t" />, but the
language instruction <M tex="c" /> has dropped out. That is the whole idea in one line — once the model has imagined the
next 1.6 s of video, the instruction is no longer needed to choose actions, because the imagined future already
encodes what to do.

**Why that is a "shift".** A VLA learns <M tex="p(a \mid o,\, c)" /> in a single step, so its action labels must simultaneously
teach *what should happen* (semantics, goal grounding) and *how to move* (embodiment-specific dynamics). Every
bit of that has to be paid for with teleoperated action labels, which is why the conventional recipe needs many
tightly repeated demonstrations per task. Under the WAM factorization the "what" is carried by video prediction —
inherited from web-scale video pretraining and learnable with no action labels at all — leaving robot action
labels responsible only for the narrower question "given this visual future, which motor commands realize it?".
The paper argues this narrower target is what makes heterogeneous data usable (Sec. 4.1) and what makes
cross-embodiment transfer cheap: video from another robot or a human can teach the "what", while a small amount
of target-robot data teaches the "how".

**Two precisions worth keeping.** (1) DreamZero does **not** train a separate IDM. The factorization is
conceptual; one 14B model is trained end-to-end, so the IDM is *implicit* — the authors argue this gives tighter
video-action alignment than the two-model designs of prior work (p.7). (2) The paper concedes it cannot measure
how good that implicit IDM is: "Quantifying the accuracy of implicit IDMs remains a challenge" (p.19), and it
expects the visual-future-to-command mapping to grow combinatorially with kinematic complexity. Observed failures
are mostly video-prediction errors rather than action extraction (p.17) — consistent with the split, but not
proof of it.
</QA>

<QA question="What are 'repeated demonstrations', and why does the paper make a point of not relying on them?" asked_by="Huch0" concepts="">
**The conventional recipe.** Standard robot-learning data collection fixes a task list and records many
demonstrations *per task* in a controlled setting, with similar object positions and configurations each time.
The paper's own comparison set is "70 tasks with many repeated demonstrations per task using similar object
positions and configurations" (Sec. 5.3), versus "hundreds of demonstrations per task in controlled lab settings"
(App. E). Repetition is deliberate: it averages out teleoperation noise and pins down one consistent behaviour
per task.

**What DreamZero did instead.** ~500 h across 22 real environments (homes, restaurants, supermarkets, coffee
shops, offices, warehouses, laboratories, hotels). Teleoperators pick **three coarse tasks per episode** from a
printed sheet — "tidy up" rather than "place the red block on the blue block" — and execute them consecutively,
giving ~4.4-minute episodes containing roughly 42 subtasks each. The mechanism that produces the long tail is a
repetition **ceiling**: once a task has been collected in 50 episodes it is *deprecated* and removed from the
sheet, and operators are incentivized to propose new ones, so the task distribution keeps expanding (App. E.1).

**Why this matters for a WAM specifically.** Because video prediction is inherited from pretraining, the only
thing left to learn from robot data is the inverse dynamics — and "a robust IDM requires diverse state-action
correspondences across varied contexts, which repetitive data inherently lacks" (Sec. 5.3). The ablation holds
the budget fixed at 500 h and varies only *composition*: diverse data reaches **50%** task progress against
**33%** for repetitive data (Tab. 4).

**Your reading of the related work is right.** This is not aimed only at VLAs. The paper states that although
other WAMs already show video priors improve sample efficiency for action learning, "most works still focus on
repeated demonstrations" (Sec. 1, citing Liao et al. 2025 and Pai et al. 2025). The claimed novelty is therefore
the **data regime**, not the video prior itself.

**Caveat for the talk.** "Non-repetitive" is a ceiling, not zero — up to 50 episodes per task is still meaningful
repetition — and the 50%-vs-33% result is a single ablation.
</QA>

<QA question="What is YAM?" asked_by="Huch0" concepts="">
YAM is the name of the **second robot platform**, used as the cross-embodiment counterpart to the main AgiBot G1.
The paper never expands the acronym; it introduces it only as "the bimanual YAM robot" and "a new bimanual
manipulator (YAM robot)". The property that carries the argument is that YAM and AgiBot G1 are **both bimanual
parallel-gripper systems**, i.e. a narrow embodiment gap.

It plays **two different roles**, which are easy to conflate:

1. **Source of transfer data** (robot-to-robot, Sec. 5.2): 72 multi-view trajectories over 9 unseen tasks
   (8 per task, ~20 min of YAM data), co-trained 1:1 with pretraining data for 10K steps. This lifts *AgiBot*
   task progress from **38.3% to 55.4%**. The demonstrations are video-only — no YAM action labels are used.
2. **Target of adaptation** (few-shot embodiment adaptation, Sec. 5.2): the AgiBot-pretrained checkpoint is
   post-trained on 55 YAM trajectories over 11 tasks (~30 min) and then controls *YAM itself*. This result is
   **qualitative only** — Fig. 12 shows retained language following and generalization to unseen objects
   (pumpkins, teddy bears, pens, cup noodles, paper bags), but no success rate, baseline, or trial count is
   reported for it.

Their hypothesis for why so little data suffices: the visual and morphological similarity of the two platforms,
plus the sample-efficiency of learning an implicit IDM rather than a full policy (p.17).

**If asked on stage:** the expansion of "YAM" is not stated anywhere in the paper. Describe it as "a bimanual
parallel-gripper manipulator used as the second embodiment" rather than guessing the acronym.
</QA>
