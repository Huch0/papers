<QA question="What exactly is inverse dynamics, and what does 'shifting action learning from dense state-action imitation to inverse dynamics' mean?" asked_by="Huch0" concepts="">
**Forward vs inverse.** Forward dynamics asks: given the current state and an action, what state comes next?
Inverse dynamics asks the reverse: given where you are and where you want to be next, which action produces
that transition? Classically this is the torque computation for a desired trajectory; in learned policies an
**inverse dynamics model (IDM)** maps a pair of observations <M tex="(o_t,\, o_{t+1})" /> to the action <M tex="a_t" /> that connects them.

**Where it appears here.** Eq. 1 (p.6) factorizes DreamZero's joint prediction into exactly two terms —
<M tex="\pi(o_{t:t+H} \mid o_{0:t},\, c,\, q_t)" /> (video prediction) times <M tex="\pi(a_{t:t+H} \mid o_{0:t+H},\, q_t)" /> (the IDM). Read the second
term closely: it conditions on observations *through the future horizon* and on proprioception <M tex="q_t" />, but the
language instruction <M tex="c" /> has dropped out. That is the whole idea in one line — once the model has imagined the
next 1.6 s of video, the instruction is no longer needed to choose actions, because the imagined future already
encodes what to do.

**Why that is a "shift".** A VLA learns <M tex="p(a \mid o,\, c)" /> in a single step, so its action labels must simultaneously
teach *what should happen* (semantics, goal grounding) and *how to move* (embodiment-specific dynamics). Every
bit of that has to be paid for with teleoperated action labels, which is why the conventional recipe needs many
tightly repeated demonstrations per task. Under the WAM factorization the "what" is carried by video prediction —
inherited from web-scale video pretraining and learnable with no action labels at all — leaving robot action
labels responsible only for the narrower question "given this visual future, which motor commands realize it?".
The paper argues this narrower target is what makes heterogeneous data usable (Sec. 4.1) and what makes
cross-embodiment transfer cheap: video from another robot or a human can teach the "what", while a small amount
of target-robot data teaches the "how".

**Two precisions worth keeping.** (1) DreamZero does **not** train a separate IDM. The factorization is
conceptual; one 14B model is trained end-to-end, so the IDM is *implicit* — the authors argue this gives tighter
video-action alignment than the two-model designs of prior work (p.7). (2) The paper concedes it cannot measure
how good that implicit IDM is: "Quantifying the accuracy of implicit IDMs remains a challenge" (p.19), and it
expects the visual-future-to-command mapping to grow combinatorially with kinematic complexity. Observed failures
are mostly video-prediction errors rather than action extraction (p.17) — consistent with the split, but not
proof of it.
</QA>

<QA question="What are 'repeated demonstrations', and why does the paper make a point of not relying on them?" asked_by="Huch0" concepts="">
**The conventional recipe.** Standard robot-learning data collection fixes a task list and records many
demonstrations *per task* in a controlled setting, with similar object positions and configurations each time.
The paper's own comparison set is "70 tasks with many repeated demonstrations per task using similar object
positions and configurations" (Sec. 5.3), versus "hundreds of demonstrations per task in controlled lab settings"
(App. E). Repetition is deliberate: it averages out teleoperation noise and pins down one consistent behaviour
per task.

**What DreamZero did instead.** ~500 h across 22 real environments (homes, restaurants, supermarkets, coffee
shops, offices, warehouses, laboratories, hotels). Teleoperators pick **three coarse tasks per episode** from a
printed sheet — "tidy up" rather than "place the red block on the blue block" — and execute them consecutively,
giving ~4.4-minute episodes containing roughly 42 subtasks each. The mechanism that produces the long tail is a
repetition **ceiling**: once a task has been collected in 50 episodes it is *deprecated* and removed from the
sheet, and operators are incentivized to propose new ones, so the task distribution keeps expanding (App. E.1).

**Why this matters for a WAM specifically.** Because video prediction is inherited from pretraining, the only
thing left to learn from robot data is the inverse dynamics — and "a robust IDM requires diverse state-action
correspondences across varied contexts, which repetitive data inherently lacks" (Sec. 5.3). The ablation holds
the budget fixed at 500 h and varies only *composition*: diverse data reaches **50%** task progress against
**33%** for repetitive data (Tab. 4).

**Your reading of the related work is right.** This is not aimed only at VLAs. The paper states that although
other WAMs already show video priors improve sample efficiency for action learning, "most works still focus on
repeated demonstrations" (Sec. 1, citing Liao et al. 2025 and Pai et al. 2025). The claimed novelty is therefore
the **data regime**, not the video prior itself.

**Caveat for the talk.** "Non-repetitive" is a ceiling, not zero — up to 50 episodes per task is still meaningful
repetition — and the 50%-vs-33% result is a single ablation.
</QA>

<QA question="What is YAM?" asked_by="Huch0" concepts="">
YAM is the name of the **second robot platform**, used as the cross-embodiment counterpart to the main AgiBot G1.
The paper never expands the acronym; it introduces it only as "the bimanual YAM robot" and "a new bimanual
manipulator (YAM robot)". The property that carries the argument is that YAM and AgiBot G1 are **both bimanual
parallel-gripper systems**, i.e. a narrow embodiment gap.

It plays **two different roles**, which are easy to conflate:

1. **Source of transfer data** (robot-to-robot, Sec. 5.2): 72 multi-view trajectories over 9 unseen tasks
   (8 per task, ~20 min of YAM data), co-trained 1:1 with pretraining data for 10K steps. This lifts *AgiBot*
   task progress from **38.3% to 55.4%**. The demonstrations are video-only — no YAM action labels are used.
2. **Target of adaptation** (few-shot embodiment adaptation, Sec. 5.2): the AgiBot-pretrained checkpoint is
   post-trained on 55 YAM trajectories over 11 tasks (~30 min) and then controls *YAM itself*. This result is
   **qualitative only** — Fig. 12 shows retained language following and generalization to unseen objects
   (pumpkins, teddy bears, pens, cup noodles, paper bags), but no success rate, baseline, or trial count is
   reported for it.

Their hypothesis for why so little data suffices: the visual and morphological similarity of the two platforms,
plus the sample-efficiency of learning an implicit IDM rather than a full policy (p.17).

**If asked on stage:** the expansion of "YAM" is not stated anywhere in the paper. Describe it as "a bimanual
parallel-gripper manipulator used as the second embodiment" rather than guessing the acronym.
</QA>

<QA question="Is 'diversity' really the axis the data ablation varies? Repetition and diversity seem orthogonal - a dataset can cover many contexts and still contain many near-duplicate samples per context." asked_by="Huch0" concepts="">
**The objection is correct, and the paper does not resolve it.** Diversity (how many distinct
task-context-placement combinations the corpus covers) and redundancy (how many near-duplicate samples sit
inside each combination) are logically independent. A corpus can be high-coverage *and* highly repetitive —
70 tasks across 22 environments with 300 demonstrations each. The paper never builds that third condition.

**What the ablation actually varies.** Table 4 (p.17) compares two corpora at 500 h each: the diverse corpus
(22 environments, three coarse tasks per episode, ~42 subtasks per episode, every task deprecated after 50
episodes so operators must invent new ones) against a repetitive corpus described in a single clause — "70 tasks
with many repeated demonstrations per task using similar object positions and configurations" (Sec. 5.3, p.17).
At least four things differ at once:

1. number of distinct tasks (a forced long tail vs 70);
2. repeats per task (capped at 50 vs "many");
3. within-task state variation (real-site placements vs *similar object positions and configurations*);
4. episode structure (multi-task episodes vs, presumably, single-task) — never stated for the repetitive set.

**Why the two axes cannot be separated here.** Holding the budget fixed at 500 h mechanically couples (1) and
(2): more repeats per task buys fewer distinct tasks. So the experiment compares two points on a
coverage-versus-redundancy trade-off curve; it does not isolate either axis. A controlled test would need a
2x2 — high/low coverage crossed with high/low redundancy at matched hours — or a design that fixes the number
of distinct (task, context) pairs and varies only repeats per pair.

**Which factor the mechanism story actually implicates.** Their argument is about the IDM needing "diverse
state-action correspondences across varied contexts". Note that factor 3 does most of that work: repeating a task
with the *same* object placement yields nearly the same visual-future-to-action pairs, so the extra samples cost
budget without adding distinct correspondences. Read that way, redundancy matters precisely *because* it
displaces coverage — the axes are orthogonal in principle but coupled by the budget constraint, which is exactly
your point.

**One precision that sharpens the comparison.** Both ablation runs are trained for 50K steps at batch size 32
(Sec. 5.2, p.16), so they draw the *same number of training samples*. The difference is therefore distributional —
the entropy of what gets sampled — not dataset size. That makes "effective distinct correspondences per sample
drawn" a better name for the axis than "diversity".

**Status: Unclear.** The paper reports no decomposition, no intermediate points, and no description of the
repetitive corpus beyond that one clause — including whether its 70 tasks overlap the PnP-Easy evaluation set.
The claim rests on one pair of numbers (33% vs 50%) on one task category at reduced training scale.
</QA>

<QA question="How exactly does the action output work, and how is it mapped across embodiments for cross-embodiment transfer?" asked_by="Huch0" concepts="">
**The action pathway.** Actions are **relative joint positions** (deltas, not absolute targets or end-effector
poses), normalized, with idle actions filtered from training (Sec. 4.1, p.11). The model emits an action chunk of
horizon <M tex="H = 48" /> alongside <M tex="K = 2" /> latent video frames; at a 30 Hz control frequency that is
**1.6 s of video and 1.6 s of actions per chunk** (App. B, p.21). Architecturally the backbone stays a video
model: the only added parameters are a state encoder (proprioception <M tex="q_t" />), an action encoder, and an
action decoder — text encoder, image encoder and VAE are frozen, and video latents and action latents are
denoised together under one flow-matching loss with modality-aware attention masking (App. C, Fig. 14). At
run time the chunk goes to an asynchronous controller executing at 30 Hz, while the model re-plans the next chunk
at roughly 7 Hz on two GB200s.

**Your premise is what the design avoids.** For transfer, DreamZero never maps one robot's actions onto another's.
The transfer data is **video only** — the paper is explicit: "our method relies solely on visual information
**without action labels**" (Sec. 5.2, p.16). The 20 minutes of YAM footage and 12 minutes of egocentric human
footage update only the *video-prediction* half of the factorization: what the task looks like, how the motion
unfolds, which object goes where. The actions that actually execute are still produced by the **AgiBot** action
decoder trained on AgiBot data, in AgiBot's own joint space. The shared cross-embodiment representation is
**pixels, not commands** — which is the practical payoff of putting video prediction, rather than action
prediction, at the centre of the model. Mechanically it is a co-train from the AgiBot checkpoint on a 1:1 mix
with pretraining data for 10K steps (Sec. 5.2, p.16).

**What is genuinely not reported.** (1) How the joint video+action loss is handled for action-free samples —
presumably the action term is masked, but the paper never says. (2) The other direction, few-shot adaptation
*to* YAM, does require YAM actions, since the policy then controls YAM — yet the paper does not describe how the
action encoder/decoder is adapted to a different action space: whether the heads are re-initialized, how joint
layouts and normalization statistics are handled, or what YAM's action dimensionality is. It reports only that
55 trajectories (~30 min) suffice, qualitatively.

**Consistent with their own limitations section.** The embodiment-specific part of the model is exactly the
inverse dynamics: "Higher-DOF robots will require more play data to learn an accurate implicit IDM, as the mapping
from visual futures to motor commands grows combinatorially with kinematic complexity" (Sec. 6, p.19). Video
transfers across bodies; the visual-future-to-motor-command mapping does not, and must be re-learned per robot.

**A caution worth keeping.** Both transfer settings pair robots that are already close — AgiBot G1 and YAM are
both bimanual parallel-gripper systems, which the authors themselves credit for the efficiency. Nothing here
demonstrates transfer across a genuinely different morphology.
</QA>

<QA question="Does that mean the IDM can only transfer to embodiments sharing AgiBot's joint space? And what does 'zero-shot' actually range over in this paper?" asked_by="Huch0" concepts="">
**Two different axes, and the paper is careful to keep them apart — so we should be too.**

**"Zero-shot" here means zero-shot *task* transfer, never zero-shot *embodiment* transfer.** Every use of the
term attaches to tasks and environments: "zero-shot generalization to new tasks in new environments" (Sec. 1,
p.3), "zero-shot generalization to unseen verbs and motions" (Sec. 1, p.4), "zero-shot generalization to novel
tasks" (Sec. 2.2, p.5). The embodiment axis is always labelled **few-shot**: "few-shot adaptation to new robots"
(Sec. 1, p.2), and Q5/Fig. 12 are titled "Few-shot Embodiment Adaptation" (Sec. 5.2, p.16). The sentence that
joins them — "adapts to an entirely new robot (YAM) with only 30 minutes of play data, **retaining** zero-shot
generalization" (Sec. 1, p.3) — means the *task-level* generalization survives the *embodiment-level* adaptation.
The title's "Zero-shot Policies" is a claim about tasks, never about bodies.

**So: is the IDM locked to AgiBot's joint space?** Partly, and only in the zero-shot sense.

- **Yes, zero-shot.** The action decoder emits relative joint positions in one robot's joint space. Drop
  DreamZero-AgiBot onto a different robot untouched and the commands are meaningless. Nothing in the paper claims
  otherwise.
- **No, not permanently.** They **post-trained the DreamZero-AgiBot checkpoint onto YAM** — 55 trajectories over
  11 tasks, about 30 minutes — after which the model controls YAM (Sec. 5.2, p.16-17). The IDM is therefore
  *re-fittable*, not joint-space-locked. The claim is portability-with-cheap-adaptation, not zero-shot portability.

**Why the transfer direction matters.** The two cross-embodiment results move in opposite directions and only one
of them touches the IDM at all:

- *Video-only transfer (YAM or human video, then act on AgiBot)*: no action labels cross the boundary, so no
  action space is ever mapped. The executed commands stay in AgiBot's decoder. The IDM is untouched.
- *Few-shot embodiment adaptation (act on YAM)*: this is the only place the action side must change, and it is
  re-fit from ~30 min of target-robot data.

**What is not reported, and matters for exactly this question.** The paper never states either robot's joint
dimensionality, nor whether the action encoder/decoder is re-initialized, resized or reused for YAM, nor how
normalization statistics are set. So we cannot tell whether the adaptation fine-tuned an existing head because the
dimensions happen to coincide, or fitted a fresh one. The result is also qualitative only — no success rate,
baseline or trial count.

**Scope of the evidence.** Both robots are bimanual parallel-gripper systems, which the authors themselves credit
for the efficiency (Sec. 5.2, p.17), so this is the easiest possible pair. The DROID/Franka **single-arm** model
is a separate training run, not a transfer, so it shows the architecture is embodiment-general without showing an
IDM moving between bodies. Their own limitations section predicts the cost grows with kinematic complexity:
"Higher-DOF robots will require more play data to learn an accurate implicit IDM" (Sec. 6, p.19).

**The three layers, ranked by portability.**

| Layer | Crosses embodiments? |
|---|---|
| Web-scale video priors (pretraining) | Fully shared; no robot data involved |
| Task and motion semantics — what should happen | **Yes, through video alone**, with no action labels — the paper's central novelty |
| Visual-future-to-command readout (the IDM) | **Not as-is.** Embodiment-specific; claimed cheap to re-fit (~30 min), shown once, qualitatively, between near-identical bodies |

One line for a talk: *video is the interlingua, the action head is the local dialect — you re-learn the dialect,
but not the language.*
</QA>
