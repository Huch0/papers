<TLDR>DreamZero turns a 14B pretrained video-diffusion model into a robot policy by denoising future video frames and action chunks under one shared objective — the policy first imagines the next 1.6 s of the world, then reads its motor commands off that imagination — and NVIDIA reports roughly doubled real-robot generalization to unseen tasks and environments versus state-of-the-art VLAs, at ~7 Hz closed-loop re-planning.</TLDR>

## Motivation — the failure that bugged us (authors' voice)

Let me tell you what kept bothering us about the best robot policies we had. A modern
<Term def="Vision-Language-Action model: a VLM fine-tuned to emit motor actions, e.g. GR00T N1.6, pi-0.5, RT-2, OpenVLA">VLA</Term>
is genuinely smart in one direction and strangely helpless in another: it will "move the coke can
to Taylor Swift" — web knowledge binds the referent, a practiced *move* skill executes — and then
fail to untie a shoelace. In our words: VLAs "excel at semantic generalization but struggle to
generalize to unseen physical motions in novel environments" (Abstract). <Problem>The cause is
where the prior comes from: a VLM is pretrained on static image-text pairs, so it knows *what*
things are, but carries no spatiotemporal prior for *how* a motion unfolds</Problem> — geometry,
contact, dynamics (§1, p.2; §2.1, p.4). And the field's workaround is a treadmill:
<Problem>environment generalization bought by teleoperating the same task across hundreds of
sites, task generalization by enumerating a library of motion primitives</Problem> (§2.1, p.5) —
neither scales to the space of physical interactions. Underneath sits a quieter assumption we
wanted to attack directly: "learning to only predict actions without encoding the knowledge about
future world states makes it challenging to leverage highly heterogeneous, non-repetitive data
effectively, as the model must implicitly infer dynamics from noisy state-action pairs" (§4.1,
p.10). <Problem>Action-only imitation seems to *need* repetitive demonstrations</Problem> — the
opposite of how useful robot data actually gets collected.

## Contribution — imagine first, then act (authors' voice)

But there already exists a model family with exactly the missing prior. Video models watched the
internet; they "encode physical dynamics from internet-scale data" (§2.2, p.5). So we asked the
Feynman question: *if you had a model that could predict the next two seconds of video, what would
be left of the control problem?* Write the joint distribution down and it factorizes (Eq.1, p.6)
into video prediction times an
<Term def="inverse dynamics model: infers the actions that would carry the robot between observed/predicted states">IDM</Term>
— and look closely at the second factor, <M tex="\pi(a_{t:t+H} \mid o_{0:t+H},\, q_t)" />: the
instruction <M tex="c" /> has dropped out. <Novelty>Once the future has been imagined, the
instruction is no longer needed to choose actions — the imagined future already encodes what to
do.</Novelty> That is the whole shift, in our own words: "This shifts action learning from dense
state–action imitation to inverse dynamics—aligning motor commands with predicted visual futures"
(§1, p.3). Video prediction serves "as an implicit visual planner that guides action generation",
and if the reduction holds, "improving robotic capabilities reduces to improving video generation"
(§2.2, p.5).

Three design decisions follow, and each was forced, not chosen. First, we refused to build two
models: "we train a single model end-to-end with joint prediction objective. We believe that this
end-to-end design enables better video-action alignment through a deep integration between the two
modalities" (§3, p.7) — <Novelty>one DiT denoises video latents and action latents under one
flow-matching loss</Novelty>. Second, the backbone had to be autoregressive: "bidirectional
diffusion typically requires processing fixed-length sequences, which often necessitates video
subsampling that distorts native FPS, potentially harming video-action alignment" (§3.1, p.7) —
and chunk-causality is what gives a policy a *now*. We were careful about one asymmetry: "We
introduce autoregressive modeling only for the video modality to avoid error propagation coming
from closed-loop action prediction" (§3.1, p.7). Third — my favorite, because only a robot gets to
do this — <Novelty>after every executed chunk, "ground-truth observations ... replace generated
frames in the KV cache", which "eliminates the compounding error problem inherent to
autoregressive video generation—a key advantage unique to WAMs"</Novelty> (§3.1, p.8): a
daydreamer that opens its eyes every 1.6 seconds. The engineering that makes any of this testable
is the 38× inference stack (§3.2). Even the name is a bet on the factorization rather than the
medium: "video is just one possible world modeling objective—future WAMs may align actions with
other predictive modalities such as tactile sensing, force feedback, or learned latent
representations" (§2.2, p.5).

## Research questions (as we posed them)

Do WAMs (1) learn better than VLAs from *diverse, non-repetitive* robot data, (2) generalize
zero-shot to motions absent from the robot training data, (3) retain that generalization after
task-specific post-training, (4) absorb skill knowledge from *action-free* video of other
embodiments, and (5) adapt to a new robot from minutes of data — and (6) can a 14B video-diffusion
policy run fast enough to close a control loop at all?

### The objective in the paper's notation

DreamZero instantiates flow matching jointly over video latents and action latents. Within chunk
<M tex="k" />, both modalities are corrupted at the *same* level <M tex="t_k" /> — noise at
<M tex="t=0" />, data at <M tex="t=1" /> (Eq. 2) — so the actions always denoise toward the same
imagined future as the video, which is the mechanical meaning of "aligned" prediction:

<Definition term="Joint corruption and velocity target (Eq. 2)">
<MathBlock>{`z_t = t\\,z_1 + (1-t)\\,z_0, \\qquad a_t = t\\,a_1 + (1-t)\\,a_0, \\qquad z_0,\\ a_0 \\sim \\mathcal{N}(0, I)`}</MathBlock>

<MathBlock>{`v \\;=\\; [\\,z_1,\\ a_1\\,] - [\\,z_0,\\ a_0\\,]`}</MathBlock>
</Definition>

<Pass title="The full loss (Eq. 3), CFG parallelism, and the Flash schedule">
One trunk <M tex="u_\theta" /> regresses the joint velocity, conditioned on the clean previous
chunks <M tex="\mathcal{C}_k = \{(z^{i}_{1}, a^{i}_{1})\}_{i=1}^{k-1}" />, the instruction
<M tex="c" /> and proprioception <M tex="q_k" />:

<Definition term="DreamZero training objective (Eq. 3)">
<MathBlock>{`\\mathcal{L}(\\theta) = \\mathbb{E}\\left[ \\frac{1}{M} \\sum_{k=1}^{M} w(t_k)\\, \\bigl\\| u_\\theta\\bigl([\\,z^{k}_{t_k}, a^{k}_{t_k}\\,];\\ \\mathcal{C}_k,\\ c,\\ q_k,\\ t_k\\bigr) - v^{k} \\bigr\\|^{2} \\right]`}</MathBlock>
</Definition>

The per-timestep weight <M tex="w(t_k)" /> is predefined; its form is Not reported. Inference uses
16 denoising steps for smooth actions, cut to ~4 effective DiT calls by caching directionally
consistent velocities (<Finding src="Tab.1, p.10">successive velocities are consistent enough that
caching costs little</Finding>);
<Term def="classifier-free guidance: one conditional and one unconditional forward pass per step, extrapolated to sharpen instruction-following">CFG</Term>
wants two forward passes per step, which DreamZero runs on two GPUs in parallel (−47% per-step
latency, §3.2.3). DreamZero-Flash decouples the shared schedule — video
<M tex="t \sim \mathrm{Beta}(7,1)" /> (concentrated near clean), action <M tex="t" /> uniform
(Alg.1) — to reach 1-step video prediction at a measured cost
(<Finding src="Tab.3, p.17">83% → 74% on table bussing</Finding>).
</Pass>

<Pass title="DreamZero’s real-time loop: 5.7 s → 150 ms, and who waits for whom">
Naively, 16 denoising steps × a 14B DiT ≈ 5.7 s per chunk — the robot would freeze between
thoughts. The paper's fix is architectural, not model-side: run inference and execution
**asynchronously**. The controller streams the current 48-action chunk at 30 Hz (1.6 s of motion)
while the GPU denoises the *next* chunk from the latest observation; the deadline stops being
"finish before moving" and becomes "finish before the current chunk runs out" (~200 ms budget).

### The robot adapters: state encoder, action encoder, action decoder

Everything robot-specific in Figure 4 lives in three deliberately small trained modules that
translate between robot signals and the trunk's token space — the paper introduces them only as
"minimal additional parameters" (§3.1); their exact shapes are Not reported.

- **State encoder** — proprioception <M tex="q_k" /> (joint readings) → token(s) the trunk can
  attend to. This is conditioning: "where my body is now".
- **Action encoder** — the *noisy* chunk of 48 normalized
  <Term def="the action is expressed as a change in each joint angle relative to the current position, rather than an absolute target pose; normalized per-dimension; idle (no-motion) steps are filtered from training">relative joint deltas</Term>
  → action tokens, so the DiT can denoise them exactly like video tokens.
- **Action decoder** — denoised action tokens → the executable 48-step chunk for the 30 Hz
  controller.

Multi-view cameras are handled with the same minimalism: views are tiled into one frame rather than
changing the architecture. The full trained-vs-frozen inventory of Figure 4:
<Finding src="§4.1, p.11">trained — all DiT blocks + the three adapters; frozen — text encoder,
image encoder, VAE (LoRA was tried and was worse)</Finding>. This inventory is also the
cross-embodiment story in miniature: the trunk and its video prior are body-agnostic; the adapters
(and the implicit inverse dynamics they feed) are the embodiment-specific part that few-shot
adaptation re-fits.

## Methodology — how we actually built it (authors' voice)

One 14B autoregressive diffusion transformer, initialized from Wan2.1-I2V-14B-480P. Cameras enter
through the frozen VAE, the instruction through the frozen text encoder, proprioception through a
small trained state encoder; the model emits future video latents and a 48-action chunk through
separate decoders. We corrupt video and action with a *shared* per-chunk noise level — "for faster
convergence at the beginning of training" (§3.1, p.7) — and train with teacher forcing: "the model
is trained to denoise the noisy current chunk conditioned on the clean previous chunks" (§3.1,
p.7). At inference we denoise a chunk, hand it to an asynchronous controller, and overwrite our own
prediction with the real camera frames before denoising the next. Making it real-time was a
reframing, not just kernels: asynchronous execution transforms the constraint from "inference must
complete before the robot moves" to "inference must complete before the current action chunk
expires" (§3.2.2, p.8) — that is the honest meaning of our ~7 Hz.

The data follows from the model. If the video prior is inherited, robot data exists to teach
inverse dynamics — so we collected for breadth, not polish: "Our data collection philosophy
prioritizes diversity over repetition" (App.E, p.24), operators run three coarse tasks per episode
across 22 real environments, and "Once a task was collected in 50 episodes, it is deprecated and
removed from the task sheet" (App.E, p.24) — a forced long tail. We also evaluated the hard way:
the eval site is in a different geographic location from collection, so "every benchmark inherently
tests out-of-distribution generalization rather than interpolation within the training
distribution" (§4.1, p.11).

**Data and protocol.** Pretraining is ~500 h of AgiBot G1 teleoperation: 7,193 episodes, mean 4.4 min, mean 42.4 subtasks per episode, 22 real environments (homes, restaurants, supermarkets, coffee shops, offices, warehouses, labs, hotels). Collection deliberately maximizes breadth: operators pick three coarse tasks per episode from a sheet, and any task reaching 50 episodes is *deprecated and removed*, forcing a long tail of new behaviors (App.E, p.24). A second model is trained on DROID for the Franka arm. Evaluation is always in environments and with objects unseen in training — the eval site is in a different geographic location from data collection (§4.1, p.11). "Seen task" = the motion and object type appear in training (folding a different shirt); "unseen task" = the required motion is absent (folding socks). AgiBot: 10 seen + 10 unseen tasks, 8 rollouts each across 4 robots, 80 rollouts per checkpoint per category. DROID: 20 seen + 20 unseen tasks, 2 rollouts each, 80 rollouts total; object positions fixed across checkpoints. Rollouts are scored 0–1 for partial completion (§4.1, p.12). Baselines: GR00T N1.6 and pi-0.5, each in a from-scratch variant (pretrained VLM weights, no prior robot data) and a from-pretrained variant (official checkpoints, thousands of hours of cross-embodiment robot data), both continued on identical data with matched total batch size and gradient steps.

## Key findings (authors' voice, numbers as reported)

- <Finding src="Fig.10, p.15">Generalization survives post-training</Finding>: after task-specific
  fine-tuning we average 90.5% vs 79.8% for pi-0.5 — still in unseen environments; the margin
  concentrates in fruit packing (96 vs 71), shirt folding ties.
- <Finding src="Tab.4, p.17">Diversity beats repetition at equal hours</Finding>: 500 h diverse
  50% ± 6.3 vs 500 h repetitive 33% ± 4.2. Our reading: "since video prediction is largely
  inherited from pretraining, the key challenge is learning inverse dynamics. A robust IDM
  requires diverse state-action correspondences across varied contexts, which repetitive data
  inherently lacks" (§5.3, p.17). (One ablation pair, one task category — curator.)
- <Finding src="Tab.4, p.17">Scale helps the WAM and does not rescue the VLA</Finding>: 14B 50% vs
  5B 21% — the small model is "prone to visual hallucinations that propagate to erroneous actions"
  (§5.2, p.17) — while 8B/32B-derived VLAs sit at 0% on the same diverse data.
- <Finding src="Tab.2, p.16">Action-free video from other bodies transfers</Finding>: 20 min of
  YAM video lifts unseen-task progress 38.3% ± 7.6 → 55.4% ± 9.5; 12 min of egocentric human video
  → 54.3% ± 10.4. "Our method relies solely on visual information without action labels" (§5.2,
  p.16); the dream is explicit — "abundant human video data—orders of magnitude larger than robot
  datasets—could enable WAMs to acquire diverse skills without action annotation" (§5.2, p.16).
  (+44.6% and +41.8% relative — so the abstract's "over 42%" is the robot-to-robot case; both
  gains are ~1.2–1.4 SE — curator.)
- <Finding src="Tab.3, p.17">One-step denoising is affordable only with the decoupled
  schedule</Finding>: 4 steps 83% ± 6.1 at 350 ms; naive 1-step 52% ± 10.2; Flash 1-step 74% ± 10.1
  at 150 ms. Why not just cut action steps? "Because video and action are jointly trained for
  strong cross-modal alignment, naively reducing action denoising steps degrades quality" (fn.3,
  p.8).
- <Finding src="§5.1, p.14; Fig.16, p.29">Failures live in the imagination, not the
  readout</Finding>: the arm faithfully tracks the generated video even when the video is wrong —
  it hands the marker over instead of drawing because the *plan* did. Policy quality reduces to
  video quality; an unusually falsifiable claim (supported here by two illustrated examples, not a
  measured attribution — curator).
- <Finding src="§5.1, p.14">Pretrained VLAs collect partial credit by defaulting to

## Implications (authors' voice; curator notes marked)

We read this as the strongest current argument that <Related>video world models (DreamGen, Genie
Envisioner, Cosmos Policy, mimic-video, Unified World Models) can be policies, not just data
generators or planners</Related>, against <Related>latent world models (V-JEPA 2, Dreamer) and 3D
point-cloud world models</Related>, which model forward dynamics and need a separate IDM or
test-time search — our 7 Hz exists precisely because there is no test-time optimization loop
(App.A, p.20). If "improving robotic capabilities reduces to improving video generation" (§2.2,
p.5) survives scrutiny, robot learning inherits the scaling story of video generation.
<FollowUp>Does action-free transfer scale from 12 in-lab minutes to Ego4D-scale corpora, or
saturate?</FollowUp> <FollowUp>Joint denoising is never ablated against the two-model
decomposition it claims to beat — that experiment would settle "alignment" (curator).</FollowUp>

*(Curator's note, unchanged from the analytical summary:)* for my own track the transfer is
conceptual: a WAM is a policy that must emit a *checkable artifact of its intended future*
alongside each action — the same shape as a harness demanding a predicted post-condition before a
command runs. <FollowUp>Whether next-state prediction as an auxiliary objective improves outcome
verification for computer-use agents, where the future state is a DOM or filesystem and far
cheaper than video.</FollowUp> The paper also demonstrates the failure mode: a policy that
faithfully executes its own wrong prediction is *more* confidently wrong, not less.

## Limitations
- <Caveat>The headline "over 2×" holds on one embodiment and one metric.</Caveat> It is AgiBot G1 average task progress (62.2/27.4 and 39.5/16.3). On DROID-Franka the same baselines give 1.2×–1.5× on task progress. The abstract does not scope this.
- <Caveat>Task progress is partial credit with an unpublished rubric and unnamed judges.</Caveat> §4.1 (p.12) says rollouts are scored 0–1.0 "based on partial task completion; full details are provided in Appendix G", but App.G (p.25 and p.28) contains only task lists and instructions — no rubric. Who scores rollouts, whether scoring is blind to the method, and whether the same person scores all checkpoints are all unreported. For an eval where the authors themselves note baselines earn partial credit by reaching-and-grasping, the rubric is load-bearing.
- <Caveat>Small trial counts and unreported variance in the main figures.</Caveat> 8 rollouts per AgiBot task, 2 per DROID task, 10 per post-training task, one checkpoint each. Standard errors appear only in Tab.2–4; the figure captions do not define their error bars, and no seeds or significance tests are reported anywhere.
- <Caveat>The cross-embodiment gains are within noise as reported.</Caveat> 38.3 ± 7.6 → 55.4 ± 9.5 is a 17.1-point gain against a ~12.2-point standard error of the difference (about 1.4 SE); the human-transfer gain is about 1.2 SE. The direction is consistent across both settings, which is the real signal, but "over 42%" is a ratio of two noisy numbers on 72 rollouts.
- <Caveat>The few-shot embodiment-adaptation result has no numbers at all.</Caveat> Q5 (p.16) post-trains on 55 YAM trajectories across 11 tasks (~30 min) and reports only that the policy "retains strong language following" and generalizes to novel objects, illustrated in Fig.12. No success rate, no baseline, no trial count, no comparison against training YAM from scratch. "Play data" is never defined; from the description it is 55 short task-labeled trajectories with 11 global language annotations (fn.12, p.16), which is not free-form play.
- <Caveat>Compute parity between DreamZero and the VLA baselines is asserted, not shown.</Caveat> "Comparable compute budget" means matched total batch size and gradient steps (§4, p.10) — but DreamZero is 14B against ~3B-class baselines, so FLOPs are not matched, and per-baseline hyperparameter tuning is not reported. The total collapse of from-scratch VLAs (0.0–0.6%) is a strong result that would be more convincing with a learning-curve or tuning-effort disclosure.
- <Caveat>"Zero-shot" is scoped to action-labeled robot data only.</Caveat> The unseen tasks (ironing, handshakes, untying laces, painting) are absent from the robot data but are ordinary web-video content, and the 14B Wan2.1 backbone was trained on web-scale video. That is the paper's mechanism, not a flaw — but no audit of the video pretraining corpus against the eval tasks is reported, so "unseen" cannot be read as "novel to the model".
- <Caveat>"38× without degrading performance" is contradicted by the paper's own table.</Caveat> §1 (p.3) says the optimizations give 38× "without degrading performance"; §3.2.6 (p.10) correctly excludes DiT caching and quantization from the mathematically-equivalent set, and Tab.3 shows the Flash step that completes the 38× costs 9 points on table bussing (83% → 74%). Tab.3's text also calls the metric "success rate" while the table header says task progress.
- <Caveat>Author-stated limits.</Caveat> In their own words: "Higher-DOF robots will require more
  play data to learn an accurate implicit IDM, as the mapping from visual futures to motor commands
  grows combinatorially with kinematic complexity. Quantifying the accuracy of implicit IDMs remains
  a challenge" (§6, p.19). Also: no scaling laws for WAMs; human-video experiments are in-lab and tiny (12 min); 7Hz needs two GB200s where VLAs exceed 20Hz on consumer GPUs; visual memory is ~6 seconds so this is a System-1 model with no long-horizon planner; sub-centimeter precision tasks are expected to be weak; single-embodiment pretraining only (no multi-embodiment training); memory-dependent tasks are not evaluated.
- <Caveat>Claimed but unquantified side results.</Caveat> "100+ free-form tasks", non-trivial Genie Sim 3.0 performance (fn.1, p.4) and RoboArena integration are mentioned with no numbers; the AgiBot dataset is promised but not yet released.

## Critiques and Questions
1. **The central architectural claim is unablated.** Eq.1 says the joint model equals video prediction times an IDM; §3.1 asserts that training one model end-to-end beats instantiating the two factors separately, citing better "alignment". No experiment compares them. Given that the paper's own diagnosis is "failures come from video prediction", a two-model baseline sharing the same video backbone would isolate whether joint denoising buys anything beyond convenience.
2. **The metric may be doing some of the work in the 2× headline.** Partial-credit task progress rewards approach-and-grasp behavior, which the authors say is exactly the VLA failure mode — so the gap could be *larger* on binary success, or the absolute numbers could be much lower for everyone. AgiBot binary success rates would settle this in one column and are not reported; the DROID panel, where both metrics do appear, shows the ratio moving (1.5× on task progress vs 1.8× on success rate for unseen tasks).
3. **Diversity-vs-repetition rests on a single 33-vs-50 pair, and "diversity" is not an isolated axis.** Coverage (how many distinct task-context-placement combinations exist) and redundancy (how many near-duplicates sit inside each) are logically independent — a corpus can be broad *and* repetitive — but holding the budget fixed at 500 h mechanically couples them, so the two arms differ in task count, repeats per task, within-task placement variation and episode structure simultaneously. No 2x2 and no intermediate points are reported, so the ablation locates two points on a coverage-versus-redundancy trade-off rather than isolating either. Both arms also draw the same number of samples (50K steps, batch 32), which makes the operative quantity *distinct state-action correspondences per sample drawn*, not dataset size. The repetitive set is described only as "70 tasks with many repeated demonstrations"; overlap between that set and the PnP-Easy eval tasks is unstated.
4. **Bidirectional vs autoregressive is argued more strongly than measured.** Tab.4 shows a tie (50% both), with the AR advantage claimed as smoother motion and 3–4× faster inference; smoothness is not quantified anywhere.
5. **Is the closed-loop KV-cache overwrite doing the heavy lifting?** Replacing predicted frames with real observations every 1.6 s means the model never accumulates video error over more than one chunk. That is a strong crutch and possibly the real reason a video model can act at all — but there is no ablation running open-loop video context to size the effect.

<SelfCheck q="If DreamZero's action decoder is trained jointly with video, why does the paper call the action branch an inverse-dynamics model, and what does that predict about its failure modes?" a="Eq.1 (p.6) factorizes the joint distribution into video prediction times p(actions | observations including the predicted future). The action branch is therefore reading motor commands off a visual future rather than mapping the current observation to an action. The prediction is that errors should be inherited from the video plan, not generated by the action head — which is what §5.1 (p.14) and Fig.16 (p.29) report: the robot faithfully executes wrong plans, e.g. picking up the bread instead of opening the oven, because the generated video did the same." />

<SelfCheck q="In what sense is DreamZero a 'zero-shot policy', and in what sense is it not?" a="Operationally: the pretrained checkpoint is evaluated out of the box, with no task- or environment-specific fine-tuning, in environments and with objects never seen in the robot data, and for 10 tasks whose required motions are absent from the robot data (§4.1, p.11). It is not zero-shot with respect to the backbone: Wan2.1-I2V-14B was pretrained on web-scale video that plausibly contains ironing, handshakes and shoelaces, and the paper's whole thesis is that this prior is what makes the motions possible. No contamination audit of the video corpus is reported." />

## Glossary
- <Term def="World Action Model: a policy that predicts future world state (here, video) and actions in one aligned model; 'world' rather than 'video' because the predictive modality could later be tactile or force">WAM</Term> — the paper's own coinage for this class, covering Cosmos Policy, Genie Envisioner, mimic-video and this work.
- <Term def="forward dynamics predicts the next state from a state and an action; inverse dynamics does the reverse, inferring the action that carries the robot between two states — here, between the current observation and the model's own predicted future">inverse dynamics</Term> — what robot action labels still have to teach once video prediction is inherited from pretraining; in DreamZero the IDM is *implicit* (no separate network), and the paper concedes its accuracy cannot be measured (p.19).
- <Term def="the conventional recipe: many demonstrations of the same task with similar object placements, collected in controlled settings, so that action labels alone pin down a consistent behaviour">repeated (repetitive) demonstrations</Term> — what this 500 h corpus is deliberately *not*: each task is deprecated after 50 episodes, forcing operators to propose new ones (App.E.1, p.24). Aimed at prior WAMs as well as VLAs — "most works still focus on repeated demonstrations" (§1, p.3).
- <Term def="in this paper zero-shot always ranges over tasks and environments — no task-specific action data, no fine-tuning for the site — and never over embodiments">zero-shot</Term> vs <Term def="the embodiment axis: post-training the pretrained checkpoint on about 30 minutes of a new robot's data before it can control that robot">few-shot embodiment adaptation</Term> — two different axes. "Retaining zero-shot generalization" after the YAM post-training (§1, p.3) means the *task-level* generalization survives the *embodiment-level* adaptation; the title's claim is about tasks, never about bodies.
- <Term def="average partial completion per rollout, scored 0 to 1; e.g. folding stages out of 5, fruits packed out of 10">task progress</Term> — the primary metric; distinct from binary success rate, which is reported only for DROID.
- <Term def="mobile bimanual manipulator with parallel grippers; the paper's main embodiment, ~500 h of in-house teleoperation data">AgiBot G1</Term>, <Term def="a second bimanual parallel-gripper robot used for cross-embodiment transfer and few-shot adaptation">YAM</Term>, <Term def="large open in-the-wild Franka single-arm dataset (Khazatsky et al. 2024) used as the reproducible setting">DROID</Term>.
- <Term def="generative training objective that regresses the velocity field of a linear interpolation between noise and data; used here jointly over video and action latents">flow matching</Term>, <Term def="training the model to denoise the current chunk conditioned on clean ground-truth previous chunks">teacher forcing</Term>, <Term def="diffusion transformer — the denoising backbone">DiT</Term>, <Term def="classifier-free guidance: conditional and unconditional forward passes combined at inference; parallelized here across 2 GPUs">CFG</Term>.
- <Term def="the 30-minute YAM corpus: 55 trajectories over 11 tasks with one short language label per task; the paper never defines the term formally">play data</Term>.
- <Term def="Wan2.1-I2V-14B-480P, a 14B image-to-video diffusion model used as the frozen-VAE, trainable-DiT backbone">Wan2.1</Term>; baselines <Term def="NVIDIA VLA (Bjorck et al. 2025)">GR00T N1.6</Term> and <Term def="Physical Intelligence VLA with open-world generalization (2025)">pi-0.5</Term>.
