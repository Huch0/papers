Cast every NLP task as text-in → text-out so a single encoder-decoder Transformer with one loss and one decoding rule covers translation, classification, regression, and summarization — then use that fixed harness to ablate the entire transfer-learning design space (objective, architecture, corpus, adaptation, scale) and push the winning recipe to 11B parameters on the new ~750 GB C4 corpus, reaching state of the art on 18 of 24 tasks.

T5 standardized the “everything is text-to-text” interface that prompting-era LLMs inherit, released C4 (the ancestor of most open web-scale pre-training corpora), and framed the “you have 4× compute — where do you spend it?” question that scaling-laws work then formalized. A cornerstone of the LLM milestone path.

Motivation

By late 2019, transfer learning owned NLP: pre-train on unlabeled web text, fine-tune per task (the ELMo/GPT/BERT/XLNet/RoBERTa lineage). But the field was advancing by bundled changes: each new method altered the pre-training objective, architecture, corpus, compute budget, and fine-tuning scheme simultaneously, so published comparisons could not attribute a gain to any single ingredient (§1). And the data axis was nearly unstudiable: pre-training corpora were often unreleased, differently scoped (news-only, Wikipedia-only), and rarely compared head-to-head — there was no standard pre-training data set at all (§3.4). If I want to know what actually matters, I have to hold everything else fixed — and the existing zoo of task-specific heads and losses makes that impossible.

Contribution (what’s new)

The enabling insight is the interface, not a new method (the paper is explicit that it proposes none, §1). Casting every task — generative, classification, even regression — as “feed text in, train the model to emit the answer as text” means one model, one maximum-likelihood loss, and one decoding procedure serve every task, so any factor (objective, architecture, corpus, adaptation strategy) can be swapped in isolation while the rest of the pipeline stays byte-identical. The form is forced: with per-task heads, an architecture swap requires re-engineering every task, and objective comparisons leak through task-specific plumbing; prior unifications (QA-format, span extraction) cannot express generative tasks. On top of this harness sit two more deltas: C4, a ~750 GB heuristically filtered Common Crawl corpus, released publicly so the data axis becomes an experimental variable (§2.2), and the empirical map itself — a coordinate-ascent sweep over the design space whose combined insights, plus scale, become the T5 recipe (§3).

Research questions

  1. Holding a fixed text-to-text pipeline, which transfer-learning ingredients — unsupervised objective, architecture, pre-training corpus, fine-tuning/multi-task strategy — actually move downstream performance? (§3)
  2. Given 4× more compute, is it better spent on a bigger model, longer training, or ensembling? (§3.6)
  3. How far do the combined insights go when scaled to billions of parameters and ~1T tokens? (§3.7)

Methodology

Start from a deliberately typical baseline: a BERT-Base-sized encoder-decoder Transformer (~220M parameters), pre-trained on C4 for 524,288 steps (~34B tokens) with a span-replacing denoising objective, then fine-tuned separately per task (§3.1). Every task is serialized with a text prefix. The paper’s own running example: “translate English to German: That is good.” trains the model to emit “Das ist gut.”; even the STS-B similarity regression becomes text by rounding scores to 0.2 increments and predicting the string “3.8” (§2.4). From this baseline, change exactly one axis at a time — architectures, objectives, corpora, adaptation strategies, scaling strategies — measuring GLUE, SuperGLUE, SQuAD, CNN/Daily Mail, and three WMT translation pairs each time. Statistical grounding comes from 10 reruns of the baseline, whose variance is assumed to carry over to all variants (§3.1.5).

Method details: baseline, objective, and the compared variants

Baseline model and training (§3.1). Encoder and decoder each have 12 blocks, dmodel=768d_{\text{model}}=768dmodel​=768, dff=3072d_{\text{ff}}=3072dff​=3072, 12 heads; simplified LayerNorm (no bias, outside the residual path) and relative position embeddings shared across layers. AdaFactor optimizer; inverse-square-root LR schedule for pre-training, constant 0.001 for fine-tuning (262,144 steps); greedy decoding; SentencePiece vocabulary of 32,000 wordpieces trained on English C4 plus German/French/Romanian pages. Pre-training covers ~34B tokens — a quarter of BERT’s budget — and never repeats data.

The baseline objective, on the paper’s running sentence (Fig.2). From “Thank you for inviting me to your party last week .” randomly drop 15% of tokens; consecutive dropped tokens collapse into one sentinel. Input becomes “Thank you <X> me to your party <Y> week .” and the target is only the dropped material: “<X> for inviting <Y> last <Z>”. Predicting just the missing spans (not the full sentence) keeps targets short, which is what makes pre-training cheap.

Architecture grid (§3.2). Encoder-decoder (2P params, cost M — the P-parameter single stack is applied to both input and output, so an L+L encoder-decoder matches an L-layer decoder-only model in FLOPs); shared-parameter encoder-decoder (P, M); half-depth encoder-decoder (P, M/2); decoder-only language model (P, M); decoder-only prefix LM (P, M) which applies fully-visible attention over the input portion — the closest text-to-text analog of BERT.

Objective taxonomy (§3.3, Tab.3). High-level: prefix LM vs BERT-style masked reconstruction vs deshuffling. Then simplifications of the winner: MASS-style masking, replace-spans-with-sentinels (baseline), drop-tokens-entirely; corruption rates 10/15/25/50%; explicit span corruption with mean span lengths 2/3/5/10.

Adaptation and multi-task (§3.5). Adapter layers (inner dim d from 32 to 2048) and gradual unfreezing vs full fine-tuning; multi-task mixing by examples-proportional sampling with dataset-size cap K, temperature-scaled mixing, or equal mixing; and multi-task pre-training followed by per-task fine-tuning (MT-DNN-style), including a leave-one-out variant.

The final T5 recipe (§3.7): span corruption (mean length 3, 15%), 1M steps at batch 2048×512 ≈ 1 trillion tokens on C4, multi-task pre-training, per-task GLUE/SuperGLUE fine-tuning with small batches, beam search (width 4, length penalty 0.6). Sizes: Small 60M, Base 220M, Large 770M, 3B, and 11B (24 layers, dff=65,536d_{\text{ff}}=65{,}536dff​=65,536, 128 heads — widened because dense matmuls are what TPUs do efficiently).

Main result

Scaling the study’s combined insights delivers state of the art on 18 of 24 tasks: GLUE 90.3 (prev 89.4), SuperGLUE 88.9 — within 0.9 of the 89.8 human baseline on a benchmark designed to be hard for transfer learning — SQuAD EM 91.26, and CNN/DM ROUGE-2 21.55 (Tab.14, §3.7). Translation is the telling failure: English-only pre-training never beats back-translation-based SOTA on any WMT pair (e.g. EnDe 32.1 vs 33.8) (Tab.14).

Crucially, scale is not the whole story: pre-training the plain baseline on the same 1T tokens (“baseline-1T”) reaches GLUE 84.80, while T5-Base — identical compute, plus the study’s design changes — reaches 85.97 (SQuAD 85.44 vs 83.01) (Tab.15), isolating the value of the non-scaling insights.

Key findings

  • At matched FLOPs, the encoder-decoder with a denoising objective beats a decoder-only LM by huge margins (GLUE 83.28 vs 74.70; SQuAD 80.88 vs 61.14) (Tab.2): sharing encoder/decoder parameters costs almost nothing (82.81 GLUE at half the parameters), while halving depth clearly hurts — so the win is partly “2× parameters at equal FLOPs”. The prefix LM closes most of the gap (81.82 GLUE).
  • Denoising decisively beats language-modeling and deshuffling pre-training (GLUE 82.96 vs 80.69 vs 73.17), but among denoising variants — masking style, corruption rate 10–25%, span length — differences are marginal (Tab.4, §3.3.5): the paper’s advice is to choose the variant with the shortest targets (span corruption, mean length 3) purely for training speed.
  • Data quality and domain matter: unfiltered C4 (6.1 TB) is the worst corpus on every task despite being 8× larger, and in-domain pre-training gives big targeted gains — Wikipedia+TBC lifts MultiRC EM from 25.78 to 50.93 (fiction domain), RealNews lifts ReCoRD from 68.16 to 73.72 (Tab.8).
  • Repeating pre-training data degrades performance — 64 repeats is benign, but 1,024 repeats drops GLUE 83.28 → 79.55 — and the accompanying fall in training loss suggests memorization (Tab.9, Fig.6): an early data-constrained-scaling result.
  • Full fine-tuning beats parameter-efficient alternatives (adapters need d matched to task size; d=32 collapses translation to 13.84 BLEU), and pure multi-task training underperforms pre-train-then-fine-tune — but multi-task pre-training + per-task fine-tuning matches the baseline (83.11 vs 83.28 GLUE), and leave-one-out multi-task pre-training barely hurts (Tab.10–12): task interference is mild once you fine-tune.
  • Given 4× compute, model size beats training length: 2× size + 2× steps reaches GLUE 86.18 vs 85.33 for 4× steps alone; ensembling 4 models is a strong orthogonal use of the same compute (best CNN/DM and WMT EnDe) (Tab.13).

Implications

The text-to-text interface became the field’s default abstraction: task prefixes are proto-prompts, and the instruction-tuning line (FLAN-T5 and successors) fine-tunes exactly this interface on thousands of tasks. C4 seeded the open pre-training corpus lineage, and the repeat-degradation and data-filtering results anticipate data-centric scaling work. The scaling section’s question — where to spend marginal compute — is answered qualitatively here (“bigger model”) and quantitatively by Kaplan et al.’s scaling laws, published three months later. Worth chasing: why the field converged on decoder-only models anyway despite Tab.2 (zero-shot generation, KV-cache inference, and scaling behavior differ from this fine-tuning-centric comparison), and the paper’s own outlook item that span denoising is an inefficient knowledge-extraction mechanism — the thread ELECTRA and later objective work pull on (§4.2).

Limitations

  • Coordinate ascent: each axis is varied around one baseline, so interaction effects (e.g. an objective that only wins at larger scale) are invisible by design — author-stated (§3).
  • All ablations run at Base scale (220M params, ~34B tokens); conclusions like “encoder-decoder wins” are extrapolated to 11B without re-verification (§3, inferred).
  • Each variant is a single run; significance rests on the assumption that 10 baseline reruns’ variance transfers, and high-variance small tasks (CoLA, CB, COPA) make GLUE/SGLUE averages noisy (§3.1.5).
  • English-only pre-training with a fixed 4-language vocabulary: no WMT SOTA, and the model cannot process unanticipated languages at all (§3.7, §4.2).
  • MultiRC and ReCoRD exceed human performance, which the authors read as metric bias toward machine predictions; SQuAD is reported on validation because the test server could not run the 11B model (§3.7).

Critiques and Questions

  1. The architecture comparison is fair-by-construction but ambiguous in interpretation: at matched FLOPs the encoder-decoder holds 2× the parameters, and at matched parameters (shared variant) it still sees each token through both stacks. “Encoder-decoder is better” and “parameters-per-FLOP is better spent with an encoder” are not distinguished.
  2. Casting the LM architecture into denoising via concatenation (§3.2.3) plays away from its home game; the decisive Tab.2 gap partly measures objective-architecture mismatch, not architecture alone.
  3. The text-to-text claim “we never observed illegal label outputs” holds for fine-tuned models but is a fragile property — it silently depends on fine-tuning; zero-shot use of the same interface (GPT-2 style) is explicitly out of scope, which limits what the comparison says about the prompting era that followed.
  4. C4’s filters (drop any page with a curly brace, bad-word list, terminal-punctuation lines) are pragmatic but consequential — they excise code and legitimate content wholesale, shaping (and biasing) everything trained on it; the paper does not audit what the filters removed.
  5. SOTA at 11B leans on per-task fine-tuning, task-specific checkpoint selection, and beam search; the headline “limits of transfer learning” is really “limits of this fine-tuning recipe under generous adaptation budget”.
