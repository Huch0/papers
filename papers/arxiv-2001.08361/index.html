Curated byChiyeong Heo

Language-model loss falls as a smooth power law in model size, data, and compute — over up to seven orders of magnitude, nearly independent of architecture shape — and the fitted laws combine into a predictive framework whose punchline is that a fixed compute budget is best spent on very large models trained on modest data, stopped far short of convergence.

This is the paper that made “just scale it” a quantitative engineering discipline: it justified GPT-3’s design, introduced the loss-prediction machinery every frontier lab now runs before training, and set up the compute-allocation question that Chinchilla later revised. The essential companion to T5 and GPT-3 on the LLM milestone path.

Motivation

By early 2020 it was clear that bigger language models trained on more data performed better — but only qualitatively. Nobody could say how much better: there was no quantitative relationship between loss and model size, data size, or compute, so training decisions (how big a model, how much data, when to stop) were folklore rather than calculation (§1). And with each ingredient entangled with the others — a bigger model, trained longer, on more data — the field could not tell whether performance was limited by capacity, data, or optimization, nor whether architectural tinkering (depth vs width, heads) mattered at all compared to raw scale. Language modeling is the ideal testbed: cross-entropy gives a smooth, unbounded metric with a high ceiling and low floor, so trends can be measured across many orders of magnitude.

Contribution (what’s new)

The empirical laws themselves: test loss is a precise power law in each of non-embedding parameters N, dataset tokens D, and optimally-allocated compute, and single two-variable equations — L(N,D)L(N,D)L(N,D) and L(N,S)L(N,S)L(N,S) — govern their joint behavior, including overfitting and the whole shape of training curves (§1.2, §4–5). The form is forced by three constraints: tokenization can only rescale the loss (so constants like NcN_cNc​ carry no fundamental meaning), each variable’s law must be recovered when the other goes to infinity, and overfitting should admit a 1/D1/D1/D expansion (§4.1). From the fitted laws follows the second delta: a derived, then empirically confirmed, compute-allocation rule — spend budget overwhelmingly on model size, grow data sublinearly, and stop training at roughly 10% above the converged loss (§6, App.B). A methodological choice makes all of it work: counting only non-embedding parameters, which collapses models of wildly different depths onto one clean trend (Fig.6).

Research questions

  1. How does language-modeling loss depend, quantitatively, on model size, dataset size, compute, and architecture shape — and over how many orders of magnitude do the dependencies hold? (§3)
  2. How do N and D trade off — how fast must data grow with model size to keep overfitting controlled? (§4)
  3. Given a fixed compute budget and freedom over everything else, what allocation of model size, batch size, and training steps minimizes loss? (§5–6)

Methodology

Train a large family of decoder-only Transformers (768 to 1.5B non-embedding parameters; shapes from 2 layers × 128 wide to 207 × 768) on WebText2 (22.9B BPE tokens, vocab 50,257, context 1024), varying exactly one bottleneck at a time: model size on unlimited data, dataset subsets (22M–23B tokens) with early stopping, fixed compute with a scan over model sizes (§2–3). Compute is estimated as C≈6NBSC \approx 6NBSC≈6NBS FLOPs (forward + backward, embeddings and context-dependent terms excluded). Two corrections make the trends clean: measure N without embedding parameters, and convert step counts to Smin⁡S_{\min}Smin​ — the steps that would be needed at very large batch — using the critical-batch-size theory of gradient noise, since most runs used one fixed batch size (§5.1). Then fit the two master equations and derive the compute frontier from them, checking the derivation against the empirical optimum (§6).

Main result

Three clean single-variable laws (Fig.1, Tab.5): L(N) = (8.8×10^13 / N)^0.076 for models trained to convergence on ample data; L(D) = (5.4×10^13 / D)^0.095 for large models on limited data with early stopping; L(C_min) = (3.1×10^8 / C_min)^0.050 for optimally allocated compute — trends holding over six to eight orders of magnitude with no visible deviation at the top (Eq.1.1–1.3, Tab.5). Combined, they dictate the allocation answer: as compute grows 10×, the optimal model grows ~5× (N ∝ C^0.73) while data processed grows only ~2× (D ∝ C^0.27) — almost all of it absorbed by larger batches (B ∝ C^0.24), with serial steps nearly constant (S ∝ C^0.03) (Fig.3, Fig.14, §6.1). Big models trained briefly, not small models trained long.

Key findings

  • Shape barely matters: at fixed N, varying depth/width aspect ratio by 40× moves loss only a few percent (a 6-layer, 4288-wide model lands within 3% of GPT-2’s 48×1600), and depth curves collapse onto one trend once embedding parameters are excluded (Fig.5–6, §3.1) — scale, not architecture tuning, is the lever.
  • Overfitting is governed by a single ratio: the penalty depends on N^0.74/D, so data may grow sublinearly — 8× the model needs only ~5× the data, and D ≳ 5000·N^0.74 suffices to overfit by less than run-to-run variance (Eq.4.4, Fig.9).
  • Larger models are drastically more sample-efficient, reaching a fixed loss with up to ~100× fewer examples and far fewer serial steps than the smallest capable model (Fig.2, Fig.19) — the mechanism behind “train big, stop early”.
  • Transfer to other text distributions (books, Wikipedia, Common Crawl) costs only a roughly constant loss offset: out-of-distribution performance is a function of in-distribution validation loss alone, independent of depth or training phase (Fig.8, App.D.8) — validation loss is a sufficient statistic for generalization.
  • LSTMs match Transformers on early context tokens but plateau after ~100 tokens, while Transformer per-token loss keeps improving as a power law in context position — larger models also learn patterns from less context (Fig.7, App.D.5).
  • Extrapolated, the laws self-contradict: compute-optimal data grows as C^0.26 but overfitting control demands C^0.54, forcing an intersection at roughly C* ~ 10^4 PF-days, N* ~ 10^12 parameters, D* ~ 10^12 tokens, L* ~ 1.7 nats/token — either where the laws break down, or (conjecturally) where models exhaust the reliable information in language (§6.3, Fig.15); the point is uncertain by an order of magnitude in either direction.

Implications

The paper converts scaling from an article of faith (the “bitter lesson” reading of T5’s scaling section and its “given 4× compute” question) into arithmetic — and its prescription was executed months later as GPT-3, a very large model trained on relatively few tokens, stopped far short of convergence. Its loss-prediction methodology became standard pre-training practice. The allocation exponents, however, did not survive: Hoffmann et al. 2022 (Chinchilla) redid the analysis with learning-rate schedules matched to each budget and found N and D should scale roughly equally (about 20 tokens per parameter), meaning GPT-3-era models were substantially undertrained on data — the qualitative framework held, the 0.73/0.27 split did not. Threads worth pulling: whether smooth loss improvements hide discontinuous capability changes — the paper’s own “more is different” warning, which becomes the emergent-abilities debate, the conjectured universality of these laws across modalities (tested by the same group’s follow-up on autoregressive generative modeling), and a theory that derives the exponents — the “statistical mechanics beneath the thermodynamics” the discussion explicitly asks for (§8).

Limitations

  • No theoretical understanding of any of the laws — the authors state the scalings with model size and compute are “especially mysterious”, and without a theory of corrections it is unclear when the laws can be trusted (App.C).
  • Everything is cross-entropy on one dataset with one tokenizer: WebText2, BPE-50257, context 1024. The scale constants are explicitly tokenization-dependent, and no downstream task is evaluated (§1.2, §2.3).
  • The fitted exponents come from models up to only 1.5B parameters and ~10^0 PF-days; the headline allocations are extrapolations across many untested orders of magnitude, and the L(N,D) fit already fails for the smallest data subsets (40 steps per epoch) (§4.2, App.C).
  • Bcrit(L) is trusted far outside the measured loss range, and the compute estimate C ≈ 6N drops context-dependent terms, confounding the laws when context length rivals 12·d_model (App.C).
  • Regularization was fixed (10% dropout, early stopping) rather than tuned per (N, D), and hyperparameters beyond learning rate were not swept — either could shift the overfitting law (§4.2, App.C).

Critiques and Questions

  1. The most consequential number — N ∝ C^0.73 — was later shown to be an artifact: with cosine schedules tuned to each compute budget (rather than a fixed 250k-step schedule) and larger-scale fits, Chinchilla obtained ~C^0.5 for both N and D. The paper’s own caveat about untuned optimization hyperparameters (App.C) turned out to be exactly where the error lived. Lesson for reading any scaling paper: exponents inherit every fixed hyperparameter of the sweep.
  2. “Performance depends weakly on shape” is measured within the vanilla Transformer family at fixed N ≤ 1.5B; it licensed years of architectural conservatism, but the claim never covered attention variants, sparsity, or scale-dependent shape effects.
  3. Excluding embedding parameters is what makes the laws clean (Fig.6) — but it is a post-hoc variable choice justified by its own cleanliness; with today’s vocabularies and tied embeddings the accounting convention materially changes reported exponents at small scale.
  4. The L* ≈ 1.7 nats “entropy of natural language” reading of the contradiction point is intriguing but unfalsifiable as stated: the intersection moves an order of magnitude with small exponent perturbations, and the two extrapolated laws need only one to bend.
  5. Loss is treated as the objective; the paper flags but does not test whether equal loss from different (N, D) mixes yields equal capabilities — later work on data-constrained scaling and memorization shows it does not always.
You are given a 10× larger compute budget. According to this paper, how do you spend it, and when do you stop training?
Grow the model ~5× (N ∝ C^0.73), process only ~2× more data (D ∝ C^0.27), and take almost no additional serial steps (S ∝ C^0.03) — the extra data throughput comes from larger batches near the critical batch size. Stop when the loss is still ~10% above what the model would reach at convergence: training to near-convergence is compute-inefficient, costing ~3× the compute of the optimal early stop (App.B.2–B.3).
