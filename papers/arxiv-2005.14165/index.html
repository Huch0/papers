Scaling an essentially unchanged autoregressive Transformer to 175B parameters (GPT-3) turns prompting-with-examples — in-context learning — into a practical substitute for fine-tuning: few-shot performance rises with scale faster than zero-shot does, and on some benchmarks matches or beats fine-tuned state of the art.

This is the founding document of the prompting era: it defines the zero/one/few-shot evaluation protocol, demonstrates that capability scaling is smooth and roughly predictable, and introduces the train–test contamination audit that every later web-scale LLM paper inherits. Core reading for the LLM milestone path — FLAN, InstructGPT, and the scaling race all respond directly to it.

Motivation

By 2020 the dominant recipe was pretrain-then-fine-tune. It works, but every new task still demands a labeled dataset of thousands to hundreds of thousands of examples — impractical for the long tail of useful language tasks (§1). Worse, fine-tuning a very expressive model on a narrow task distribution invites exploiting spurious correlations, so nominally human-level benchmark scores can exaggerate real task competence (§1, citing poor out-of-distribution generalization). Humans need neither: an instruction plus a demonstration or two suffices. GPT-2 had hinted that a language model could pick up tasks from its prompt alone, but its in-context results were far below fine-tuning — e.g. 4% on Natural Questions (§1), leaving in-context learning a curiosity rather than a method.

Contribution (what’s new)

Nothing about the architecture is new — that is the point. The insight is that in-context learning is itself a capability that scales: pretraining acts as an outer loop that teaches the model to recognize and adapt to tasks inside a single forward pass (the inner loop), and this inner-loop ability improves with parameter count faster than raw zero-shot performance does (Fig.1.1–1.3). The design is forced by the hypothesis: to test whether meta-learning emerges from scale you must hold the recipe fixed and vary only size, so the paper trains 8 models from 125M to 175B on the same 300B tokens and evaluates every task under a uniform zero-/one-/few-shot protocol where the only variable is how many demonstrations fit in the 2048-token context (§2, Tab.2.1). A second lasting contribution: a systematic benchmark-contamination measurement (conservative 13-gram overlap, clean-subset re-evaluation) for web-scale training data (§4).

Research questions

  1. Does in-context learning improve smoothly with model scale — and enough to rival fine-tuned systems? (§1, §3)
  2. Where does prompting work and where does it fail across ~40 NLP benchmarks and synthetic probes? (§3)
  3. Are the gains explained by test-set memorization from Common Crawl? (§4)
  4. What breaks socially when generated text becomes indistinguishable from human text? (§3.9.4, §6)

Methodology

Take GPT-2’s architecture (with alternating dense and locally-banded sparse attention), scale it across 8 sizes, pretrain each on the same quality-weighted 300B-token mix dominated by filtered Common Crawl, then never touch the weights again. Every benchmark is rendered as a text prompt. The mechanics are easiest to see on LAMBADA: a plain LM assigns probability to many valid continuations, but a few-shot prompt of the form “Alice was friends with Bob. Alice went to visit her friend ___. → Bob” teaches the model, inside the context window, that exactly one word is wanted — reframing the task without changing a single weight (§3.1.2). K demonstrations (typically 10–100, chosen to fit 2048 tokens) are drawn randomly from the task’s training set; multiple-choice answers are scored by comparing per-token likelihoods, free-form answers by beam search (§2.4).

Main result

Across ~40 benchmarks, few-shot GPT-3 with frozen weights is sometimes competitive with — and occasionally better than — fine-tuned state of the art, while zero-shot lags and the zero→few gap widens with model size (Fig.1.3, §1). The spread across task families is the real story:

Key findings

  • Larger models are better in-context learners, not just better models (Fig.1.3, Fig.3.8, §1 p.6): the gap between zero-, one-, and few-shot widens with capacity across the 42-benchmark aggregate; on SuperGLUE, fewer than 8 examples per task suffice for GPT-3 to pass a fine-tuned BERT-Large (§3.7). Few-shot LAMBADA framing helps GPT-3 by 10 points while hurting the smallest model by ~20 — small models cannot exploit demonstrations (§3.1.2).
  • The synthetic probes show genuine test-time computation, not retrieval (Tab.3.9–3.10, §3.9.1–3.9.2): 175B does 2-digit addition at 100% and 3-digit subtraction at 94.2%, yet only 17 of 2,000 test additions (0.8%) appear in the training corpus, and errors look like forgotten carries; word unscrambling is near-impossible zero-shot (random insertion 8.26%) but reaches 67.2% few-shot, despite requiring the model to see inside its own BPE tokens (~0.7 words/token).
  • A consistent failure family: comparing two text snippets (Tab.3.8, Fig.3.9, §3.8, §5) — WiC at chance (49.4%), ANLI near chance for all models below 175B (which “closes about half the gap” only on R3), RTE/CB weak. The authors’ own diagnosis: unidirectional autoregression may be structurally bad at look-back-and-compare tasks that reward bidirectionality.
  • The contamination audit mostly exonerates the headline results but sets a precedent (§4, Fig.4.2): with a conservative 13-gram overlap flagging a quarter of benchmarks at over 50% “dirty”, clean-subset scores shift negligibly and uncorrelated with contamination level; PIQA (−3 pts on clean) and Winograd (132 schemas literally in the training set, −2.6%) get permanent asterisks, and Wikipedia LM benchmarks, 1BW, and the Children’s Book Test are dropped entirely. A filtering bug made prevention fail, so measurement had to substitute — honestly disclosed (§2.2, App.C).
  • Humans cannot detect GPT-3 news articles: 52% accuracy vs 50% chance (Tab.3.11–3.12, Fig.3.13), for both ~200- and ~500-word articles (control model: 86–88%), with detection accuracy falling with model size as a power law even though participants spend more time per article (Fig.E.1).
  • Internet-trained models have internet-scale biases (§6.2): 83% of 388 occupations complete male-leaning; female-associated descriptors skew to appearance (“beautiful”, 158 co-occurrences); “Black” ranks lowest in sentiment in 5 of 7 models; “violent”/“terrorism” sit in Islam’s top-40 co-occurring words — measured by prompted generation plus co-occurrence statistics, a preliminary but influential protocol.
  • Even 175B barely overfits 300B deduplicated tokens (Fig.4.1): the train–validation gap grows only minimally with size and time, which is why contamination, though frequent, matters less than feared.

Implications

The paper reframes “one model per task” into “one model, many prompts”: task specification becomes an interface question, which is the direct ancestor of prompt engineering and of treating LMs as general-purpose systems. It vindicates Kaplan et al.’s scaling laws as a planning tool (predictable loss → broadly predictable capability), and its admitted gaps chart the next five years of the field: instruction tuning (FLAN) attacks the gap between zero- and few-shot; RLHF/InstructGPT attacks “predict every token equally” having no notion of intent; retrieval models (RETRO/RAG) attack storing all knowledge in parameters; distillation and MoE attack inference cost (§5, §7). Worth chasing: whether in-context learning learns tasks de novo at inference time or merely locates tasks already seen in pretraining — the authors explicitly cannot tell (§5), and fine-tuning GPT-3, which the paper defers and which becomes the codex/InstructGPT line.

Limitations

  • Text synthesis still degrades over long passages: semantic repetition, lost coherence, contradiction, non-sequiturs; weak “common-sense physics” (“If I put cheese into the fridge, will it melt?”) (§5).
  • Few-shot results depend on per-task prompt phrasing (Appendix G) and K tuned on dev sets — the protocol is standardized, but not free of human task-specific engineering (§2.4).
  • Architecture is deliberately frozen: no bidirectionality, no denoising objectives — likely costing performance exactly on the comparison/re-reading tasks where GPT-3 fails (§5).
  • Pretraining sample efficiency is far below human: the model reads more text than a person sees in a lifetime (§5).
  • It is unresolved whether few-shot “learning” is learning or recognition; the paper’s own framing (“meta-learning”) deliberately spans both (§5, footnote 1).
  • 175B-scale inference is expensive and awkward to deploy; contamination prevention failed (only measurement succeeded); bias analysis is preliminary and prompt-dependent (§4–6).

Critiques and Questions

  1. Single evaluation runs throughout — no variance bars over demonstration sampling, though results demonstrably depend on which K examples are drawn; the ANLI dev set’s ±1.2% std (Fig.3.9) is the only variance estimate offered.
  2. Internal number churn suggests late edits: §3.9.1 prose says 80.2% for 3-digit addition vs 80.4 in Tab.3.9; §3.9.2 prose says 66.9%/38.6%/40.2% for RI/CL/A2 vs 67.2/37.9/39.7 in Tab.3.10. Trivial magnitudes, but a reminder to trust tables over prose.
  3. The headline comparison is asymmetric in both directions: GPT-3 few-shot gets no gradient updates (disadvantage) but its prompts and K are dev-tuned per task and some baselines are old (the paper itself doubts the Fr→En/De→En “supervised SOTA” rows are current, §3.3).
  4. Figure 1.3’s 42-benchmark average mixes incommensurable metrics; the authors flag it as non-rigorous, yet it became the paper’s most-quoted picture.
  5. The contamination analysis cannot rule out that memorization inflates results while an opposite statistical bias cancels it on clean subsets (§4 admits this); the PIQA asterisk story (“smaller model shows the same drop, so it’s bias not memorization”) is plausible but unproven.
Why is the widening gap between zero-shot and few-shot performance as models grow the paper's central piece of evidence?
Because it isolates in-context learning as the thing that scales. If bigger models were simply more knowledgeable, zero-shot and few-shot curves would rise in parallel; instead few-shot rises faster (Fig.1.3), and demonstrations that confuse a 125M model (LAMBADA cloze format, −20 pts) help the 175B model (+10 pts). Scale buys the ability to absorb task structure from the context window itself.
