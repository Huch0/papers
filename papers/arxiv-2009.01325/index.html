RLHF made to actually work: with batch (offline) preference collection, tightly quality-controlled labelers, and a KL-regularized PPO policy whose value function is a separate network initialized from the reward model, a 1.3B GPT-3-style model beats a 10× larger supervised model at Reddit summarization — and both human-feedback models beat the human-written reference summaries themselves.

This is the paper that turned the Ziegler et al. 2019 prototype into the recipe InstructGPT and ChatGPT run on, and it contributes the canonical reward-model overoptimization curve — the empirical face of Goodhart’s law in alignment. Core reading on the LLM/RL milestone path.

Motivation

Supervised fine-tuning maximizes the likelihood of human-written text, but that objective is misaligned with what we actually want — high-quality outputs: it weighs a fabricated fact the same as a synonym choice, spreads probability over low-quality demonstrations, and suffers distributional shift once the model samples its own text (§1). Evaluation has the same disease: ROUGE and reference-matching are rough proxies that correlate poorly with human judgments of summary quality (§1). And the one prior attempt to optimize human preferences directly at scale — our own 2019 work — hit a wall: online data collection produced labelers who rewarded copying, yielding models that scored well with labelers but poorly with researchers (§3.3). The question is whether human preference optimization can be made to genuinely beat imitation, not just game it.

Contribution (what’s new)

The pipeline is inherited (reward model from comparisons → KL-regularized PPO), so the delta is everything that makes it work. Move entirely to the batch setting — alternate between large offline batches of comparisons and retraining — and invest heavily in labeler quality: onboarding with feedback, a shared chat room, hourly (not per-label) pay, and continuous labeler-researcher agreement monitoring, which lifts agreement to 77%, statistically indistinguishable from researchers agreeing with each other (§3.3). Without this, the reward model learns the labelers’ shortcuts, not the task. Algorithmically, the PPO value function gets completely separate parameters, initialized from the reward model — sharing parameters with the policy (as in 2019) lets value-function updates damage the pretrained policy early in training (§3.4, Fig.11). Finally, the analysis contributions: the reward-model overoptimization curve, RM scaling laws in data and model size, and head-to-head evidence that the learned RM beats ROUGE both as a predictor and as an optimization target (§4.3–4.4). The 64,832-comparison dataset is released.

Research questions

  1. Can optimizing human preferences beat strong supervised imitation on a real task — including the human demonstrations themselves? (§4.1)
  2. How far can you optimize against a learned reward model before it stops tracking true human preferences? (§4.3)
  3. How does reward-model quality scale with comparison data and parameters, and what has the RM actually learned — semantics or heuristics like length? (§4.3)
  4. Do preference-trained policies and reward models generalize out of domain (Reddit → news)? (§4.2, §4.4)

Methodology

Three-step loop on the filtered Reddit TL;DR dataset (123,169 posts, reference summaries restricted to 24–48 tokens to control the length confound). Step 1: sample summary pairs from a zoo of policies (supervised baselines, current RL policies, best-of-N, references) and have labelers pick the better one. Step 2: train a reward model — a GPT-3-style Transformer with a scalar head, initialized from the supervised baseline — on the cross-entropy comparison loss. Step 3: run PPO against the reward, with a KL penalty to the supervised policy:

R(x,y)=rθ(x,y)−β log⁡[πϕRL(y∣x)/πSFT(y∣x)]R(x, y) = r_\theta(x, y) - \beta\, \log\left[\pi^{RL}_{\phi}(y \mid x) / \pi^{SFT}(y \mid x)\right]R(x,y)=rθ​(x,y)−βlog[πϕRL​(y∣x)/πSFT(y∣x)]

then collect more comparisons from the improved policy and repeat, in large deliberate batches rather than a live stream (§3.1). A concrete instance (Tab.1): for a first-date-advice post, the supervised model parrots the post’s framing, while the human-feedback model writes “Going on my first ever date tomorrow, cycling to the beach. Would like some tips on what to do and bring. I’m a bit nervous and excited.” — covering intent, situation, and emotional state.

Method details: models, hyperparameters, and evaluation design

Models. GPT-3-architecture decoders at 1.3B and 6.7B for the main experiments (RM ablations span 160M–13B). Pretraining: 200–300B tokens of CommonCrawl, WebText, books, Wikipedia (App.B). Supervised baselines: 1 epoch on filtered TL;DR. Sampling at T=0 for evaluations, found best over a temperature/nucleus sweep (App.B.1).

Reward model. Loss − E [log⁡σ(rθ(x,yi)−rθ(x,y1−i))]-\,\mathbb{E}\,[\log \sigma(r_\theta(x, y_i) - r_\theta(x, y_{1-i}))]−E[logσ(rθ​(x,yi​)−rθ​(x,y1−i​))] over pairs; one epoch; outputs normalized so reference summaries score mean 0. Selection sweeps over learning rate and 3–10 seeds — both data order and head initialization matter (App.B.1).

PPO. 1M episodes, γ=1\gamma = 1γ=1, GAE λ=0.95\lambda = 0.95λ=0.95, KL coefficient 0.05 for main runs, batch 512/256 for 1.3B/6.7B. Value function: separate Transformer, initialized from the RM (App.B.1, G.1).

Best-of-N. Sampling N summaries and keeping the RM’s favorite serves as a training-free, mildly optimized policy (KL grows as log⁡N−(N−1)/N\log N - (N-1)/NlogN−(N−1)/N); BoN and PPO give similar human-judged quality at equal average reward, with PPO drifting further in KL (App.G.3).

Evaluation. Pairwise preference vs the reference summary, plus 7-point Likert ratings on coherence, accuracy, coverage, and overall quality; length-controlled numbers via regression on the log length ratio (App.F). Labelers write “naive interpretations” of summaries before seeing the post — a trick that surfaces ambiguity (App.C.1).

Main result

The 1.3B human-feedback policy is preferred to the dataset’s human reference summaries 61% of the time — beating a 6.7B supervised model (43%), 10× its size; the 6.7B human-feedback model does better still, and remains ~65% preferred after controlling for its longer summaries (Fig.1, §4.1). On Likert axes the gain is concentrated where it should be: human-feedback models improve most on coverage, and the 6.7B PPO model earns a perfect 7/7 overall score on 45% of summaries vs 20% for the supervised baseline and 23% for the references (Fig.3). The models are not copying their way to victory: bigram overlap with the post is lower than the supervised baseline’s (46.0% vs 48.9% at 6.7B, App.G.5) — the 2019 failure mode is gone. Transfer is the kicker: on CNN/DM news, never seen in training, the 6.7B human-feedback model nearly matches a 6.7B model supervised on CNN/DM itself, while writing summaries half as long (Fig.4).

Key findings

  • Optimizing the reward model helps, then hurts: as the policy moves further from the supervised baseline (lower β, more KL), the RM’s predicted preference keeps climbing while actual human preference peaks and then falls below the starting point — the RM eventually becomes anti-correlated with real preferences (Fig.5). The overoptimized policy’s outputs are a template of RM-pleasing tics (”… want change this dumbass shitty ass policy pls halp”, Tab.29).
  • Reward modeling scales predictably: doubling comparison data buys ~1.1% validation accuracy, doubling RM parameters buys ~1.8%, and the 6.7B RM trained on all 64k comparisons approaches single-human accuracy (Fig.6) (an ensemble of humans remains above it).
  • The RM tracks semantics, with a known bias: it prefers human-improved edits nearly as often as independent humans do (82.8% vs 84.1% at 6.7B) and catches role-reversal perturbations 97.2% of the time — but favors length, endorsing edits that shorten a summary only 62.6% of the time vs 76.4% for humans (App.G.6, Tab.17–18).
  • ROUGE fails both as predictor and as target: its agreement with labelers drops from ~57% on supervised samples to ~50% (chance) on human-feedback samples, and best-of-N optimization against ROUGE peaks sooner and at a substantially lower human-preference rate than optimization against any RM (§4.4, Fig.7). Supervised-model log probability does no better (≤50%).
  • The CNN/DM “reference summaries” are themselves weak: labelers prefer lead-3 over them even after length adjustment, because the news highlights omit key points (13/20 audited cases) and introduce information absent from the article (10/20) (App.E) — a warning about treating scraped references as gold.
  • Separating value-function and policy parameters clearly outperforms the shared network used in the 2019 work (App.G.1, Fig.11): a small architectural decision with outsized effect on RL stability.

Implications

This paper completes the arc from Christiano et al. 2017 (preference RL) through Ziegler et al. 2019 (LM fine-tuning prototype) to a recipe that demonstrably exceeds imitation — the same team and pipeline produce InstructGPT and thence ChatGPT, with this paper’s batch collection, labeler-relationship management, and separate value network carried over nearly verbatim. The overoptimization curve (Fig.5) becomes the reference phenomenon for reward-model Goodharting, formalized in later “scaling laws for reward model overoptimization” work, and best-of-N-vs-PPO equivalence at matched KL prefigures inference-time alignment methods. Worth chasing: the paper’s own point that comparisons are one signal among many — demonstrations, edits, and explanations as richer feedback (the thread leading to critique and process supervision), and scaling feedback to tasks humans cannot directly evaluate, which the discussion names as the hard case (recursive reward modeling / amplification lineage) (§5).

Limitations

  • Cost, and a missing baseline because of it: the 6.7B RL run took ~320 GPU-days and the dataset thousands of labeler-hours, so there is no equal-labeler-budget baseline of high-quality demonstrations for supervised fine-tuning — “human feedback beats supervised” is shown against existing references, not against equally curated imitation data (§5, App.D).
  • Length confounds a third of the headline gap at 6.7B; the correction is a regression-based estimate, not a controlled generation-length experiment (Fig.1 caption, App.F).
  • The task definition is circular by construction: quality is “as good as possible according to our judgments”, labelers are trained to agree with the researchers, and researcher preference is the gold standard — the paper itself flags that broader tasks will need affected groups, not researchers, defining ‘good’ (§3.2, Broader impacts).
  • Domain narrowness: two-thirds of the filtered TL;DR corpus is relationship/relationship-advice posts, and posts run only to 512 tokens (App.A) — CNN/DM transfer mitigates but does not eliminate the concern.
  • The training corpus’s effective label quality is ~73% agreement, and attempts to filter to high-confidence labels or drop weak labelers reduced RM accuracy — noise is load-bearing and not fully understood (App.C.2).
  • A known RM length bias survives the pipeline; the policy exploits it (summaries cluster at the upper length limit) (App.G.6, App.F).

Critiques and Questions

  1. “Better than the human references” is partly a statement about reference quality: TL;DRs are casual Reddit self-summaries and CNN/DM highlights are demonstrably flawed (App.E). Against genuinely expert-written summaries, the margin is untested — the honest claim is “better than the imitation targets available”, which is still the practically relevant one.
  2. Labeler-researcher agreement (77%) exceeding researcher-researcher agreement (73%) is presented as success, but training labelers to mirror researchers narrows the preference distribution to one team’s taste; the same machinery would faithfully amplify a worse taste.
  3. The overoptimization study (Fig.5) uses an earlier RM (rm3) and varies β across runs; there is no equivalent curve for the final RM, so the safe operating point for the headline models is extrapolated, not measured.
  4. Data composition is admittedly ad hoc — comparisons come from a shifting zoo of policies chosen “in the spirit of exploratory research” (App.C.6); how sensitive the RM is to this mixture is unknown and matters for anyone replicating on a budget.
  5. BoN matching PPO quality at equal reward (App.G.3) quietly undercuts the necessity of RL itself; the paper does not pursue when expensive PPO is actually preferable to rejection sampling — a question the field returned to years later.
What happens as the policy is optimized ever harder against the learned reward model, and why is this the paper's most durable lesson?
Predicted reward keeps rising, but actual human preference peaks and then declines — eventually the RM becomes anti-correlated with true preferences (Fig.5), producing degenerate template-like summaries (Tab.29). The RM is only a proxy fit to a narrow distribution of comparisons; hard optimization exits that distribution. This is the canonical empirical demonstration of proxy-reward Goodharting in LLM alignment, and it is why every descendant system caps KL or limits optimization pressure against a frozen reward model.
