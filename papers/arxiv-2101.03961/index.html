Routing every token to just one expert (top-1, not top-2+) plus three stability fixes — selective float32 in the router, 10x-smaller initialization, expert dropout — turns Mixture-of-Experts from a fragile translation trick into a general recipe: 7x faster pre-training than a FLOP-matched T5-Base and the first trillion-parameter (1.6T) language model.

This is the paper that made sparsity a first-class scaling axis for LLMs: parameters can grow independently of FLOPs per token. Every later MoE LLM (GLaM, Mixtral, DeepSeek-MoE, GPT-4-era rumors) inherits its routing simplification, capacity-factor framing, and stability toolkit. Essential context for the milestone path right after GPT-3’s dense-scaling thesis.

Motivation

Dense scaling à la GPT-3 works but every added parameter costs FLOPs on every token — the compute bill grows in lockstep with capacity (§1). Mixture-of-Experts promised parameters without proportional compute, yet after Shazeer 2017 and GShard, MoE saw little adoption outside translation because of (1) model and implementation complexity, (2) training instabilities — worse in bfloat16, forcing GShard to train entirely in float32 — and (3) all-to-all communication costs (§1, §2.4, §7). A background belief also blocked simplification: Shazeer et al. conjectured you must route each token to at least k=2 experts, or the router gets no signal to compare experts and cannot learn (§2.1).

Contribution (what’s new)

The central move is deliberately destructive: route each token to exactly one expert (the “Switch” layer) and show the k≥2 requirement was a myth — the gate value pi(x)p_i(x)pi​(x) multiplying the chosen expert’s output already carries gradient to the router (§2.1, Eq.1–2). k=1 is not just simpler; it is forced by the large-model regime: it halves each expert’s batch (“capacity”), cuts routing computation, and halves the all-to-all traffic — exactly the three costs that blocked adoption. Around it, a stability toolkit that makes sparse models trainable at bfloat16 and at trillion-parameter scale for the first time: selective precision (float32 only inside the local router softmax, recast to bfloat16 before any communication), truncated-normal initialization shrunk 10x, and “expert dropout” (0.4 inside experts vs 0.1 elsewhere) for fine-tuning on small tasks (§2.4). Finally, a demonstration that parameter count — at fixed FLOPs per token — is its own scaling axis, validated from 2 experts up to Switch-C’s 1.6T parameters (§3, §5.6).

Research questions

  1. Can top-1 routing match or beat top-k MoE in quality per FLOP and per hour? (§2.3)
  2. For a fixed compute budget and wall clock, should you train a dense or a sparse model? (§3.2)
  3. Do upstream perplexity gains survive fine-tuning, distillation, and multilingual multi-task training? (§4)
  4. How do data-, model-, and expert-parallelism compose up to a trillion parameters? (§5)

Methodology

Start from T5: encoder-decoder, span-corruption masked LM on C4, quality measured as negative log perplexity. Replace the FFN in every other block with a Switch layer of N expert FFNs. Follow one token through it: the router (one matrix WrW_rWr​) computes a softmax over N experts; the token — say x1x_1x1​ = “More” — gets p=0.65 for expert 2, so it is sent to expert 2 only, and expert 2’s output is scaled by 0.65 (Fig.2). That scaling is the whole trick for differentiability. Because TPUs need static tensor shapes, every expert has a fixed bucket size, expert capacity = (tokens per batch / N) × capacity factor; a token routed to a full expert is dropped — skipped by the layer, passed on through the residual connection (Fig.3, Eq.3). An auxiliary loss pushes routing toward uniform so drops stay rare (typically under 1%, §2.2). All comparisons are FLOP-matched: a Switch-Base applies exactly T5-Base’s compute per token, whatever its parameter count.

Selective precision (Tab.2): pure bfloat16 diverges (−3.780 vs −1.718 for float32); casting only the router’s local softmax computation to float32 recovers float32 stability (−1.716) at bfloat16 speed (1390 vs 1160 examples/s), because no float32 tensor ever enters all-to-all communication.

Initialization (Tab.3): truncated normal with σ=s/n\sigma=\sqrt{s/n}σ=s/n​, scale s reduced from 1.0 to 0.1. At 3.5k steps (32 experts, 3 seeds): quality −2.72 ± 0.01 vs −3.60 ± 0.68 — both better and drastically lower variance. The same scheme trains 223M through 1.6T+ models.

Capacity factor (Tab.1): Switch prefers lean buffers — CF 1.0 gives 1000 examples/s vs 860 at CF 2.0, with little quality cost; MoE top-2 needs bigger buffers and still trains slower. Router exploration: multiplicative jitter noise on the router input beats argmax, input dropout, and softmax sampling (Tab.11, App.C).

Expert dropout (Tab.4): fine-tuning with uniform higher dropout hurts; 0.1 everywhere except 0.4 inside expert FFNs is best (GLUE 85.2 vs 84.7 at uniform 0.1) — the sensible regularization when 99% of parameters live in experts and downstream tasks are tiny.

Main result

For fixed FLOPs, hardware, and wall clock, sparse wins across every regime tested. Switch-Base with 64 experts reaches T5-Base’s quality in one-seventh the training time (Fig.5, §3.2); on a step basis it hits T5-Base’s 450k-step perplexity at 60k steps (7.5x, §3.1). It even beats the bigger dense alternative: T5-Large applies 3.5x more FLOPs per token, yet Switch-Base is still 2.5x faster to equal quality (Fig.6, §3.3). Head-to-head at 128 experts, Switch beats top-2 MoE per step and per hour (time to −1.50 nats: 62.8h vs 68.7h best-MoE; Tab.1). Fine-tuned:

Key findings

  • Top-1 routing is not worse than top-2 — it is better per FLOP and per hour (Tab.1, §2.1): the field’s founding conjecture (routers need ≥2 experts for gradient signal) was simply wrong, and the savings compound (halved capacity, halved communication).
  • Experts are a clean scaling axis (Fig.4): at fixed FLOPs per token, quality improves consistently from 2 to 256 experts (223M → 14.7B params), and even 2–8 experts beat T5-Base — one expert per core, no supercomputer required (App.D, Fig.12).
  • FLOPs per token still matter independently of parameters (§5.6, §8): Switch-XXL (395B params, 10x the FLOPs/seq) fine-tunes to 89.6 SQuAD vs 87.7 for the 1.6T-parameter Switch-C at similar perplexity — the paper’s own headline axis (params ↑, FLOPs fixed) buys perplexity but not all of downstream quality.
  • Upstream-downstream transfer is task-dependent (App.E, Fig.13): perplexity correlates with downstream quality for both dense and sparse, but at fixed perplexity dense models often do better on SuperGLUE (reasoning) while Switch does better on TriviaQA (knowledge) — sparse capacity seems to buy memorized knowledge more readily than reasoning.
  • Instability tracks FLOPs per token, not parameter count (§5.6): the 1.6T Switch-C trains with no instability at all, while the FLOP-heavy Switch-XXL sporadically destabilizes despite the full stability toolkit — left unsolved (§8.1).
  • No-Token-Left-Behind, an obvious-seeming fix, does nothing (App.B): iteratively rerouting overflowed tokens to their second-choice expert guarantees near-zero drops yet yields no quality gain — evidence the network learns specific token→expert associations that a substitute expert cannot honor.
  • Sparse teachers distill usefully into dense students (Tab.6–8, §4.2): initializing the student’s non-expert weights from the teacher plus a 0.75-hard/0.25-soft loss keeps ~30% of the sparse quality gain at 95–99% compression (28% at 99%); same 30% holds when distilling a fine-tuned SuperGLUE model (81.3 → 76.6 vs 74.6 baseline).
  • The gains are universal across 101 languages (Fig.7–8): mSwitch-Base beats mT5-Base on every language, mean 5x step speedup, 91% of languages ≥4x — multi-task interference does not erase the sparsity advantage.

Implications

Together with GPT-3, this sets up the two-axis view of scale: dense FLOPs and sparse parameters. It rehabilitates the Shazeer 2017 sparsely-gated MoE and GShard lineage for general NLP and directly enables the GLaM / ST-MoE / Mixtral / DeepSeek-MoE line of sparse LLMs; its distillation result previews the deploy-time pattern (train sparse, serve dense/small). The paper is also an early, candid case study in hardware-software co-design: static TPU shapes force capacity factors, which force load-balancing losses and token dropping — machinery that exists for the accelerator, not the math (§2.2, Fig.3). Worth chasing: why sparse capacity transfers to knowledge tasks better than reasoning tasks (App.E) — foreshadows later fine-tuning pathologies of MoE, heterogeneous or variable-compute experts (§8.4), and expert layers in attention, which improved quality but diverged in bfloat16 (App.A).

Limitations

  • Switch-XXL instability is unsolved; the model was not trained the full 1M steps, so its comparisons are partial (§5.6, §8).
  • Downstream SOTA is not reached where it counts for reasoning: SuperGLUE 87.5 vs T5’s 89.3 despite better perplexity; only ANLI (65.7 vs 49.4) and the closed-book QA trio (NQ 34.4, WebQ 41.0, TriviaQA 47.5) set SOTA (§5.6).
  • Quality is measured almost entirely as C4 negative log perplexity for the scaling claims; speedup numbers depend on implementation and interconnect (admitted in footnote 5), so “7x” is a TPU-mesh-specific figure (§2.3).
  • Fine-tuned ARC shows no gains at either scale — an unexplained failure case (Tab.5, §4.1).
  • T5-XXL baselines were pre-trained on the older, duplicated C4, making the sparse-vs-dense gap at XXL scale a lower bound but also not perfectly controlled (footnote 10).
  • Token dropping means some tokens skip the layer entirely; the paper reports under 1% drops but the quality cost of dropping is not isolated (§2.2).

Critiques and Questions

  1. The headline “7x speedup” compares against T5-Base at equal FLOPs per token but not equal memory or interconnect: 64 experts require sharding weights across 64 cores with all-to-all communication — the fair unit of cost (device-hours on a given cluster) is used, but portability to GPU clusters with weaker interconnects is asserted, not shown (§2).
  2. Perplexity-to-capability slippage is documented by the paper itself (App.E, SQuAD anomaly) yet the abstract’s speedups are all perplexity-based; a skeptical reading is “sparsity buys perplexity cheaply, capability less cheaply.”
  3. Time-to-quality-threshold (−1.50) is an “arbitrarily chosen” bar (Tab.1); speedup ratios can be sensitive to where the bar is placed on converging curves.
  4. Expert-parallelism ties expert count to core count (one expert per core), so the architecture search space is partly dictated by cluster topology — a hardware-lottery effect the discussion acknowledges by citing Hooker but does not quantify.
  5. Load balance vs specialization tension is unexamined: the auxiliary loss pushes uniform routing while the NTLB result shows experts are not interchangeable — what does the uniformity pressure cost in specialization quality?
Why can the router still learn with k=1 routing, when prior work claimed at least two experts were needed for a gradient signal?
The selected expert's output is multiplied by its router probability p_i(x) (Eq.2). Because that gate value is a differentiable function of the router weights, the loss gradient flows through the scaling factor even though only one expert runs — increasing p_i when the expert helped, decreasing it when it hurt. Comparison across experts happens implicitly over many tokens, not within one forward pass.
