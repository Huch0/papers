For a fixed training-compute budget, model size and training tokens should scale in equal proportion (double the model ⇒ double the data) — validated by Chinchilla, a 70B model trained on 1.4T tokens with Gopher’s exact compute budget that beats the 4×-larger Gopher on essentially every benchmark.

This is the paper that reset how the field sizes language models: “Chinchilla-optimal” replaced Kaplan scaling as the default sizing rule, and it reframed data, not parameters, as the binding constraint on LLM progress. Essential foundation for reading any later pretraining or scaling paper.

Motivation

You typically get one shot at training a frontier model: the budget — how many accelerators, for how long — is fixed before you start. So the allocation question “given CCC FLOPs, how big a model on how many tokens?” must be answered in advance, and if the allocation rule is wrong, every flagship run wastes its budget.

The rule the field was using came from Kaplan et al. (2020): per 10× more compute, grow the model 5.5× and the data only 1.8× (§1). The field obeyed — GPT-3 (175B), Jurassic-1 (178B), Gopher (280B), MT-NLG (530B) were all trained on roughly 300B tokens regardless of size (Tab.1). We saw two structural flaws in that estimate: Kaplan et al. reused one fixed ~130B-token learning-rate schedule for every run, so every intermediate loss overestimates what a properly-scheduled shorter run achieves — systematically undervaluing training on more data (§2); and their fits were dominated by very small models (mostly under 100M params), while the frontier shows curvature at scale (§2, App.E).

Contribution (what’s new)

The compute-optimal frontier is symmetric: Nopt∝C0.5N_{opt}\propto C^{0.5}Nopt​∝C0.5 and Dopt∝C0.5D_{opt}\propto C^{0.5}Dopt​∝C0.5 — every doubling of model size demands a doubling of training tokens, which means the 2022 generation of LLMs was drastically oversized and undertrained. The measurement has to differ from Kaplan et al.’s: the cosine learning-rate schedule length must be matched to each run’s token horizon, otherwise the schedule artifact above re-enters and biases you toward “params matter more”. We estimate the frontier three independent ways, and then make the result falsifiable with one decisive bet: Chinchilla — the same 5.76e23 FLOPs as Gopher, spent as 70B params × 1.4T tokens instead of 280B × 300B (§4).

  2. Are current large models correctly sized for their budgets — and if not, by how much?
  3. Does the loss-optimal reallocation actually win downstream (few-shot benchmarks), not just in training loss?

Methodology

Train over 400 models (70M–16B params, 5B–500B tokens), then estimate the frontier three ways that share no fitting machinery. Carry one example through everything: Gopher’s budget, 5.76e23 FLOPs — Kaplan’s rule spent it as 280B params on 300B tokens; each of our estimators answers what it should have bought.

The three estimation approaches
  1. Approach 1 — minimum over training curves (§3.1) — Fix a family of model sizes (70M to 10B+); train each for 4 token horizons spanning 16×, each with its cosine schedule decaying 10× over that horizon. Smooth and interpolate every loss curve, take the lower envelope of loss vs FLOPs, and at 1500 log-spaced FLOP values read off which size wins. Power-law fit: a = 0.50, b = 0.50. Projects 67B / 1.5T tokens at the Gopher budget (Tab.3).
  2. Approach 2 — IsoFLOP profiles (§3.2) — Fix 9 total budgets from 6e18 to 3e21 FLOPs; at each budget vary model size (up to 16B) with tokens set so total FLOPs is constant. Final loss vs size shows a clear valley; fit a parabola per budget to locate the minimum. Power-law fit through the valley points: a = 0.49, b = 0.51. Projects 63B / 1.4T at the Gopher budget (Fig.3).
  3. Approach 3 — parametric loss surface (§3.3) — Fit a closed-form loss to all final losses from Approaches 1 and 2 (Huber loss, δ=1e-3, L-BFGS from a grid of inits), then minimize it under FLOPs ≈ 6ND. Gives a = 0.46, b = 0.54, and projects an even smaller 40B model at the Gopher budget (Fig.4).

Chinchilla training setup (§4.1)

Same architecture and data (MassiveText) as Gopher, so the comparison isolates the allocation. Differences: 80 layers but d_model 8,192 vs 16,384 and 64 vs 128 heads; 4× higher max LR (1e-4); batch 1.5M→3M tokens (Tab.4); AdamW instead of Adam; a SentencePiece tokenizer without NFKC normalization (94.15% token overlap with Gopher’s — helps math/chemistry text); bfloat16 compute with a float32 master copy of weights in the optimizer state. Trained on TPUv3/v4 with JAX. Data reweighted for 1.4T tokens — Wikipedia is repeated 3.40 epochs, MassiveWeb 1.24 (Tab.A1).

Main result

At identical training compute, the smaller-but-longer-trained model wins nearly everywhere: MMLU 5-shot 67.6% vs Gopher’s 60.0% (+7.6), beating GPT-3 (43.9%) and even the expert-forecast SOTA for June 2023 (63.4%) (Tab.6, p.11), with more than 90% accuracy on 4 of 57 subjects — the first model to do so on any subject.

Key findings

  • Three independent estimators converge on equal scaling: a/b = 0.50/0.50, 0.49/0.51, 0.46/0.54 — versus Kaplan et al.’s 0.73/0.27 (Tab.2): the conclusion is not an artifact of one fitting method.
  • The schedule artifact is real and large: a cosine cycle that overshoots the training horizon by more than ~25% visibly degrades final loss (Fig.A1, App.B) — this is the mechanism by which Kaplan et al.’s fixed-length schedule inflated intermediate losses and undervalued data.
  • The implied undertraining is enormous: a 175B (GPT-3-sized) model is compute-optimal only with ~3.85e24 FLOPs and 3.7T tokens; a 1T-param model is not optimal below ~1e26 FLOPs (220+ Gopher budgets) (Tab.3) — so data collection, not model engineering, becomes the bottleneck for further scaling.
  • The equal-scaling law replicates on C4 (0.50/0.50) and GitHub code (0.53/0.47) (Tab.A2): within the single-epoch regime the allocation rule is dataset-independent.
  • A direct small-scale duel at 1e21 FLOPs confirms the fit: our predicted-optimal 2.80B model ends at lower loss than the Kaplan-predicted 4.74B model (Fig.A4, App.D.4) — the same experiment that Chinchilla-vs-Gopher runs at 5.76e23.
  • TruthfulQA improves 14.1% 0-shot (43.6% vs 29.5%) purely from better pretraining (§4.2.5) — contradicting the prior narrative that better language models are necessarily more prone to imitative falsehoods.
  • The FLOP-loss frontier shows concavity at high compute, and Approach 3 (which downweights small runs) predicts the smallest optimal models (App.E, §3.4) — meaning even this paper likely overestimates optimal model size at large budgets.
  • The optimizer choices matter independently: AdamW beats Adam and a float32 master-weight copy helps at every scale tested (Fig.A6–A7, App.G) — though AdamW’s advantage only materializes in the last ~20% of the cosine cycle (fn.8).

Implications

The sizing rule for the field changed overnight: Gopher and GPT-3-era models were retroactively revealed as ~4× oversized, and subsequent models (the LLaMA line most explicitly) trained smaller models on far more tokens — often deliberately past Chinchilla-optimal, since inference cost amortizes over a model’s lifetime, a trade-off this paper motivates but does not formalize. The result also elevates data-side approaches like retrieval augmentation (RETRO), which effectively multiply tokens seen. Threads worth chasing: what replaces the law when data runs out — the multi-epoch / data-constrained regime the paper explicitly excludes, inference-aware “overtraining” as an explicit objective, and whether the fitted exponents (α=0.34, β=0.28 — both below the theoretical 1/2) can be raised by better architectures or data quality.

Limitations

  • Only two comparable runs exist at frontier scale (Chinchilla and Gopher) with nothing at intermediate scales — the extrapolation spans two orders of magnitude on faith in power laws (§5).
  • The power-law form is assumed despite observed concavity at high budgets (App.E), so optimal sizes at large C may be overestimated (§5).
  • Everything is single-epoch; the analysis is silent about repeating data — yet its own projections (e.g. 3.7T+ tokens for 175B, Tab.3) exceed the curated data available at the time, making the multi-epoch regime unavoidable (§5).
  • Chinchilla is trained once (no variance estimate, model card Tab.A8) and differs from Gopher in optimizer, tokenizer, LR, and batch size — App.G shows the optimizer change alone improves loss, so the headline gap is allocation plus tuning, not allocation alone.
  • Language-modelling comparisons (Pile, Wikitext103) are confounded by 4× more training data raising leakage risk — the paper itself redirects emphasis to MMLU/BIG-bench for this reason (§4.2.1).

Critiques and Questions

  1. Internal number inconsistencies. The abstract says MMLU 67.5% but Tab.6 says 67.6%; §3.4 prose says a 175B model needs 4.41e24 FLOPs / 4.2T tokens while Tab.3 (Approach 1) says 3.85e24 / 3.7T; §4.2.6 prose quotes Gopher NQ at 21%/28% while Tab.9 says 24.5%/28.2%. All minor, but they suggest prose written against a different fit or model revision than the tables.
  2. “Compute-optimal” optimizes the wrong objective for practice. The frontier minimizes pretraining loss per training FLOP; total lifetime cost (training + inference) would push optima to even smaller models trained longer. The paper argues this qualitatively (§1, §4) but never re-derives the frontier under it.
  3. How much of Chinchilla’s downstream win comes from lower loss vs from seeing 4× more facts? Closed-book QA (Tab.9) plausibly rewards data coverage directly, independent of the loss argument — the paper does not separate the two.
  4. The three approaches disagree exactly where it matters most (40B vs 63B vs 67B at the Gopher budget); choosing 70B — outside all three point estimates — was justified by “dataset and computational efficiency considerations” (§4), i.e., partially pragmatic.
Why did Kaplan et al. (2020) conclude that model size should scale much faster than data (0.73 vs 0.27), and what did this paper change to overturn it?
They trained every model with one fixed ~130B-token cosine LR schedule, so runs evaluated mid-schedule show inflated losses relative to a model properly scheduled for that shorter horizon — making extra data look less valuable; their fits were also dominated by sub-100M-param models. This paper matches the cosine cycle length to each run's actual token horizon (overshooting by >25% measurably hurts, Fig.A1) and uses mostly 500M+ models, after which all three estimation approaches yield equal ~C^0.5 scaling for both parameters and tokens.
