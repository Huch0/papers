Scaling a dense decoder-only Transformer to 540B parameters (on 6144 TPU v4 chips via Pathways, at record 46.2% model-FLOPs utilization) shows few-shot gains have not plateaued: new SOTA on 28/29 English NLP benchmarks, above-average-human BIG-bench performance, ~25% of tasks improving discontinuously with scale, and — combined with chain-of-thought prompting — few-shot reasoning that matches finetuned SOTA.

PaLM is the “scale axis pushed to its limit” landmark: it made emergent capabilities and chain-of-thought reasoning the field’s central storyline, introduced MFU as the standard training-efficiency metric, and (read against Chinchilla, published a week earlier) is the clearest natural experiment on parameters-vs-tokens allocation.

Motivation

After GPT-3, every frontier model (GLaM, Gopher, Chinchilla, MT-NLG, LaMDA) improved few-shot performance through some mix of four levers: size, training tokens, data quality, and sparsity (§1). Two things blocked us from pushing the first lever further. Nobody knew whether pure scale was already saturating — whether the next 4× of parameters buys log-linear gains, nothing, or something qualitatively new. And no training system could efficiently go past a single accelerator pod without pipeline parallelism, whose “bubble” overhead and per-microbatch weight reloading waste exactly the compute you are trying to scale (§4). A secondary annoyance: reported “hardware FLOPs utilization” numbers are implementation-dependent (rematerialization inflates them), so training-efficiency claims across systems were not comparable (§4.1).

Contribution (what’s new)

Not a new architecture — a deliberate bet on a well-established recipe at unprecedented scale, made possible by systems work. Pathways orchestrates two full TPU v4 pods (6144 chips) with pod-level two-way data parallelism and no pipelining — each pod holds a complete model copy (12-way tensor × 256-way sharded-data parallel), pods exchange 1.3 GB of gradients per host pair per step (81 Tbps bursts), reaching 1.95× single-pod throughput (§4). MFU — observed tokens/sec over the theoretical peak counting only required forward+backward FLOPs (≈ 6N per token) — makes efficiency comparable across systems (§4.1, App.B). On top of that infrastructure, the scientific payload: evaluating three identically-trained scales (8B/62B/540B) exposes discontinuous capability jumps that log-linear extrapolation from the two smaller models cannot predict, and scale × chain-of-thought prompting replaces task-specific finetuning pipelines on multi-step reasoning (§6.2, §6.3).

Research questions

  1. Have few-shot improvements from model scale plateaued at the ~200–500B regime? (§1)
  2. Which capabilities emerge only past a scale threshold, rather than growing smoothly? (§6.2)
  3. Can few-shot prompting alone (no finetuning, no verifiers, no domain architectures) reach finetuned SOTA on multi-step reasoning? (§6.3)
  4. How do you train past one pod at high efficiency without pipelining? (§4)

Methodology

Train three dense decoder-only Transformers — 8B, 62B, 540B (118 layers, dmodel=18432d_{model}=18432dmodel​=18432, 48 heads, Tab.1) — identically on the same 780B-token corpus for exactly one epoch, then compare scaling curves internally. The corpus (from the LaMDA/GLaM line): 50% social-media conversations, 27% filtered webpages, 13% books, 5% GitHub code, 4% Wikipedia, 1% news; ~22% non-English (Tab.2, §1). No new objective, no sparsity — so any capability difference is attributable to scale.

Architecture and training choices (and why)

Modifications to the vanilla Transformer, each with a stated reason (§2):

  • SwiGLU MLP activations — quality gain in compute-matched comparisons.
  • Parallel layers — y = x + MLP(LN(x)) + Attn(LN(x)) instead of sequential composition: ~15% faster large-scale training since MLP and attention input matmuls fuse; measured quality-neutral at 62B (small degradation at 8B), extrapolated neutral at 540B.
  • Multi-query attention — one shared key/value head, kkk query heads: neutral for training, large speedup for autoregressive decoding.
  • RoPE embeddings, shared input/output embeddings, no bias terms (stability), and a lossless 256k SentencePiece vocabulary (whitespace-preserving for code; digits split individually).

Training (§5): Adafactor without factorization (= Adam + parameter scaling), LR 10−210^{-2}10−2 for 10k steps then 1/k1/\sqrt{k}1/k​ decay, β2=1−k−0.8\beta_2 = 1-k^{-0.8}β2​=1−k−0.8 (rare-token second moments are poorly estimated with the standard 0.99), global-norm clip 1.0, dynamic weight decay lr2lr^2lr2, auxiliary z-loss 10−4⋅log⁡2Z10^{-4}\cdot\log^2 Z10−4⋅log2Z to keep the softmax normalizer near zero (stability), no dropout, sequence length 2048, batch ramp 512 → 1024 → 2048 (1M → 4M tokens), 255k steps, bitwise-deterministic restarts.

Instability (§5.1): ~20 loss spikes at the 540B scale despite clipping, absent in smaller models. Mitigation: restart ~100 steps before the spike and skip 200–500 batches. Replaying the same batches from a different checkpoint does not spike — so spikes come from a specific data + parameter-state combination, not “bad data”. No principled fix; this is admitted engineering folklore.

Main result

Scale still pays, across the board: few-shot SOTA on 28 of the 29 most-evaluated English NLP benchmarks (24/29 at 1-shot) versus the best per-task result from GPT-3, GLaM, Gopher, Chinchilla, MT-NLG, and LaMDA — and PaLM 540B beats the similarly-sized MT-NLG 530B on every one (Tab.4, §6.1), so tokens and data quality matter beyond raw size.

On BIG-bench, 5-shot PaLM 540B exceeds the average human score on both the 58-task and 150-task aggregates and beats prior SOTA on 44/58 common tasks (Fig.3–4) — though average humans still beat it on 35% of individual tasks and “best human” remains far ahead (Fig.6–7).

Key findings

  • Discontinuous improvement is common, not exotic: 25% of 150 BIG-bench tasks jump more than +10% over the log-linear projection from 8B→62B (15% jump more than +20%) (§6.2, Fig.5). Example: logical_sequence scores 13/25/87 across 8B/62B/540B where extrapolation predicts ~37; english_proverbs goes 25% → 87% from 62B → 540B. This is the paper’s core evidence that capabilities emerge past a scale threshold.
  • Chain-of-thought is the multiplier on scale: on GSM8K, 540B goes 17% → 54% just by adding CoT exemplars (calculator adds only ~4 more points), and scaling 62B→540B fixes 22 of 45 analyzed reasoning errors (Tab.10, Fig.9) — SOTA on 4 of 7 reasoning benchmarks with 8-shot prompting alone; neither CoT nor scale suffices by itself (62B+CoT: 33%).
  • The concurrent Chinchilla comparison undercuts pure parameter scaling: Chinchilla (70B/1.4T tokens, 588 ZFLOPs) sits above PaLM’s interpolated compute-scaling curve on BIG-bench while PaLM 540B used 2527 ZFLOPs (§13, Tab.21, Fig.24) — the authors concede they cannot answer “would 120B on 3.6T tokens have matched 540B?” partly because their corpus starts repeating after 780B tokens.
  • Training 62B longer (to 1.325T tokens) on refreshed data closes much of the gap — MMLU 53.7→62.8, GSM8K 33.0→48.7, HumanEval 15.9→23.7 — while repeating subcorpora is measurably worse than adding new data; but even +67% FLOPs does not bridge to 540B (App.F, Tab.32–33): both axes matter, and repeated data has degraded value.
  • PaLM 62B beats GPT-3 175B on both average NLU (67.3 vs 65.4) and NLG (57.7 vs 52.9) (Tab.5, §13) — data quality (lever 3) can substitute for ~3× parameters, though the paper does not ablate this.
  • Memorization scales with size and duplication: 540B exactly reproduces 2.4% of sampled 50-token training continuations (8B: 1.6%); spans seen once are memorized 0.75% of the time, spans seen 500+ times over 40% (§7, Fig.18) — and the contamination audit (§8, Tab.18) shows balanced clean-vs-full deltas across scales, arguing headline results are not memorization-inflated.
  • Model continuations track prompt toxicity far more tightly than human continuations do (Fig.23, §10.2), and identity-term bias co-occurrences (e.g., Islam with “terrorist”) are ~70% identical between 62B and 540B — bias is inherited from data, not created by scale (§10.1.2).
  • 0-shot translation jumps discontinuously too: +13 BLEU (en-de) and +17 BLEU (en-fr) going 62B→540B, with de-en and ro-en few-shot beating (dated) supervised SOTA (Fig.15, §6.5); translating into English remains much stronger than out of it.

Implications

PaLM set the agenda for the next two years: the “emergent abilities” research program (Wei et al.), chain-of-thought prompting as default methodology, and the Flan-PaLM / U-PaLM / Minerva / PaLM 2 line all build directly on this model; MFU became the standard way to report training efficiency, and multi-query attention + parallel layers became standard inference-friendly architecture choices. Read together with Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 2022), which §13 engages honestly, PaLM marks the moment the field pivoted from “more parameters” to “more (fresh) tokens per parameter”. Worth chasing: whether discontinuities are real capability phase-changes or artifacts of exact-match metrics (the later “mirage” debate), the value of repeated data when fresh data runs out (App.F is an early data-constrained-scaling result), and principled explanations for large-scale loss spikes.

Limitations

  • No size-vs-tokens ablation at scale — the paper’s own “critical open scaling question” (62B@7T vs 540B@780B) is explicitly left unanswered due to cost, batch-size limits, and data exhaustion (§13).
  • Corpus data begins repeating at 780B tokens, capping the token axis for this data pipeline (§13).
  • The parallel-layers choice was validated at 62B and extrapolated to 540B without a check (§2); similarly, 540B loss-spike mitigation (skip batches) is unprincipled (§5.1).
  • Fairness/bias analysis is English-only despite a multilingual model, uses brittle prompt templates, and Winogender-style benchmarks are acknowledged proxies (§10.3).
  • Checkpoint-to-checkpoint 1-shot variance is large on some tasks (WebQuestions was SOTA at the 770B-token checkpoint but worse at the reported final one; 62B was evaluated at 795B tokens “due to an oversight”) (§6.8, Fig.17).
  • The “explaining jokes / logical inference” showcase (§9) uses author-written, author-selected examples with no quantitative evaluation.

Critiques and Questions

  1. Is “discontinuous” a property of the model or the metric? The discontinuity statistic is computed on normalized exact-match-style preferred metrics; a smoothly-improving underlying capability can look like a jump when a multi-step task only scores on the final token. The paper does not test this alternative (later literature made this exact argument).
  2. The headline comparisons are compute-unfair in both directions. PaLM beats per-task SOTA from models with 4–70× less training compute; meanwhile its own Fig.24 shows Chinchilla above PaLM’s compute-efficiency curve on BIG-bench. “Scale works” and “this was a compute-suboptimal way to buy the capability” are both true, and only §13 says the second part out loud.
  3. “Above average human” on BIG-bench is a weak bar: crowdworkers of unknown effort, with the model still losing on 35% of tasks and losing badly to best-human (Fig.6–7); several model “wins” are multilingual tasks the raters likely couldn’t read (§6.2).
  4. Davinci Codex comparisons are uncontrolled — unknown size, data, and contamination (§6.4 admits this) — so the code-model rankings are indicative only.
  5. The 46.2% MFU claim depends on their own new metric; HFU (57.8%) is reported but the GPT-3/Gopher/MT-NLG MFU numbers are reconstructed from throughput figures and personal communication, with error bars unstated.
Why did PaLM's authors conclude that both chain-of-thought prompting AND 540B scale were necessary for reasoning SOTA, rather than either alone?
Ablations in Tab.10: PaLM 540B without CoT gets 17% on GSM8K, PaLM 62B with CoT gets 33%, but PaLM 540B with CoT gets 54% (58% with a calculator), beating the finetuned GPT-3+verifier SOTA of 55%. Without both together, PaLM would have reached SOTA on only one of the seven reasoning benchmarks (SVAMP).
