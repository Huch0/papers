The paper that made “IO-aware” part of the LLM vocabulary: it explains why a decade of approximate-attention papers didn’t speed anything up, and its kernel became the default attention implementation underlying long-context training everywhere.

Motivation

Attention is quadratic in sequence length, so the field spent years approximating it — sparse, low-rank, hashed. Almost none of it got adopted, and here is the uncomfortable reason: approximate-attention methods optimize FLOPs, but attention is not compute-bound — on modern GPUs most of its operations are bottlenecked by memory traffic, so reducing FLOPs often buys no wall-clock time (§1). Concretely, an A100 moves data from HBM at ~1.5–2 TB/s but from SRAM an order of magnitude faster (§2.1), and the standard implementation writes the N×N matrices S = QKᵀ and P = softmax(S) out to HBM and reads them back — for GPT-2, N = 1024 against head dimension d = 64, so this traffic dwarfs the inputs. Deep-learning frameworks expose no fine-grained control over memory movement, and naive kernel fusion doesn’t help training because intermediates still get written to HBM for the backward pass (§1, §2.1).

Contribution (what’s new)

Treat attention as an IO problem: the object to minimize is reads/writes between HBM and SRAM, not FLOPs. Two classical techniques make exact attention sub-quadratic in HBM accesses: tiling with an online softmax (the softmax couples whole rows, so you carry running max/normalizer statistics and rescale partial outputs block by block), and backward-pass recomputation from those saved statistics instead of storing S and P. The counterintuitive punchline is forced by the memory-bound regime: recomputation adds FLOPs yet speeds up the backward pass, because regenerating the attention matrix on-chip is cheaper than reading it from HBM (§3.1, Fig.2). The paper also proves this is essentially the end of the line: no exact-attention algorithm can asymptotically beat Θ(N²d²/M) HBM accesses across all SRAM sizes M (Prop.3).

Research questions

  1. Is HBM traffic — not FLOPs — the controlling variable for attention runtime, and can exact attention be computed with asymptotically fewer HBM accesses (§3.1–3.2)?
  2. What does the IO saving buy end-to-end: training wall-clock, feasible context length, and downstream quality (§4.1–4.2)?
  3. Can IO-awareness rescue approximate attention — does block-sparsity on top of the same kernel finally beat dense speed (§3.3)?

Methodology

Never materialize the N×N matrix. The kernel loops over blocks of K and V (outer loop), loads them into SRAM, then streams blocks of Q (inner loop); each Q-block/K-block pair produces a small score tile entirely on-chip, and the numerically-stable softmax is assembled incrementally by tracking, per row, the running maximum m(x)m(x)m(x) and normalizer ℓ(x)\ell(x)ℓ(x) and rescaling the partial output before accumulation (§3.1, Alg.1). All of attention — matmul, masking, softmax, dropout, matmul — is fused into one CUDA kernel. For the backward pass, only O and the statistics (m,ℓ)(m, \ell)(m,ℓ) (plus the RNG state for dropout) are saved; S and P are recomputed on-chip per block — selective gradient checkpointing that is faster, not slower (§3.1, App.B). Block-sparse FlashAttention is the identical kernel that skips zero blocks of a fixed block mask (butterfly pattern in the experiments) (§3.3).

Main result

The IO analysis cashes out at every level of the stack. BERT-large trains 15% faster than Nvidia’s MLPerf 1.1 record submission (17.4 ± 1.4 vs 20.0 ± 1.5 min to target accuracy) (Tab.1); GPT-2 small trains 3.5× faster than HuggingFace and 1.7× faster than Megatron-LM at identical perplexity (18.2) (Tab.2); on Long-Range Arena, 2.4× speedup over standard attention at equal-or-better accuracy, and block-sparse FlashAttention (2.8×) is faster than every approximate-attention baseline tested (Tab.3).

Key findings

  • HBM access volume, not FLOPs, determines attention runtime (Fig.2, §3.2) — the paper’s central empirical claim, demonstrated by winning on wall-clock while losing on FLOP count. This is the diagnosis of why approximate attention failed to deliver speedups.
  • Longer context is now a free quality lever (Tab.4, §4.2): GPT-2 small with a 4× longer context (4K) still trains 30% faster than Megatron at 1K and reaches 0.7 better perplexity (17.5 vs 18.2).
  • Long context converts into capability, not just perplexity (Tab.5–6, §4.2): +4.3 micro-F1 on MIMIC-III (16K vs 512) and +8.5 on ECtHR (8K vs 512) document classification, and the first better-than-chance Transformer on Path-X (61.4% at 16K) and, via block-sparsity, Path-256 (63.1% at 64K) — tasks where every prior Transformer OOMed or sat at random.
  • Crossover map vs approximate attention (Fig.3, §4.3): FlashAttention beats even approximate methods below sequence length ~512–1024 (fewer memory accesses win outright); beyond that some (e.g. Linformer) get faster — but block-sparse FlashAttention stays fastest at all lengths, and memory is linear (20× less than exact baselines; everything but Linformer OOMs before 64K).
  • Speedup is a function of the memory hierarchy, as the theory predicts (App.E.5): 2–4× on A100 (more with dropout/masking fused in), 2.5–4.5× on RTX 3090 (lower HBM bandwidth → bigger win), less on T4 (smaller SRAM → smaller blocks), and reduced at head dimension 128 (blocks must shrink to fit SRAM) though a causal mask restores ~3×.
  • Exactness costs nothing asymptotic (§3.2): the matching lower bound (Prop.3) says the algorithm is IO-optimal for exact attention over the SRAM-size range — further gains must come from approximation (block-sparsity) or hardware.

Implications

The paper relocates the attention-efficiency question from “which approximation?” to “which memory hierarchy?”, importing the IO-complexity tradition (Aggarwal & Vitter 1988), the roofline model, and Halide-style separation of algorithm from schedule into deep learning. It is the reason later long-context training exists at practical cost, and its stated limitations are a roadmap that the field then followed: compiling high-level attention variants to IO-aware kernels instead of hand-writing CUDA (the Triton/torch.compile direction), IO-aware treatment of the rest of the network beyond attention, and multi-GPU IO analysis where inter-GPU transfer joins the hierarchy (the direction sequence parallelism took) (§5). Block-sparse FlashAttention also repositions sparse/structured-matrix methods (butterfly patterns, the “hardware lottery” critique): approximation finally pays off once the dense baseline is IO-optimal. It also competes with the S4/state-space line, which was winning Path-X by replacing attention — FlashAttention shows a plain Transformer can get there too.

Limitations

  • Every new attention variant requires hand-writing a new CUDA kernel — low-level, engineering-heavy, and not portable across GPU generations (§5).
  • IO-optimality is single-GPU; the analysis ignores inter-GPU transfer, which matters exactly in the large-scale regime the method targets (§5).
  • Speedup degrades where the analysis says it must: small SRAM (T4) and large head dimension (128) force smaller blocks (App.E.5).
  • Beyond ~1K tokens some approximate methods are faster than dense FlashAttention; the all-lengths win requires the block-sparse (approximate) variant (§4.3).
  • Path-X is solved only via transfer (pretrain on Path-64, interpolate positional embeddings), and 61.4% is modest — S4-class models later exceeded it (Tab.6, §4.2).

Critiques and Questions

  1. The quality gains from long context (Tab.5) are a claim about context, but the paper itself flags an alternative reading — MIMIC’s dip at 1K–2K suggests length-distribution shift effects, and no controlled study separates “more tokens seen per document” from “better length generalization”.
  2. The lower bound is over a range of SRAM sizes; a parameterized bound for fixed M is explicitly left open (§3.2) — “IO-optimal” is slightly weaker than it sounds.
  3. Speedups are reported against reference implementations (PyTorch, HuggingFace, Megatron, and reference approximate-attention code); against Nvidia’s hand-tuned FMHA fused kernel the margin is thin (−4% to +8% across 128–512, App.E.4) — the asymptotic story matters most at lengths those fused kernels didn’t support.
  4. Adoption pressure cuts the other way too: baking dense attention this deep into hardware-efficient kernels raises the bar any architectural alternative (S4, linear attention) must clear — the hardware-lottery effect the authors cite (App.A).
Why does the backward pass get faster even though FlashAttention recomputes the attention matrix, doing strictly more FLOPs?
Because the backward pass is memory-bound. The standard approach reads the stored N-by-N matrices S and P from HBM at Θ(Nd + N²) accesses; FlashAttention instead stores only O and the per-row softmax statistics (m, ℓ) and regenerates S and P block-by-block in SRAM, cutting HBM accesses to Θ(N²d²/M). Since HBM traffic, not arithmetic, determines runtime (Fig.2: more GFLOPs, 9× less traffic, 5.7× faster), trading FLOPs for IO is a net win.
