Curated byChiyeong Heo

BLOOM is a 176B-parameter open-access causal LM trained by the ~1200-participant BigScience collaboration on ROOTS — a documented, consent-conscious 1.61TB corpus spanning 46 natural and 13 programming languages — on a French public supercomputer; it is competitive with same-generation LLMs, and multitask finetuning (BLOOMZ) unlocks its multilingual zero-shot ability.

The counterpoint to OPT on the open-LLM milestone path: not a replication of GPT-3 but a redesign of every component — data governance, tokenizer, architecture ablations, license — around multilinguality and collective governance, proving a state-scale model can be built outside a corporate lab.

Motivation

Every 100B+ language model in 2022 belonged to a resource-rich lab, and it showed in three ways. The research community was locked out of studying LLMs — most were unreleased, and only EleutherAI, outside China, was building them non-corporately (§1, §2.1). The models were overwhelmingly English, because corpora are assembled by cheap heuristic filtering over crawls — a practice that also silently suppresses marginalized voices (block lists removing LGBTQ+ and AAE text) and destroys provenance, making the data ungovernable after the fact (§3.1). And a handful of industrial actors were de facto writing the regulations of a technology that shapes everyone’s lives, without consulting them (§2.1). The question is whether hundreds of loosely-organized researchers, on public compute, can build a comparable model differently — and document how.

Contribution (what’s new)

Multilinguality and accountability are treated as design constraints that propagate through every component, not evaluation afterthoughts: language communities (3+ fluent speakers) decide inclusion and set per-language filter thresholds; the tokenizer is validated by per-language fertility rather than defaults; architecture choices come from published zero-shot ablations; the license (RAIL) encodes 13 behavioral-use restrictions. Second, the choices are backed by dedicated studies rather than convention — causal decoder-only wins the architecture/objective ablation (Wang et al. 2022a), ALiBi beats learned and rotary embeddings at the original sequence length (Le Scao et al. 2022) — and third, xP3 multitask finetuning shows that multilingual prompted finetuning, not just English P3, is what converts a multilingual base model into a zero-shot multilingual task-solver (BLOOMZ) (§3.1.4, §4.7).

Research questions

  1. Can a large open collaboration on public compute produce a GPT-3-class model, and what organizational/engineering form does that take (§2.2, §3.4)?
  2. What does responsible data work look like at LLM scale — governance, consent, documentation, per-language curation (§3.1)?
  3. How does a deliberately multilingual 176B model behave — on English benchmarks, translation, summarization, code, bias — and what does multitask finetuning add (§4)?

Methodology

Follow one design constraint — “59 languages” — through the stack. Data: the ROOTS corpus composes 498 datasets: a community-built catalogue (252 sources, at least 21 per language category, assembled in hackathons with Masakhane, Machine Learning Tokyo, LatinX in AI), pseudocrawled locally-relevant websites, GitHub code, and an OSCAR CommonCrawl slice capped at 38% of the corpus; “quality” is defined as text written by humans for humans, with filter thresholds set per language by fluent speakers, then deduplication and PII redaction (§3.1.2–3.1.3). Tokenizer: byte-level BPE with a 250,680-item vocabulary, validated by fertility — at most ~10 points worse than each language’s monolingual tokenizer (Tab.2) — no text normalization and no English-centric splits (§3.3). Architecture: a causal decoder-only Transformer (the ablation winner for zero-shot generalization), with ALiBi and an embedding LayerNorm adopted for training stability (§3.2). Training: 366B tokens on Jean Zay (384 A100s, 3.5 months, Megatron-DeepSpeed 3D parallelism + ZeRO-1, bfloat16). Evaluation: zero/one-shot with prompts written by humans before the model existed, no model-in-the-loop prompt tuning — deliberately measuring what a new user gets, not a best case (§4.1.1).

Model, training, and carbon details
  • Scale suite: 560M / 1.1B / 1.7B / 3B / 7.1B / 176B. BLOOM-176B: 70 layers, 112 heads, hidden 14336, sequence 2048, GELU, tied embeddings; LR 6e-5, batch 2048 sequences, cosine decay scheduled over 410B tokens (never completed); ROOTS is ~341B tokens, extended by 25B repeated tokens after the Chinchilla scaling results appeared mid-training (Tab.3, §3.5).
  • Stability: preliminary 104B runs in float16 diverged irreversibly; the final bfloat16 mixed-precision training with embedding LayerNorm had exactly one loss spike, which recovered on its own. Hardware attrition was 1–2 GPU failures/week with 3-hourly checkpoints — markedly less human intervention than OPT’s run (§3.4.3–3.4.5). Embedding LayerNorm measurably hurts zero-shot generalization and was adopted anyway as divergence insurance; the authors note bfloat16 may have made it unnecessary (§3.2.3).
  • Throughput: up to 156 TFLOPs/GPU — half of A100 theoretical peak — via fused CUDA kernels (fusing bias-add into GeLU is free because the op is memory-bound) (§3.4.2, §3.4.4).
  • BLOOMZ: finetune on xP3 (prompts for 83 datasets, 46 languages, 16 tasks, mirroring ROOTS’ language distribution) for 13B tokens; performance plateaus after 1–6B. xP3mt machine-translates the prompts themselves (§3.1.4, §3.5).
  • Carbon, life-cycle style (§3.5.1): ~81 t CO2eq total = 11 t equipment manufacturing (14%) + 25 t training energy (30%) + 45 t idle cluster consumption (55%). BLOOM used more energy than OPT (433 vs 324 MWh) but emitted ~1/3 as much thanks to France’s 57 gCO2eq/kWh nuclear-heavy grid vs 231 for OPT (Tab.4). Final training is only 37% of overall project emissions; the live API emits ~19–20 kg CO2eq/day on 16 GPUs.
  • Data governance: explicit permissions sought where possible (S2ORC, Le Monde); sources kept separate until final preprocessing for traceability; composite release — 223 of the 498 components directly distributable (§3.1.1).

Main result

BLOOM is competitive with same-generation models while being the only one of them that is massively multilingual. On SuperGLUE one-shot, BLOOM-176B beats OPT-175B on Ax-b, CB, WSC, and WiC and matches it elsewhere — multilinguality does not tax English performance (Fig.7–8, §4.2); on HELM, BLOOM’s accuracy is on par with GPT-3 davinci v1 and J1-Grande, behind InstructGPT davinci v2 and OPT, while ranking among the best models for fairness (Fig.10, §4.6). In one-shot machine translation it approaches or beats a supervised translation model on well-resourced pairs:

And the multitask-finetuning result: pretrained BLOOM sits near random on multilingual held-out tasks (XNLI, XWinograd, XCOPA, XStoryCloze), and finetuning on xP3 (BLOOMZ) lifts it far above — while English-only T0 fails on the same multilingual tasks (Fig.11, §4.7).

Key findings

  • Cross-lingual competence follows representation and relatedness, not language lists (Tab.8, §4.3.3): translation between Romance languages is strong even into Galician, which is not in the training data, while Swahili↔Yoruba — included but with under 50k tokens each — collapses to near-zero BLEU. Being “in the corpus” is not the operative variable; data mass and neighbors are.
  • Zero-shot translation fails for mundane reasons — over-generation and answering in the wrong language — and one shot largely fixes both (Tab.6, §4.3.1); prompt wording swings WMT BLEU by 3× (verbose “version-target” 21.96 vs “gpt3-target” 7.90 zero-shot en→fr). BLOOM also gains more than OPT from zero→one shot on SuperGLUE, plausibly because one example disambiguates the expected language (§4.2).
  • On multilingual summarization (WikiLingua, source→source language), BLOOM beats OPT-175B at every size tested (Fig.9, §4.4) — the clearest direct payoff of multilingual pretraining.
  • Code: BLOOM pass@1 15.52% on HumanEval, essentially GPT-NeoX-20B level (15.4%), far below Codex-12B (28.81%); BLOOMZ does not improve at code (Tab.9, §4.5) — xP3 contains code-related tasks but almost no pure code completion, a clean demonstration that instruction tuning only transfers what the mixture contains.
  • Probing surprise: BLOOM-1.7B encodes morphosyntax on par with or better than BLOOM-176B (significant by Mann-Whitney U) (Tab.12–13, §4.9), but the small model’s performance is tightly correlated with per-language pretraining data size while the 176B model is more uniform across languages — scale seems to buy evenness of generalization, not more grammar.
  • On multilingual CrowS-Pairs (English + French), overall accuracy sits at ~0.50 with small significant deviations (religion highest at ~53.8) (Fig.13, Tab.14, §4.10) — homogeneous across bias categories, unlike masked-LM studies; the authors carefully flag CrowS-Pairs validity limits and the near-absence of multilingual bias tooling.

Implications

BLOOM demonstrates a second, non-corporate path to frontier-scale models: public compute (a GENCI grant on Jean Zay), community data work, and openly published design studies in place of proprietary lore. Its components outlived the model: ROOTS-style documented data curation, PromptSource/xP3, the prompted evaluation harness, and the RAIL license family, which became the template for use-restricted open releases. It sits in dialogue with OPT (the GPT-3 replication with a request-gated license) and GPT-NeoX, and downstream with mT0/BLOOMZ’s multitask-finetuning line (Muennighoff et al. 2022) and the T0/FLAN instruction-tuning lineage. Worth chasing: whether under-represented “included” languages (Swahili, Yoruba) can be rescued by continued pretraining or targeted finetuning rather than corpus growth, the probing result that scale evens out cross-language generalization, and life-cycle (manufacturing + idle + inference) carbon accounting as standard reporting (§3.5.1).

Limitations

  • Trained for 366B tokens — far under Chinchilla-optimal for 176B; the schedule itself was cut short of its 410B-token decay horizon, and the mid-training +25B patch acknowledges the budget was set pre-Chinchilla (§3.5).
  • Under-represented included languages perform very poorly (Swahili/Yoruba MT near zero; lowest probing scores for Bengali, Wolof, Yoruba) — inclusion without volume did not deliver capability (§4.3.3, §4.9).
  • Zero-shot performance of the base model hovers near chance on many prompted tasks; usable zero-shot behavior requires the BLOOMZ finetuning (§4.2, §4.7).
  • Bias evaluation covers only English and French CrowS-Pairs, with acknowledged construct-validity issues and no multilingual coverage of the other 44 languages (§4.10).
  • By HELM’s measures BLOOM is poorly calibrated and slightly more toxic than average in English (§4.6).
  • ROOTS is only partially distributable (223/498 components), so full training-data reproducibility is gated (§3.1.1).

Critiques and Questions

  1. The headline “competitive performance” is generation-relative: by release (and the v4 revision), Chinchilla-style compute-optimal training and instruction-tuned closed models had moved the frontier; BLOOM’s real contributions are the process artifacts, and the paper is candid about this framing.
  2. The evaluation philosophy (human-written prompts, no model-in-the-loop refinement) is principled but conflates model capability with prompt luck — the 3× BLEU spread across prompts shows how much variance the choice absorbs; reporting both realistic and best-prompt numbers would separate the two.
  3. The probing conclusion that 1.7B ≥ 176B on morphosyntax is provocative but rests on layer-averaged linear probes of a single pooled token; it may measure representational accessibility rather than knowledge, and the paper’s own discussion lists this as open.
  4. Is the RAIL license “open”? Its 13 behavioral restrictions are a governance experiment; the paper does not evaluate enforceability or downstream chilling effects, which later open-source debates made central.
Why did BLOOM emit only about a third of OPT's training CO2eq despite consuming more energy?
Grid carbon intensity, not efficiency: BLOOM used 433 MWh vs OPT's 324 MWh, but Jean Zay draws on France's nuclear-heavy grid at 57 gCO2eq/kWh versus 231 for OPT's grid — 25 vs 70 tons. The paper's life-cycle accounting also shows training energy is only 30% of BLOOM's own ~81-ton total; idle cluster consumption (55%) and equipment manufacturing (14%) dominate the rest (Tab.4, §3.5.1).
