OpenAssistant Conversations (OASST1) is a fully open, human-written and human-ranked assistant-conversation corpus — 161k messages in 35 languages with 461k quality ratings across 10k+ complete conversation trees — produced by 13,500+ volunteers through a gamified, peer-reviewed crowd pipeline, making the whole InstructGPT recipe (SFT → RM → RLHF) replicable outside corporate labs.

This is the open-source counterpart to Llama 2’s proprietary 1.4M preference comparisons: the first at-scale proof that volunteers can produce alignment data, and a staple ingredient of the 2023 open chat wave (Guanaco, the Falcon instruct mixes, countless SFT recipes). Its off-policy-reward-model lesson is a foundation for understanding why on-policy preference data matters.

Motivation

The alignment step is what turned LLMs into products (ChatGPT), yet the human feedback data that powers SFT and RLHF is expensive to create and remains proprietary — the decisive ingredient is exactly the one the open community cannot get (§1). The open substitutes are inadequate: synthetic instruction sets (self-instruct, Alpaca, GPT4All) are bounded in complexity, creativity, and quality by their template lists and by the model that generated them, and even Vicuna pairs human prompts with model-written responses (§1). The result is a monopoly: alignment research — arguably the field’s most consequential problem — confined to a few labs with annotation budgets.

Contribution (what’s new)

The dataset, and just as importantly the machinery that made it possible: a crowd-sourcing design that atomizes conversation-building into single-step tasks (write a prompt, reply as assistant, reply as prompter, label, rank) over conversation trees, so thousands of anonymous volunteers can jointly author both sides of multi-turn dialogues without attrition destroying partial work (§2–3). Quality control is structural, not aspirational: every message passes peer review (3 reviewers, acceptance threshold 0.6), rankings come from 3 independent rankers fused by Tideman’s ranked-pairs voting, turning noisy individual preferences into consensus ranks usable for reward modeling (App.B, App.G), and a points/leaderboard economy plus a “Trollboard” concentrates scarce moderator attention on likely abusers (§3.4). Because trees carry multiple ranked assistant replies per prompt, one corpus serves SFT, reward modeling, and RLHF at once. Everything — data, collection platform, trained SFT/RM/RLHF models — ships under permissive licenses.

Research questions

  1. Can unpaid, worldwide crowd-sourcing produce assistant-conversation and preference data of sufficient quality and scale to substitute for proprietary vendor annotation? (§1, §3)
  2. Do models fine-tuned on this data measurably improve over their base models — and how close do they get to ChatGPT? (§6.1)
  3. Does community moderation plus peer review actually keep spam and toxicity out? (§3.4, §6.2)

Methodology

A web platform advances thousands of conversation trees one step at a time through a state machine (initial-prompt review → growing → end; spam-aborted or moderator-halted otherwise), with a prompt lottery throttling the most popular task and target shapes of depth ≤ 5, ≤ 2 children per node, ~9 messages per tree (§3.2, App.G). Guidelines steer contributors toward informed-consent-style safety — warn and contextualize rather than blanket-refuse — and explicitly ban copy-pasting from ChatGPT (§3.3, App.A). Validation trains Pythia/LLaMA/Falcon SFT models, reward models, and PPO-based RLHF models on the corpus (§6.1, App.H).

Method details — task types, preference fusion, training setup

Task decomposition (§3.1): five task types, assigned by choice or weighted sampling. Labels: binary flags (spam, language mismatch, PII, hate speech, sexual content, not appropriate) route to moderators; five-point Likert scales (quality, creativity, humor, politeness/rudeness, harmlessness/violence, helpfulness) are stored as metadata. Points are partly deferred — an assistant reply earns more if others later rank it highly — aligning contributor incentives with downstream quality.

Preference fusion (App.B): each ranking task collects K independent rankers; pairwise preference counts are sorted by strength and added as edges to a directed graph, skipping any edge that would create a cycle (ranked pairs / Tideman); peeling source nodes off the resulting DAG yields the consensus rank stored per assistant message.

Filtering ChatGPT leakage (App.D): guideline bans, user bans with contribution deletion, automatic phrase searches (“as a large language model”, “knowledge cutoff…”), and community down-votes — acknowledged as incomplete.

Training (App.H): threads serialized with <prompter_token>/<assistant_token> markers; SFT loss masked to assistant tokens only. RM replaces the LM head with a scalar head and trains on all (K2)\binom{K}{2}(2K​) pairwise comparisons with the InstructGPT sigmoid ranking loss. PPO (trlx) adds a per-token KL penalty against the SFT policy. The sft-mix variant blends OASST with Vicuna, code_alpaca, Dolly-15k, GSM instructions, and wiki-QA data.

Main result

Models fine-tuned on OASST1 consistently beat their base models on the lm-eval-harness NLU average — Falcon-40B rises from 72.29 to 74.40, and an OASST SFT of LLaMA-30B (68.03) outscores the LLaMA-65B base (67.24) (Tab.1, §6.1) — while instruction-style evaluations tell a split story: competitive with ChatGPT on LLM-judged Elo, far behind on OpenAI Evals and code.

Key findings

  • Benchmark rankings are mutually inconsistent — the OASST Falcon-40B outranks ChatGPT on Vicuna Elo (1192 vs 1110) yet scores 0.26 vs 0.87 on OpenAI Evals (Tab.1, §6.1); the paper reads this as evidence that automatic chat evaluation was unreliable, and that data mixture choice (top-ranked-only vs mixed) visibly steers what kind of model you get.
  • RLHF does not uniformly beat SFT (68.51 vs 68.03 LMEH; Elo 1068 vs 979 but OAIE 0.51 vs 0.52), and the authors’ hypothesis is precise: their reward model was trained on rankings of human-written replies, not of the SFT model’s own samples as in InstructGPT (§7) — an early, honest articulation of the off-policy preference-data problem.
  • Community moderation demonstrably filters toxicity: deleted messages score 4.7x higher on Detoxify (4.625% vs 0.988% avg toxicity), yet absolute toxicity is low in both groups (Tab.2, §6.2) — most deletions target factual errors, grammar, or cascade from deleted parents, so automated toxicity scoring alone could not replace the human pipeline.
  • The corpus has documented skews: English 42.8% + Spanish 31.4% dominate; a small set of power users contributes an outsized share; survey respondents are 89.1% male with median age 26 (Fig.2, §4, §7) — the values encoded in the data come from a specific, self-selected population.
  • Preliminary political-compass probes show the OASST-trained model more balanced and varied in its leanings than ChatGPT (App.J, Fig.12) — weak evidence, flagged as such, that decentralized data collection diversifies model values.
  • The tree economics are lopsided: of 66,497 trees, 52,159 never left the single-prompt lottery; the 10,968 completed trees carry 92,365 of the messages (§4, App.G) — prompt-writing enthusiasm vastly outstrips the labor-intensive assistant-reply supply.

Implications

OASST1 turned alignment from a budget question into a coordination question: it fed Guanaco and the era’s open SFT mixes, its ranked sibling replies became free reward-model and (later) DPO training pairs, and its conversation-tree format is now a standard shape for dialogue corpora. It is the community answer to InstructGPT’s recipe and Anthropic’s HH data, the human-authored alternative to the self-instruct/Alpaca/Vicuna synthetic line, and the open twin of Llama 2’s contemporaneous closed 1.4M-comparison effort — which independently confirmed the on-distribution preference-data lesson OASST learned the hard way. Collecting rankings over the SFT model’s own outputs (the authors’ stated plan), and whether volunteer pipelines can sustain the data refresh cadence that iterative RLHF demands.

Limitations

  • Reward models were trained on rankings of human-written messages rather than model samples, which the authors blame for RLHF’s non-uniform gains over SFT (§7).
  • Demographic and contribution-frequency biases are acknowledged and only partially measurable: 89.1% male respondents, median age 26, power-user over-representation, and a 270-response Discord-recruited survey that oversamples engaged contributors (§5, §7).
  • Residual unsafe content and ChatGPT-generated text may survive the filters; the authors recommend the released models for academic research only and note susceptibility to prompt injection (§7, App.D).
  • No human evaluation of the trained models is performed by the paper itself — assistant quality claims beyond Table 1 rest on anecdotal user reports (§6.1).
  • Language coverage is nominally 35 but effectively two: English + Spanish are ~74% of messages (§4).

Critiques and Questions

  1. “Consistent improvements on standard benchmarks” is the weakest possible headline for an alignment dataset: LMEH is an NLU suite, not an assistant-quality measure, and the instruction-side evidence (Elo vs OpenAI Evals) is contradictory. For a paper about human data, the absence of a controlled human evaluation is a real gap.
  2. The Vicuna Elo win over ChatGPT (1192 vs 1110) is reported without interrogating LLM-judge bias (style, verbosity, format preferences) — later work showed these judges are gameable, so this number should not be quoted as a capability claim.
  3. “Human-generated” is an approximation: keyword filters catch verbatim ChatGPT boilerplate, not paraphrased machine text, and the paper concedes some automated contributions likely slipped through — an early instance of the AI-contamination problem now endemic to crowd data.
  4. Quality control measures adherence to guidelines, not correctness: three volunteer reviewers at threshold 0.6 filter spam well (Tab.2) but cannot verify factual accuracy at scale, and the guideline “rank don’t-know above wrong” depends on reviewers detecting wrongness in the first place.
  5. Aging: as on-policy preference collection and DPO-style pipelines became standard, static off-policy human rankings lost centrality — the dataset’s durable contributions are the SFT conversations and the collection playbook, more than the preference data it was named for.
Why did the authors' RLHF models fail to show the uniform gains over SFT that InstructGPT reported, by their own diagnosis?
Their reward model was trained on rankings of human-written replies collected for general quality control, whereas InstructGPT ranked samples drawn from its own SFT model. The RM therefore judged a distribution different from the policy's actual outputs — off-policy preference data — so PPO optimized a misaligned reward signal (§7). Their stated fix: collect rankings over their own SFT models' outputs.
