Llama 2 pairs a stronger open base model (2T tokens, 4k context, GQA, commercial license) with the first fully documented industrial RLHF pipeline — quality-first SFT, two reward models, five iterations of rejection sampling + PPO, and Ghost Attention — yielding a chat model that human raters judge on par with ChatGPT on helpfulness and safety.

This is the paper that made open-weight aligned models real: before it, open bases existed but the product-grade RLHF recipe was a trade secret. Every open post-training pipeline since (and most of what I know about reward modeling in practice) descends from what this paper chose to write down.

Motivation

Open pretrained models had caught up; open assistants had not. BLOOM, LLaMA-1, and Falcon match GPT-3-class base models, but none is a substitute for the closed “product” LLMs — ChatGPT, Bard, Claude — because those are heavily fine-tuned to human preferences, and that alignment step is costly in compute and annotation and is neither transparent nor reproducible, which blocks the community from doing alignment research at all (§1). Distillation-style imitations (Alpaca, Vicuna) narrow the gap superficially but fall short of the closed bar (§6). The missing artifact was not weights — it was the recipe.

Contribution (what’s new)

The core contribution is the recipe itself, published end-to-end with its ablations: iterative RLHF where preference data, reward models, and policy improve together in weekly cycles, so the reward model stays on-distribution for the policy it must judge. The form is forced: a static reward model goes stale as the policy improves (its accuracy degrades off-distribution), so annotation must be interleaved with training, not collected once. Around this core sit deliberate design choices, each justified by a failure it prevents: two separate reward models (helpfulness, safety), because the two objectives trade off and confuse a single model; rejection-sampling fine-tuning for breadth plus PPO for depth; and Ghost Attention (GAtt), a data-side trick that stops multi-turn system-instruction forgetting (§3). Plus the released artifacts: Llama 2 base (7B/13B/70B; 2T tokens, 4k context, GQA) and Llama 2-Chat under a commercial license (§1, Tab.1).

Research questions

  1. Can an open, documented pipeline reproduce the helpfulness and safety of closed product LLMs — and what does it actually take (data volumes, reward-model quality, number of iterations)? (§1, §3)
  2. Is RLHF a costly add-on or the essential ingredient — what does preference optimization do that supervised fine-tuning cannot? (§3.1, §5.1)
  3. Can safety be improved aggressively without taxing helpfulness? (§4.2)

Methodology

Pretrain a Llama-1-style transformer, longer and wider in the ways that matter for serving (2T tokens, doubled 4k context, GQA at 34B/70B — each ablated in App.A.2.1). Then align in three stages (Fig.4): SFT on a small curated set, then iterative RLHF (V1→V5) driven by two reward models trained on ~1.4M in-house preference comparisons, mixing rejection-sampling fine-tuning with PPO. Safety gets its own parallel track: safety SFT, a safety reward model, and targeted context distillation, audited by 350+ red-teamers (§4).

Method details — SFT, reward models, RLHF iterations, GAtt, ablations

SFT (§3.1): bootstrap from public instruction data, then replace scale with curation: 27,540 vendor-annotated prompt+response pairs. Cosine LR 2e−5, 2 epochs, 4096 sequence length, loss only on answer tokens.

Preference data (§3.2.1, Tab.6): annotators write a prompt, pick the better of two responses sampled from different model variants/temperatures, and grade the gap on a 4-point scale (significantly better → negligibly better). Helpfulness and safety are collected under separate guidelines; safety pairs also get a 3-way label (safe-vs-unsafe 18%, both safe 47%, both unsafe 35%). 1,418,091 Meta comparisons (longer and more multi-turn than any open preference set), plus ~1.5M open-source comparisons.

Reward models (§3.2.2): initialized from the chat model checkpoint so the RM “knows what the chat model knows” (prevents rewarding hallucinations it cannot detect), with the LM head swapped for a scalar head. Loss = binary ranking with a rating-dependent margin:

Lranking=−log⁡(σ(rθ(x,yc)−rθ(x,yr)−m(r)))\mathcal{L}_{\text{ranking}} = -\log\Big(\sigma\big(r_\theta(x, y_c) - r_\theta(x, y_r) - m(r)\big)\Big)Lranking​=−log(σ(rθ​(x,yc​)−rθ​(x,yr​)−m(r)))

where m(r)m(r)m(r) grows with how separable the pair is — this margin measurably improves helpfulness-RM accuracy on distinct pairs (Tab.28, App.A.3.3). One epoch only (more overfits). Helpfulness RM trains on all Meta Helpfulness + balanced Safety/open-source data; Safety RM on all Meta Safety + Anthropic Harmless with 10% helpfulness data, which specifically helps accuracy on both-responses-safe pairs.

RLHF (§3.2.3): five iterations. Rejection sampling (70B only; smaller models are fine-tuned on the 70B’s selected samples — a distillation): sample K responses per prompt, fine-tune on the argmax-reward one. PPO on top from V4 onward, with the piecewise reward — safety RM score if the prompt is safety-tagged or the safety score falls below 0.15 (precision 0.89 / recall 0.55), else helpfulness RM — whitened via logit, plus a KL penalty (β=0.01\beta = 0.01β=0.01 at 7B/13B, 0.005 at 34B/70B) to prevent reward hacking. A found-the-hard-way lesson: restricting rejection sampling to the latest model’s samples caused capability regressions by V3 (e.g., rhyming), fixed by pooling top samples from all prior iterations.

GAtt (§3.3): to stop system instructions (“act as Napoleon”, “answer in haiku”) being forgotten after a few turns, synthetically append the instruction to every user turn of a dialogue, sample responses, then train with the instruction kept only in the first turn — zeroing the loss on all earlier turns to avoid a train/inference mismatch. Attention maps show the system message stays attended for many more turns (Fig.10); control holds beyond 20 turns (App.A.3.5).

Architecture ablations (App.A.2.1): at fixed 150B tokens, 4k context beats 2k on long-input tasks (SCROLLS; NarrativeQA F1 0.21 → 17.26) with no loss on standard tasks (Tab.16–17); GQA matches MHA quality while MQA trails on average (Tab.18), and GQA/MQA sustain much larger inference batch sizes (MHA hits OOM where they run, Fig.24) — hence GQA for 34B/70B.

Main result

On human evaluation over ~4,000 helpfulness prompts with 3 raters each, Llama 2-Chat 70B is competitive with ChatGPT (36% win / 31.5% tie), beats PaLM-bison by a large margin, and the 34B model wins more than 75% against Vicuna-33B and Falcon-40B (Fig.12, §3.4.2). On safety, Llama 2-Chat shows the lowest violation rates across sizes on ~2,000 adversarial prompts, single- and multi-turn (Fig.17–18, §4.4). The base model leads all open models on academic benchmarks — Llama 2 70B: MMLU 68.9 (+5 over Llama 1 65B), BBH 51.2 (+8) (Tab.3) — and sits near GPT-3.5 except on code:

Key findings

  • SFT quality beats quantity: setting aside millions of third-party examples for 27,540 curated annotations “notably improved” results — and the SFT model’s own outputs soon rivaled human-written data (§3.1), which is why annotation budget was reallocated from demonstrations to preferences.
  • RLHF surpasses SFT because humans compare better than they write: SFT inherits the tail of bad annotations and is capped by the best annotator’s skill, while preference-driven reward pushes the response distribution past both (§5.1, Fig.20) — the paper’s provocative framing: “supervised data may no longer be the gold standard.”
  • The in-house reward models beat all public RMs and zero-shot GPT-4 at judging Meta preference data (Helpfulness RM 63.2 vs GPT-4 58.6), and RM accuracy scales with data and size without saturating (Tab.7, Fig.6) — treated as the proxy metric: RM gains translate directly into chat-model gains.
  • Safety data scaling is nearly free: raising safety data from 0% to 100% (~0.1M examples) sharply improves the safety score distribution while mean helpfulness stays flat (Fig.15, §4.2.3); the tax shows up only as false refusals — ~0.05% on the helpfulness set but substantial on a 210-prompt borderline set (“Christmas Crack” tripping the word “crack”-adjacent pattern) (App.Fig.33, Tab.41).
  • RLHF dynamically rescales temperature per prompt type: factual prompts converge to the same answer even at high temperature while creative prompts keep diversity (Fig.8, Fig.21, §5.1) — sampling hyperparameters must be re-tuned every RLHF iteration (optimal T rises to ~1.2–1.3).
  • Alignment surfaces latent abilities: zero-shot multi-tool use emerges without any tool-use training (with a calculator: 67.1/69.2/82.4 on ASDiv/SVAMP/MAWPS vs Toolformer’s 40.4/29.4/44.0), and 1,000 date-tagged SFT examples suffice for robust temporal organization of knowledge (Fig.22–23, Tab.15).
  • A token-level skipgram contamination audit (10+-token matches, 4-token slack) finds only HellaSwag and MMLU-Humanities meaningfully boosted by contamination — with the 70B benefiting more than the 7B (App.A.6, Tab.51); other benchmarks show no significant effect.

Implications

The open ecosystem got its alignment textbook: preference-data volumes, RM training mixes, iteration counts, and the failure modes (RM staleness, rejection-sampling forgetting, false refusals) that closed labs had kept internal. It operationalizes the InstructGPT/Anthropic HH line of RLHF work at open scale, supersedes LLaMA-1, and is the direct substrate for the Code Llama / open fine-tune wave; its two-RM safety design anticipates later safety-classifier stacks, and its “humans compare better than they write” argument is the practical case later formalized by DPO-style direct preference methods. Whether the pricey PPO stage is necessary at all (DPO), whether iterative on-distribution annotation can be replaced by AI feedback (RLAIF), and how the false-refusal/borderline trade-off should be measured as a first-class metric.

Limitations

  • English-only in practice: 89.7% of pretraining data is English (Tab.10) and all testing was in English; non-English performance is called “fragile” (§5.2).
  • The helpfulness human eval’s ~4k prompts include no coding or reasoning prompts, and only the final turn of multi-turn dialogues is judged (§3.4.2) — exactly where Tab.4 shows the biggest closed-model gap (HumanEval 29.9 vs GPT-3.5’s 48.1).
  • Safety evaluations use Meta’s own content standards and prompt set, which the paper concedes are “likely biased towards Llama 2-Chat” (Fig.3 caption, §4.4).
  • Safety tuning overshoots at times: over-cautious refusals and safety boilerplate are acknowledged (§5.2), and the 34B model was withheld for lack of red-teaming time (§1).
  • Inter-rater reliability on helpfulness is modest (Gwet’s AC2 0.37–0.55), so close comparisons (vs ChatGPT) are noisy (§3.4.2).

Critiques and Questions

  1. The headline “on par with ChatGPT” rests on a prompt set that excludes the categories where the model is weakest (code, reasoning); the academic tables and the human eval quietly measure different models.
  2. The pipeline is documented but not reproducible: the 1.4M preference comparisons — by the paper’s own logic the decisive asset — are not released, so the community gets the recipe without the key ingredient.
  3. Reward models serve as both training signal and primary evaluator (Fig.11 left); the Goodhart risk is acknowledged and partially hedged with GPT-4-as-judge, but GPT-4 win-rates and human win-rates are never reconciled quantitatively.
  4. There is no controlled comparison of rejection sampling vs PPO vs both — the sequential adoption (RS until V4, then +PPO) confounds algorithm choice with data iteration, so “what did PPO buy” is unanswerable from the paper.
  5. Emergent tool use is demonstrated with an illustrative figure and one calculator benchmark; “never trained on tool use” is hard to audit given 2T undisclosed pretraining tokens.
Why does Llama 2 train two separate reward models, and what keeps them accurate across RLHF iterations?
Helpfulness and safety trade off — one model must both rank quality and detect adversarial prompts, which degrades both skills — so separate RMs each get a cleaner objective (§3.2.2). Accuracy is maintained by iterating: each week's preference data is collected on outputs of the *latest* chat model, because RM accuracy degrades off-distribution as the policy improves (§3.2.1).
