Llama 3 is a herd of dense open-weight Transformers (8B/70B/405B) whose flagship — 405B parameters, 15.6T tokens, 3.8×10253.8\times10^{25}3.8×1025 FLOPs, 128K context — reaches GPT-4-class quality; the paper’s real contribution is the disclosed industrial recipe: data curation, downstream-predictive scaling laws, a 16K-H100 training system with its failure statistics, and a six-round SFT + rejection-sampling + DPO post-training loop.

This is the closest thing to a public blueprint for building a frontier LLM — the infrastructure and training-recipe sections document what every other frontier lab hides (MFU numbers, interruption root-causes, batch ramps, data-mix percentages), and its “managing complexity” thesis (dense over MoE, DPO over PPO) is a deliberate counter-bet to Mixtral/GPT-4-era orthodoxy that the field has been arguing with since.

Motivation

Open-weight models trailed the closed frontier (GPT-4, Claude) by a full generation, and the recipes behind frontier models — data composition, scaling decisions, infrastructure, alignment loops — were undisclosed, so the gap could not even be diagnosed. Internally, our own diagnosis of Llama 2 was that quality was gated not by architecture but by three levers we under-exploited: data quality/quantity (1.8T tokens, lightly curated), scale (the largest Llama 2 used ~50x less compute than we could deploy), and complexity that does not survive scaling — unstable MoE training and finicky RL pipelines burn calendar time that data work would spend better (§1). A further gap: classical scaling laws predict next-token loss, not benchmark accuracy, and are fit on tiny-compute runs — useless for deciding, before a 3.8×10253.8\times10^{25}3.8×1025-FLOP commitment, what the model will actually score (§3.2.1).

Contribution (what’s new)

The demonstration that data + scale + simplicity — a standard dense Transformer with minor tweaks, SFT + rejection sampling + DPO, no MoE, no PPO — closes the open/closed gap, with the full recipe published: exact data-mix fractions, hyperparameters, parallelism configs, and failure logs. Three specific deltas are reusable beyond this model: a two-stage scaling law that maps FLOPs → downstream negative log-likelihood → benchmark accuracy (via a sigmoid fit anchored by Llama 2 checkpoints), accurately forecasting the flagship’s ARC-Challenge score across four orders of magnitude of extrapolation (§3.2.1, Fig.4); a post-training flywheel where each round’s best models generate, filter, and rank the next round’s data (execution feedback for code, stepwise reward models + MCTS for math, model-as-judge everywhere) (§4); and the first frank, quantified reliability account of frontier training (§3.3.4). Simplicity is argued from results, not taste: the dense 405B beats every open MoE, “suggesting dense architectures are not the limiting factor” (§9.1).

Research questions

  1. Do data, scale, and simplicity suffice to match GPT-4-class quality with a dense open model? (§1, §5)
  2. Can benchmark performance be forecast before committing the full training budget? (§3.2.1)
  3. How far does a compositional adapter approach take image/video/speech without touching the language weights? (§7-8)

Methodology

Architecture is deliberately boring (§3.2, Tab.3): a dense decoder with GQA, a 128K-token vocabulary (tiktoken + 28K non-English tokens; English compression improves 3.17 → 3.94 chars/token), RoPE base 500,000, and an attention mask preventing cross-document attention (matters mainly for long-context training). 405B = 126 layers, dim 16,384, 128 heads. The recipe carries the interesting decisions:

  • Pre-training (§3.1, §3.4): ~15T tokens; the final mix is 50% general knowledge, 25% math/reasoning, 17% code, 8% multilingual, chosen by scaling-law experiments over candidate mixes. AdamW, peak LR 8×10−58\times10^{-5}8×10−5, batch ramped 4M → 8M → 16M tokens for stability; the mix itself was adjusted mid-run (more non-English, more recent web data). Then six staged context extensions 8K → 128K (800B tokens), each gated on short-context recovery plus perfect needle-in-a-haystack; finally annealing over the last 40M tokens with Polyak checkpoint averaging.
  • Post-training (§4): six rounds of reward modeling → rejection sampling (K=10-30 samples, best-of picked by the RM) → SFT → DPO (β=0.1), with model averaging across data/hyperparameter variants at each stage. Two stabilizing DPO tweaks: mask the chat-format special tokens out of the loss, and add an NLL term (0.2) on chosen responses.
  • Multimodal (§7-8, experimental, unreleased): keep the LLM frozen; attach a ViT image encoder via cross-attention every 4th layer (the 405B adapter alone is ~100B params), a perceiver-resampler video aggregator over 64 frames, and a 1B-param Conformer speech encoder whose adapter emits token embeddings directly.
The infrastructure and data details (the sections worth studying)

Compute fabric (§3.3). Up to 16K H100s on Meta production clusters; storage is a 240PB SSD Tectonic fabric absorbing bursty checkpoints at 2-7 TB/s. The 24K-GPU RoCE cluster is a three-layer Clos: 3,072-GPU pods with full bisection bandwidth, but 1:7 oversubscription between pods — so parallelism placement is network-aware. Load balancing abandons plain ECMP: the collective library splits each GPU pair’s traffic into 16 flows and Enhanced-ECMP hashes them across paths; deep-buffer spine switches absorb collective bursts, and the cluster runs without DCQCN congestion control.

4D parallelism (§3.3.2). Order [TP, CP, PP, DP(FSDP)] from highest- to lowest-bandwidth domain. Notable choices: FSDP does not reshard weights after forward (saves a backward all-gather); the pipeline schedule makes the number of contiguous micro-batches N tunable between depth-first and breadth-first, strips one Transformer layer from first/last stages to balance embedding/loss compute, and uses an interleaved schedule with async point-to-point; context parallelism is all-gather-based rather than ring — affordable precisely because GQA makes K/V tensors small, and flexible enough for the document mask. Result: 43% / 41% / 38% BF16 MFU at 8K / 16K GPUs / 128K-token sequences (Tab.4), with 8K-token pre-training running without activation checkpointing. FP32 gradient accumulation and reduce-scatter guard numerics.

Reliability (§3.3.4, Tab.5). 466 interruptions in 54 days (419 unexpected; 78% hardware, 58.7% GPU — faulty GPUs 30.1%, HBM3 17.2%; SDC 6 events); at least one automated-maintenance interruption daily; only 3 incidents needed manual intervention. Tooling: NCCL flight recorder, straggler prioritization, NCCLX (an NCCL fork) co-designed with PyTorch for hang localization. Two physical curiosities: 1-2% diurnal throughput swing from midday temperatures throttling GPUs, and tens-of-megawatt datacenter power oscillations when all GPUs synchronize on checkpoints or startup.

Data curation (§3.1). A custom HTML parser tuned for math/code preservation (markdown markers are removed — they hurt web-trained models); URL → MinHash document → aggressive line-level dedup (removing lines seen 6+ times per 30M-doc bucket); heuristic filters (duplicated-n-gram ratio, dirty-word counts, token-distribution KL); model-based quality filters (fasttext Wikipedia-reference classifier; DistilRoberta regressors trained on Llama 2 judgments), plus dedicated code/math extraction pipelines. Annealing doubles as a cheap data evaluator: anneal a half-trained 8B on 40B tokens with 30% of the candidate mixed in and read off the benchmark delta (§3.1.3).

Capability data engines (§4.3). Code: a “code expert” made by continuing pre-training on 1T tokens at 85%+ code; 2.7M synthetic SFT dialogs where generated solutions must pass parser/linter + model-generated unit tests in a sandbox, with error feedback rounds (~20% of initially wrong solutions self-correct). Math: skill taxonomy, answer-filtered and self-verified traces, outcome + stepwise reward models to prune bad intermediate steps, MCTS for hard prompts. Multilingual: an expert branched with 90% multilingual continued pre-training; translated data avoided except for math (translationese). Long context: only 0.1% synthetic long-context SFT data is needed — and DPO can stay entirely short-context. Tools: Brave Search / Python / Wolfram Alpha with message-level human preference annotation; zero-shot function-calling data mined from the Stack. Factuality: knowledge probing that teaches the model to refuse what it consistently gets wrong (“know what it knows”) rather than injecting facts.

Main result

Llama 3 405B Instruct performs on par with GPT-4 (0125) across the board — e.g. MMLU 0-shot CoT 88.6 vs 85.4, MATH 73.8 vs 64.5, IFEval 88.6 vs 84.3 — and sits within a few points of GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet, while the 8B and 70B are best-in-class at their sizes (Tab.2, p.3). Blind pairwise human evaluation confirms rough parity with GPT-4-0125 (win rates within the margin of error), with losses to GPT-4o on multilingual prompts and to Claude 3.5 Sonnet on coding and reasoning (Fig.17, p.41).

Key findings

  • Scaling laws were made decision-grade (§3.2.1, Fig.2-4): IsoFLOPs runs (6e18-1e22 FLOPs) give N⋆(C)=ACαN^\star(C)=AC^\alphaN⋆(C)=ACα with (α,A)=(0.53,0.29)(\alpha, A)=(0.53, 0.29)(α,A)=(0.53,0.29), prescribing 402B params / 16.55T tokens at the real budget — and the two-stage NLL→accuracy fit predicted the flagship’s ARC score almost exactly. Crucially, the IsoFLOPs minima flatten at large budgets, so the exact size barely matters — which is why they could round to 405B for other reasons.
  • The complexity bet paid off (§9.1, Tab.2): the dense 405B outperforms every open MoE (Mixtral 8x22B, Arctic), and DPO beat PPO in their scaling regime (“less compute, performed better, especially on IFEval”, §4.1.4) — evidence that at the frontier, stability and iteration speed are worth more than architectural cleverness.
  • Self-improvement is capability- and scale-dependent (§4.3.1, §3.1.3): training 405B on its own raw generations does not help (and can hurt); it becomes useful only once execution feedback filters the data — while 8B/70B gain plainly from 405B-generated data. Symmetrically, annealing on GSM8K/MATH train data lifts the 8B by +24.0%/+6.4% but does nothing for 405B, which no longer needs in-domain samples.
  • The contamination self-audit undercuts standard benchmarks (Tab.15, §5.1.4): HellaSwag is ~85% contaminated with an estimated ~14-point performance gain; AGIEval is 98% contaminated; and the 8-gram method outright fails on MMLU, MMLU-Pro, HumanEval, and MBPP. Adversarial variants (Fig.15) show math/QA scores drop substantially off-distribution while PAWS-style paraphrase adversariality is now solved.
  • Quantization damage is invisible to benchmarks (§6.2, Fig.26): naive FP8 passes standard evals yet occasionally emits corrupted text (high-perplexity tokens like dates spike dynamic scales into underflow). The fix — cap scales at 1200, row-wise scaling, skip first/last layers and attention — was validated by comparing reward-model score distributions over 100K responses, not benchmarks; FP8 then gives up to 50% prefill throughput on one 8-GPU node.
  • Safety capacity scales (Fig.18, §5.4): smaller models need a larger fraction of safety data to reach the same violation/false-refusal trade-off, larger models discern adversarial vs borderline better; system-level Llama Guard 3 cuts violations by -65% on average at a real false-refusal cost (+102% FRR for full filtering, Tab.25-26). Uplift studies for cyber and chemical/biological attacks found no significant uplift over internet-only controls — but 405B complies with code-interpreter-abuse prompts 10.4% of the time and prompt injection succeeds 21.7% of the time (Fig.22).
  • Compositional multimodality works without touching the LLM (Tab.29-32, §7-8): frozen-backbone adapters reach GPT-4V-level vision and better-than-Whisper speech, guaranteeing text performance is untouched — an explicit architectural counter-position to natively-multimodal Gemini.

Implications

Llama 3 reset what “open model” means: GPT-4-class weights plus the recipe, spawning the entire 3.1-derivative ecosystem (distillation targets, synthetic-data teachers, Llama Guard-based safety stacks) and legitimizing the over-train-small-models strategy (train far past compute-optimal for inference efficiency, as Mistral/LLaMA-1 pioneered). The systems sections became reference material the way PaLM’s and Gemini’s infrastructure notes were, but with unprecedented specificity. The post-training flywheel — rejection sampling + execution/RM verification + DPO — is the direct ancestor of open reasoning-model pipelines; whether verifier-filtered self-generation scales into the RL-heavy reasoning training that followed (o1-style), and whether the dense-over-MoE bet survives the next compute jump (Llama 4 reversed it) are the live questions. Also worth chasing: their scaling exponent α=0.53 implies notably more tokens-per-parameter than Chinchilla’s α≈0.5 — evidence that the “optimal” ratio is data-distribution-dependent, and reward-distribution-based evaluation as the right tool for detecting distribution shift benchmarks miss (quantization, decoding changes).

Limitations

  • Open weights, closed data: the 15T-token corpus is described only qualitatively (no sources, no release), so the decisive ingredient — data — is not reproducible (§3.1).
  • Multimodal (vision/video/speech) results are experimental and the models were not released (§7-8), so those comparisons could not be externally verified.
  • Safety comparisons use internal benchmarks with anonymized competitors — the authors state these numbers are not externally reproducible (§5.4.4, footnote 13).
  • Author-stated human-eval caveat: results are sensitive to tone, verbosity, and annotator preference, and ties are excluded (§5.3).
  • Long-context capability is thinner than “128K” suggests: needle tasks are saturated, but InfiniteBench En.QA F1 is only 30.5 for the 405B — long-range reasoning, not retrieval, remains open (Tab.21).
  • Red teaming found multi-turn escalation, mixed-language prompts, and tool-forcing attacks still produce violations; larger models generate more insecure code on CyberSecEval (§5.4.5-5.4.6).

Critiques and Questions

  1. The contamination audit is commendably honest but self-undermining: the method fails exactly on the headline benchmarks (MMLU, MMLU-Pro, HumanEval, MBPP), so Table 2’s flagship comparisons carry unquantified contamination on all sides. The organizational mitigation (a separate, contamination-incentivized data team, §10) is asserted, not auditable.
  2. “Dense beats MoE” (§9.1) is a cross-lab observation, not an ablation — Llama 3 405B vs Mixtral 8x22B differs in data, compute, and post-training simultaneously. The claim that simplicity, not sparsity, wins is a plausible reading of the evidence, not a controlled result (and Meta itself moved to MoE the following generation).
  3. Similarly, DPO-over-PPO is reported as a scaling/compute observation without a controlled comparison or numbers; given how consequential this claim became for open post-training practice, the evidence in the paper is thin.
  4. Competitor numbers mix API self-collection, reported values, and “best of both” per cell; GPT-4o/Claude comparisons on some rows use different prompting regimes (Tab.2 footnotes). Fairer than most, but still not a fixed-protocol evaluation.
  5. The 405B is called “approximately compute-optimal”, yet the fitted law says 402B/16.55T — the herd’s smaller models are then deliberately trained far past optimal. The paper never quantifies how much quality the 8B/70B leave on the table versus a distillation strategy (Phi-style), which §9.1 names as the alternative.
How did the team predict Llama 3 405B's benchmark accuracy before training it, given that scaling laws normally predict only next-token loss from small-compute runs?
A two-stage fit (§3.2.1): first, IsoFLOPs experiments between 6e18 and 1e22 FLOPs identify compute-optimal models and establish a linear relation between training FLOPs and the normalized negative log-likelihood of correct answers on the downstream task; second, a sigmoidal relation maps that NLL to task accuracy, fit using both the scaling-law models and older Llama 2 checkpoints (which add high-compute anchor points from a different data mix). Composing the two extrapolates FLOPs to accuracy over four orders of magnitude — on ARC Challenge the forecast only slightly underestimated the flagship's final score (Fig.4).
