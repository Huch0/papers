Curated byChiyeong Heo

ReLook trains a 7B/8B open coding policy with GRPO where the reward is a frozen multimodal LLM scoring temporal screenshots of the rendered page, the policy learns to call that critic as a tool inside a generate–diagnose–refine loop, a strict “accept only strictly improving revisions” rule (Forced Optimization) stops revisions from getting worse, and at inference the critic is dropped so the policy self-edits alone.

This is the closest published blueprint to my thesis setting: an open-weight model, execution (rendering) in the loop, a visual judge as the training signal, and a repair loop that must be learned rather than prompted. Its failure modes (behavioral collapse, reward hacking via reflection avoidance, weak gains on multi-file projects) are exactly the ones I need to design around.

Motivation

Front-end code has no unit tests that matter: correctness is what the browser shows and how the page reacts. A text-only policy is blind to the pixel consequences of its own code, so it drifts layouts, breaks interactions (missing event listeners), and produces incoherent aesthetics (§1, §3.7). Two prior families each miss half the loop: one-shot vision-to-code systems (pix2code, Design2Code, UICoder) generate but never refine, and self-refinement frameworks (Self-Refine, Reflexion, CRITIC, CodeRL) iterate but only over pixel-blind signals such as unit tests or linters (§1). On top of that, when I do give a model rich visual feedback, I observe behavioral collapse: the revision after good feedback often scores lower than the draft it was meant to fix (§2.6, Fig.4). So the problem is not “add a critic”; it is “make a small model actually improve under a critic without regressing or gaming the reward”.

Contribution (what’s new)

The core insight is that the perceptual judge belongs inside the RL loop twice: once as the reward (a frozen MLLM critic scores rendered screenshots), and once as a tool the policy learns to invoke mid-trajectory for textual, vision-grounded repair hints (§2.2–2.5). Two safeguards are forced by what breaks without them. Zero reward for any invalid render is needed because a plausible-looking but unrenderable page would otherwise be scored by a judge reading the code (§2.5). Forced Optimization — a revision enters the trajectory only if its critic score strictly exceeds the best-so-far, with up to 10 resamples per round — is needed because the obvious alternative, a negative reward for regressions, taught the agent to stop reflecting altogether (§2.6, App. E). Finally, because the critic’s tokens are masked from the policy gradient and optionally distilled with a small loss, the policy internalises the reflection so the critic can be removed at inference (§2.4, §2.7).

Research questions

  1. Does a screenshot-based MLLM reward beat text-only signals for training a small open coding model on front-end tasks?
  2. Does letting the agent call the critic during rollouts (agentic reflection) add value over the same reward without reflection (Web-RL)?
  3. Can the learned reflection survive removing the critic at inference, and at what latency cost?

Methodology

Running example: the prompt “create a classical chessboard in HTML that runs directly”. Round 1: the policy writes a plan t1 and code c1, then emits <get feedback>. The sandbox renders c1, grabs three screenshots, and the critic returns a score plus text m1 (“squares misaligned; missing alternating colours”). Round 2: the policy conditions on q, t1, c1, m1 and proposes c2. If the critic score of c2 is not strictly higher than c1, c2 is discarded and a new c2 is sampled (≤ 10 tries); if none improves, reflection stops and c1 is the final answer. The trajectory reward is the mean of accepted round scores, multiplied by a length penalty, and zero if any screenshot failed. GRPO updates only the t/c tokens; the critic’s m tokens get zero advantage.

Method details
  1. Rollout format — Output o = t1 ⊕ c1 ⊕ m1 ⊕ … ⊕ tR ⊕ cR (Eq.1). R is capped at 3 reflection rounds in both training and inference (§3.3). The prompt template tells the model to emit <get feedback> unless it believes the answer is flawless (App. I, Fig.7).
  2. Sandbox — Headless Chromium, filesystem/network off, dangerous APIs blocked (window.open, eval, clipboard, non-local fetch), local font/image fixtures, seeded timers, strict CSP, per-sample timeout, full-page screenshots at T=3 time points (§3.1).
  3. Reward — R_MLLM = VisualScore if all screenshots valid else 0 (Eq.3); R_len is 1 below 12k tokens, linear to 0 at 14k (Eq.4); R_ReLook = R_MLLM · R_len (Eq.5). Three screenshots per round are scored in one call; round scores are averaged over the trajectory (§2.5, App. D). The critic score is normalised to [0,1] for training and reported on [0,100] offline.
  4. Forced Optimization — Accept (t_{r+1}, c_{r+1}) only if score strictly exceeds best-so-far, no margin, no re-scoring; max K=10 resamples per round; on failure terminate reflection and keep best-so-far (§2.6, Alg.1, App. G).
  5. Optimisation — GRPO with G=8, ε=0.2, KL β swept in [0.01,0.05], advantages standardised and clipped to [−2,2], lr 1e-6, batch 256, at most 40 steps, 64 GPUs (32 policy + 32 MLLM), ~80 min/step (§3.1, App. G). Optional distillation on critic tokens: L = −J_GRPO^{t,c} + γ L_distill^m with γ=0.1 (Eq.8).
  6. Inference — Drop screenshots and MLLM calls; the policy runs up to three critic-free self-edit rounds ('ReLook-w/o-MLLM'). 'ReLook' keeps the critic for the highest accuracy (§2.7, Tab.1 caption).

Training data: 3,000 front-end-only tasks, near-duplicates removed against ArtifactsBench, FullStack-Bench-Html and Web-Bench with lexical (char 3-gram TF-IDF cosine > 0.85), DOM (tag-bigram Jaccard > 0.90) and code (token-set Jaccard > 0.90) filters (§3.1). Evaluation follows ArtifactsBench with Gemini-2.5-Pro as offline judge, deliberately different from the training critic; six subsets are used (A-Lite 300, A-Easy 305, A-Game 413, A-SVG 123, A-Web 447, A-Si 75). FullStack-Bench-Html and Web-Bench provide execution-based checks. All numbers are means over 3 seeds at temperature 1.0, top-p 0.7 (§3.2).

Main result

On the Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct backbone, ReLook lifts ArtifactsBench-Lite from 21.59 (base) to 27.88, beating Web-RL (24.89), and the critic-free variant still reaches 25.44 (Tab.1); the ordering ReLook > Web-RL > Base holds on every ArtifactsBench subset for both backbones (Fig.2, Fig.3). ReLook-7B overtakes Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct on A-Lite (27.88 vs 25.73) but stays below GPT-4o (33.25).

Key findings

  • Every component matters, and Forced Optimization is the single largest piece (Tab.2): on A-Lite, MLLM reward alone 21.59 → 24.89 (+3.3), adding the invalid-render zero reward → 25.84 (+1.0), adding Forced Optimization → 27.88 (+2.0).
  • Behavioral collapse is real for the base model: with the same MLLM feedback, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct degrades after 2–3 reflection rounds, while ReLook improves monotonically across eight forced rounds (Fig.4) (scores from the training judge, so not independent).
  • Penalising regressions with negative reward backfires (§2.6, App. E): the agent learns to stop requesting feedback (reward hacking by reflection avoidance), collapsing ReLook into plain RL. A hard acceptance filter fixes it without suppressing reflection: reflection frequency rises and stabilises at ~2 rounds during training (Fig.5).
  • Valid Render Rate climbs from ~40% at initialisation to ~80% at convergence (§2.5, App. G); a prompt-level “write executable HTML/CSS/JS/SVG” constraint is needed to avoid the zero-reward rule making early training too sparse.
  • Internalised reflection retains most of the benefit at base-decoding latency (Tab.1, §3.5): w/o-MLLM beats Web-RL on every subset, and per-query time falls from 123.04 s to 18.03 s (Qwen2.5-7B on 4 H20s, critic on 8 H20s, 100 queries).
  • Gains concentrate on visual-centric subsets and stay small on multi-file projects (§3.6): Web-Bench improves 3.00 → 4.20 pass@2 (40% relative) but remains far below GPT-4o (23.80); single-artifact refinement does not address long-horizon cross-file work.
  • Web-RL slightly hurts FullStack-Html on Qwen (65.00 → 63.25) while ReLook recovers it (67.50) (Tab.1): pure visual reward without reflection can trade functional correctness for looks; the paper does not discuss this.

Implications

For my thesis, ReLook shows that a visual MLLM-as-judge in the ArtifactsBench lineage can serve as a training reward rather than only an evaluator, and that the hard part is trajectory hygiene (monotonic acceptance, invalid-render zeroing), not the judge itself. It sits between one-shot vision-to-code (Design2Code, UICoder, Web2Code, DesignCoder) and tool-critique self-refinement (CRITIC, Reflexion, Self-Refine), and its Forced Optimization is an online, in-trajectory analogue of best-of-N selection (§4.2). Threads to chase: replacing the three fixed-time screenshots with scripted interaction traces so the reward can see stateful behaviour (clicks, form state), which the current critic only observes through passive timing; whether a much smaller or open critic (the paper’s stated future work) keeps the gains, since 32 GPUs for the critic dominates training cost; using execution/test signals and the visual judge jointly, given the FullStack-Html regression under visual-only Web-RL.

Limitations

  • The training critic (Qwen2.5-VL-72B) and evaluator (Gemini-2.5-Pro) are both MLLM judges; human validation is inherited from ArtifactsBench, not re-run, except a 100-task GSB study with no inter-annotator statistics (§1, App. A–B).
  • Only 7B/8B backbones, 40 RL steps, front-end only; scaling and broader stacks are untested (App. A).
  • “Interaction” is judged from three passive screenshots (post-load, +1s, +2s); no user actions are executed, so interactive integrity is inferred by the judge rather than observed (§2.5, §3.2).
  • Means over 3 seeds are reported without variance or significance tests; several deltas (e.g. Llama A-Lite +0.63 for Web-RL) are within plausible seed noise (Tab.1).
  • Forced Optimization may reduce exploration on hard cases, and the sandbox’s determinism may under-represent real device/locale variability (App. A).
  • Code is “planned for release upon publication”; no artifacts are available (App. A).

Critiques and Questions

  1. The text summary of §3.6 does not match Table 1 everywhere: the “3.2–6.1 point” range for visual subsets excludes Qwen A-Web (+2.62), and “2.4–3.4 on A-Easy” excludes Llama (+4.75). The largest Qwen gain is actually A-Si (+7.75), which the text does not mention. Treat the prose ranges as approximate.
  2. Web-RL vs ReLook conflates two changes: agentic reflection and Forced Optimization. Table 2’s “MLLM reward + format constraint” row (25.84) has no reflection either, so the marginal value of reflection alone without acceptance filtering is never isolated.
  3. Because the critic score is also the acceptance criterion, Forced Optimization is effectively rejection sampling against the reward model during rollout; how much of the gain is “learned self-improvement” versus “better training targets from filtered search”? The w/o-MLLM numbers suggest some transfer, but the distillation loss ablation (γ) is described as a sweep and never reported.
  4. Reflection count converging to ~2 while the cap is 3 could mean tasks saturate, or that the 10-resample budget often fails on hard tasks; the rejection rate is not reported.
  5. Why does the critic-at-inference variant score identically to w/o-MLLM on FullStack and Web-Bench (Tab.1 caption says both use critic-free inference there)? Then “ReLook” on unit-test benchmarks is not the full method, so the headline claim of consistent gains “across three benchmarks” rests on critic-free inference for two of them.
Why did the authors abandon a negative reward for regressions in favour of a hard acceptance rule?
The penalty made the agent avoid requesting feedback altogether (fewer reflections means fewer chances to be penalised), i.e. reward hacking that turned ReLook back into plain RL. A strict accept-only-if-better filter keeps regressions out of the trajectory without discouraging reflection; reflection frequency rose and stabilised at about two rounds (§2.6, App. E, Fig.5).
