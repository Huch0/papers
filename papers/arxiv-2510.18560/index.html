A meta-evaluation benchmark of 654 rubric-annotated human preference pairs over running web apps: the best LLM judge agrees with experts 70.3% of the time versus 84.6% between experts, and neither screenshots, rubrics, nor an interactive GUI agent closes the gap.

The cleanest measurement so far of how far an AI judge sits from a human on interactive web artifacts — the “the checker, not the generator, is the bottleneck” evidence — and its protocol (pairwise vs single-answer, a feasibility-verification unit set) is the template for scoring any new checker, including a programmatic one, against ground truth.

Motivation

We built this because everyone now uses a model to grade models, and that habit has only been validated where the grader looks at a finished, static thing. A web app is not static: its quality is defined by what happens when you click, type, and submit, and the task is open-ended — there is no single reference answer. Existing judge benchmarks either grade text (MT-Bench, JudgeBench) or pre-scripted trajectories (AgentRewardBench); ArtifactsBench watches screenshots but never drives the page (§2). Worse, the raw preference labels we started from were not trustworthy ground truth: two experts re-annotating 100 pairs without guidance agreed only 65.0% of the time (with ties), and agreed with the original crowd labels 53.0% of the time (Tab.1). Any judge scored against such labels would be scored against noise.

Contribution (what’s new)

The insight that survives deleting “we propose” is that a query-grounded rubric tree turns a subjective preference into something two people can agree on: with rubrics, inter-annotator agreement jumps to 92.0% (human-written) and 90.0% (LLM-generated) (Tab.1), and the final set reaches 89.7% (§3.2). Because LLM-generated rubrics are almost as good as hand-written ones, we could annotate at scale — the trees average 29.9 verifiable leaf nodes (Tab.10). The second novelty is the environment: every pair is deployed, so a judge can be given the code, a screenshot, or a live page to operate — which is what lets us test agentic judges at all. The third is WebDevJudge-Unit, 502 task-feasibility questions that isolate one skill — “can this page actually do X?” — from the holistic preference (App.D).

Research questions

Can an LLM-as-a-judge replace human preference on an open-ended, interactive task? And which levers — comparison paradigm, guidance format, observation modality, agency — move agreement (§4)?

Methodology

Start from webdev-arena-preference-10k (10,501 pairs), remove duplicates (6,730), filter queries for safety, clarity, and interaction requirements (2,460), deploy every implementation in a Next.js sandbox and drop failures (1,713), sample 700, and annotate with rubric trees by two software-engineering annotators (654 after manual filtering) (Tab.8). Then run 17 (M)LLMs under two paradigms — pairwise comparison and single-answer grading — plus an agentic pipeline: a planner turns the rubric into test cases, a GUI agent (UI-TARS-1.5, up to 15 steps) executes them on the live page, and a summarizer decides (Eq.1, Tab.11).

Controlled variables
  • Guidance: direct judgment vs Likert dimensions (functionality, UI, code, interactivity; App.C.2) vs the rubric tree with weighted leaf pass rates (intention 1 : static 3 : dynamic 2).
  • Observation: screenshot only, code only, both (Tab.4).
  • Bias: single-pass by design; swap-debiasing reported separately (Tab.13).
  • Feasibility: WebDevJudge-Unit — 279 feasible / 223 infeasible tasks; code-only LLMs vs the trajectory-driven agent (Tab.6, Tab.12).

Main result

No judge reaches human reliability: GPT-4.1 tops out at 70.34% pairwise agreement against 84.56% human–human, and larger models plateau around 70% rather than scaling toward it. (Tab.3) The agentic workflow, which was supposed to be the natural fit for an interactive task, lands at 60.55% — below a plain code-reading model.

Key findings

  • Pairwise beats single-answer by more than 8 points on average, and it is also more stable: inter-model consistency exceeds 75–80% pairwise but sits at 50–65% under single-answer grading. (§4.1, Fig.5–6) Relative judgment removes the need for a calibrated absolute scale, which models do not have.
  • In the pairwise setting, guidance barely matters: direct judgment, Likert, and rubric all land near 70%, and reasoning models do slightly better with no rubric at all. (Fig.3) In single-answer grading a binary rubric clearly beats a Likert scale (without ties: GPT-4.1 74.5 vs 69.7, Tab.14).
  • Code is the modality that carries the judgment: withholding it costs Claude-4-Sonnet 10.7 points pairwise (59.48 vs 70.18), while withholding the screenshot costs at most 2.6. (Tab.4)
  • The agentic pipeline loses to vanilla models because errors compound: plans are too generic or too specific for ambiguous queries, and the executor misreads pages or fails to reach states. (§4.1)
  • Feasibility checking splits into complementary failures: code-only LLMs have high recall and low precision (GPT-4.1 P 72.1 / R 90.0), the interactive agent the opposite (UI-TARS-1.5 P 82.4 / R 70.3) — it labels feasible tasks infeasible because of its own operational failures (Fig.11). (Tab.6) A gated ensemble (agent OR LLM on dynamic leaves) adds only 1.2–1.3 points (Tab.7).
  • Positional bias survives explicit instructions (GPT-4.1 prefers the second position 15.8% of the time under direct judgment) and is not an artifact of ambiguous ties; swap-debiasing does not raise agreement. (Tab.5, Tab.13)
  • Judges miss functional equivalence — a “Presentation” grading row is rejected because the rubric said “Organization” — even when instructed to accept equivalents. (Fig.4, Fig.10)
  • Dropping tie cases lifts every judge (GPT-4.1 pairwise 83.49%), so ambiguous comparisons are the primary error source. (Tab.14)

Implications

For anyone using an AI judge as a reward or a gate on generated interactive programs, this paper fixes the instrument’s error bar at roughly 15 points below a human, and shows that giving the judge hands (an agent) makes it worse rather than better. It sits with ArtifactsBench, AgentRewardBench, and JudgeBench as meta-evaluation, and with WebRISE and PlayCoder as the AI-judged behavioral checkers it calls into question. WebDevJudge-Unit is a ready-made ground truth for measuring a programmatic checker’s precision and recall on the same feasibility questions; and the functional-equivalence failure is exactly what a check over exposed state (rather than labels or selectors) sidesteps.

Limitations

The annotators are two of the authors, so the 89.7% agreement is within-team, and the benchmark is 654 pairs of React/Next.js apps from one arena. The agentic workflow is a straightforward planner–executor–summarizer with 5–15 step budgets and was deliberately not optimized (§5, Tab.11). Preference labels remain subjective by construction (App.A); “human 84.56%” is inter-annotator agreement, not accuracy against a ground truth.

Critiques and Questions

  1. Rubric trees were generated by gemini-2.5-pro and can be over-specific for general queries (Fig.9, right); some of the functional-equivalence failures are arguably rubric failures, which flatters the humans who were told to read past the wording.
  2. The best models’ 70% is close to the annotator-vs-crowd agreement of 77.4% without ties (Tab.1) — how much of the residual gap is model error versus the irreducible subjectivity the authors acknowledge?
  3. Agreement is measured on 3-way labels dominated by ties as the error source; a benchmark restricted to clearly-decidable pairs would isolate judge competence better.
Why did the interactive agentic judge score below a plain code-reading LLM?
Errors compound across planner → executor → summarizer: plans are too generic or too specific for ambiguous queries, and UI-TARS-1.5 misreads pages or fails to reach states, so it marks feasible tasks infeasible (recall 70.3%, Tab.6). Its combined-rubric agreement is 60.55% versus GPT-4.1's 70.34% (Tab.3).
