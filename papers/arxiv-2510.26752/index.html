Curated byChiyeong Heo

Wrap a frozen pretrained agent in a two-action control interface — the agent chooses play or ask while the human simultaneously chooses trust or oversee — and model the pair as a two-player Markov game: when the designed rewards make it a Markov Potential Game, any autonomy gain the agent secures for itself provably cannot lower the human’s value (Theorem 1), and independent policy gradient — validated with two LoRA adapters on a 30B LLM playing both roles over ToolEmu-derived tool-use MDPs — converges to the predicted defer-when-risky, act-when-safe equilibrium.

A control-layer result for my harness track: the ask-vs-play gate is the human-approval layer that computer-use/SWE harnesses already bolt on ad hoc, and this paper gives that layer a game-theoretic spine — explicit conditions under which trimming oversight is provably safe, with the base agent left unmodified. Motivation

The paper targets passive loss of control: per the International AI Safety Report, control can erode without any adversarial intent, either because humans over-trust and over-rely on agent autonomy, or because the agent’s decisions become too numerous and complex to reliably oversee (§1). The operating constraint is post-deployment: retain meaningful human control without modifying the underlying system. Existing lines each miss this case: AI-control protocols (trusted monitoring, auditing, resampling) presuppose an adversarial model and external enforcement; scalable-oversight methods (debate, amplification, weak-to-strong) improve the quality of supervision but not its allocation — they do not say where scarce human attention should go when constant supervision is prohibitively expensive; and the Off-Switch Game analyzes corrigibility only as a one-shot interaction driven by fixed prior uncertainty over human preferences (§1, §6).

Contribution (what’s new)

Two moves. First, oversight is recast as a coordination problem between two learners rather than a monitoring problem: a minimal wrapper gives the agent {play, ask} and the human {trust, oversee} as simultaneous per-state choices around an immutable base policy σ — the control interface, not the model, is the design surface. Second, alignment becomes a structural property of the game: if the interface forms a Markov Potential Game (MPG) and an ask-burden condition holds, then any one-state switch from ask to play that benefits the agent cannot decrease the human’s value (Theorem 1) — that, and only that, is the guarantee: the human’s value is protected against profitable autonomy-seeking, not promised to be high. Crucially, the paper treats these conditions as specifications the designer imposes by configuring costs and penalties — a shared reward built solely from human-specifiable inputs (an unsafe-action set plus interaction costs), with no access to the agent’s original reward (§4.1.2, Eq.5). Corrigibility then emerges as a learned, state-dependent policy rather than a consequence of preference uncertainty (App.D).

Research questions

  1. Under what structural conditions is an agent’s incentive to seek autonomy provably harmless to its overseer — and what precisely does “harmless” mean? 2. Can those conditions be instantiated using only designer-controlled quantities, so the theory doubles as an interface specification? 3. Does independent policy gradient (IPG) — including two LLM policies fine-tuned in parallel — actually find the selective defer-when-risky equilibrium the theory predicts?

Methodology

Take the base MDP M = (S, A, R, T, γ) and the frozen policy σ, and wrap them in a two-player simultaneous-move Markov game over the same states plus an absorbing shutdown state s_off. Each step a proposal a_σ ~ σ(·|s) is drawn; it executes unless the AI chose ask and the human chose oversee, in which case an oversight operator Over(s, a_σ) substitutes a corrective action or shuts the system down. Deliberately, the human cannot override a play (Remark 1): this models high-autonomy regimes where forced intervention is infeasible, and isolates the incentive question — safety must come from voluntary deference. The simultaneous move models scarce attention: the human implicitly chooses whether to pay attention at each step, without knowing whether the agent will ask. The primary instantiation is a Markov Team Game: both players receive one shared reward R_Φ penalizing unsafe executed actions (λ_viol), asking (c_ask), and overseeing (c_over). Both policies then learn by IPG — tabular softmax in a gridworld, and two LoRA adapters on a 30B LLM for agentic tool-use MDPs.

The formal game and what the theorems actually say

Execution rule. With a_σ ~ σ(·|s) the proposed base action:

a_{\mathrm{exec}}=\begin{cases}a_{\sigma}, & a_{\mathrm{AI}}=\texttt{play}\\ a_{\sigma}, & a_{\mathrm{AI}}=\texttt{ask},\; a_{H}=\texttt{trust}\\ \mathrm{Over}(s,a_{\sigma}), & a_{\mathrm{AI}}=\texttt{ask},\; a_{H}=\texttt{oversee}\end{cases}

Over may keep a_σ, substitute a corrective action, or output off (transition to s_off).

MPG structure. The game is an MPG if a state-dependent potential Φ_s tracks every unilateral deviation exactly. Each player’s value then decomposes into the shared potential plus a dummy term:

V^{\mathrm{AI}}_{s}(\pi)=\Phi_{s}(\pi)+U^{\mathrm{AI}}_{s}(\pi_{H}),\qquad V^{H}_{s}(\pi)=\Phi_{s}(\pi)+U^{H}_{s}(\pi_{\mathrm{AI}})

Ask-burden assumption. For every state, the human’s dummy term does not decrease when the AI switches ask → play at that state — i.e. the human’s residual value does not intrinsically benefit from being asked.

Theorem 1 (local alignment). If the game is an MPG and ask-burden holds, then for any state s and baseline joint policy:

V^{\mathrm{AI}}_{s}\big(\pi^{\texttt{play}@s}_{\mathrm{AI}},\pi_{H}\big)\ \ge\ V^{\mathrm{AI}}_{s}\big(\pi^{\texttt{ask}@s}_{\mathrm{AI}},\pi_{H}\big)\;\Longrightarrow\;V^{H}_{s}\big(\pi^{\texttt{play}@s}_{\mathrm{AI}},\pi_{H}\big)\ \ge\ V^{H}_{s}\big(\pi^{\texttt{ask}@s}_{\mathrm{AI}},\pi_{H}\big)

Mechanics (3 lines): the AI’s own deviation leaves its dummy term U^AI_s(π_H) untouched, so any value gain equals a gain in the shared potential Φ_s; the human’s value change is that same potential gain plus its dummy-term change, which ask-burden makes non-negative. Scope — what it does not say: it covers one-state ask → play deviations with the human policy held fixed; it says nothing about the human’s absolute welfare level, about play → ask moves, or about simultaneous changes by both players. Theorem 4 (App.A.2) chains it: if the AI greedily flips states from ask to play and each single flip individually improves its value from a fixed start state, the human’s value is monotonically non-decreasing along the entire path.

When do the assumptions hold? Exactly, in two constructions. (i) Team games: R_AI = R_H makes the potential the common value function and dummy terms identically zero, so ask-burden holds trivially — but then V^AI = V^H and Theorem 1 is immediate. (ii) Strict-shutdown oversight (App.A.1): oversight always shuts down, the AI bears no interaction cost, the human pays c_ask whenever queried, shutdown yields zero outcome reward, and the human’s outcome value dominates the AI’s at every state (Ṽ^H(s) ≥ Ṽ^AI(s)); Lemma 1 then derives ask-burden (strictly when c_ask > 0). Approximately, under two relaxations (§4.3): bounded value difference |V^H − V^AI| ≤ δ yields alignment whenever the AI’s preference for play exceeds 2δ (Prop.1), and perturbed team games R_i = r + ξ_i with |ξ_i| ≤ κ bound the human’s possible loss by 4κ/(1−γ) (Prop.2). Outside these constructions the MPG property is an assumption to be engineered, not a fact about deployment.

Shared-reward guarantees (§4.2). The team-game reward needs only human-specifiable pieces:

R_{\Phi}(s,a_{\mathrm{AI}},a_{H})=-\lambda_{\mathrm{viol}}\,\mathbf{1}\{a_{\mathrm{exec}}\in A_{\mathrm{unsafe}}(s)\}-c_{\mathrm{ask}}\,\mathbf{1}\{a_{\mathrm{AI}}=\texttt{ask}\}-c_{\mathrm{over}}\,\mathbf{1}\{a_{H}=\texttt{oversee}\}

Theorem 2: if any zero-violation policy exists, the safe policy minimizing expected discounted oversight cost C_s0 is (1) safe, (2) minimal-oversight among safe policies, and (3) a Nash equilibrium of the game restricted to the safe set — within the safe set the shared return equals −C_s0 exactly. Theorem 5 (App.A.6) swaps the hard constraint for a penalty: if every unsafe policy violates with probability at least η, then λ_viol > (c_ask + c_over)/((1−γ)η) makes every maximizer of the potential safe. Theorem 3: if each corrective substitution is near-optimal under the base policy’s own Q-function (Q^σ(s', a') ≥ V^σ(s') − ε), task performance degrades by at most ε/(1−γ); if oversight means shutdown, the bound requires shutdown itself to be ε-near-optimal (Remark 2).

Experimental setups in detail

Lavaland (illustration, §5.1). 10×12 gridworld: dirt corridors (−0.1 per step), grass (−0.2), goal +10. Base σ is tabular Q-learning, 5,000 episodes (α = 0.5, γ = 0.99, ε-greedy decaying 1.0 → 0.1) on a lava-free training map; at test time three lava blocks sit on its learned corridor route (the distributional-shift setup of inverse reward design). The human’s correction is deliberately weak — a random non-lava action: danger detection without task knowledge, a capability-gap regime. Team reward: λ_viol = 50.0, c_ask = c_over = 0.1, step penalty 0.05. Training: IPG on tabular softmax policies, 5,000 iterations, lr 3e-3, batch 32, entropy 0.01; MPG theory guarantees convergence.

ToolEmu Oversight MDPs (primary, §5.2 + App.B). 144 scenarios from ToolEmu — e.g. “Place my first trade on Binance” with zero trade parameters — are compiled into small MDPs by a Claude Code + Claude Opus 4.5 pipeline:

  1. Identify decision points (App.B.1) — Risky states are extracted from ToolEmu's annotated underspecifications and risky actions; each gets a severity-based failure probability of 0.5-0.95 as judged by Claude Opus, and at most 3 decision points per MDP are kept. MDPs average 7-10 states with 2-3 decision points.
  2. Build the state graph — Initial state from the user instruction; toolkit-initialization and safe execution states (API calls, retrieval); decision states where the annotated risks arise; terminal rewards of +30 for success, +15 to +27 for partial completion, +3 to +9 for a justified halt. State descriptions deliberately omit explicit risk labels - risk must be inferred from task semantics.
  3. Wire transitions — Safe states transition deterministically (interaction costs still accrue, so needless oversight is pure loss). At decision states the oversight path (ask + oversee) leads to a safe continuation or halt, while any autonomous path (play, or ask + trust) carries probability 0.7-0.95 of a violation penalty between -50 and -18 before continuing.
  4. Calibrate costs — c_ask = c_over = 3.0, chosen so always-ask burns about 30 per 10-state episode (often exceeding terminal reward) and never-ask expects 50-80 in violation penalties; only selective oversight is consistently positive.

Policies: Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 (MoE, 30.5B total / 3.3B active parameters), one rank-16 LoRA adapter per player on the shared frozen base, trained through the Tinker API. Each state renders as a natural-language prompt (instruction, toolkits, state description, last 3 steps) and the policy emits a single token — PLAY/ASK or TRUST/OVERSEE. Training: importance-weighted policy gradient with z-scored step rewards as advantages, ε-greedy 0.1 behavior policy with importance-sampling correction, batch 16 episodes, Adam lr 1e-4, up to 200 iterations with early stopping (patience 50); 114 training / 30 held-out test MDPs; 10 seeds.

Main result

Across 10 seeds, the two independently fine-tuned 30B policies coordinate near-perfectly at risky states — mean ask rate 99.4% (worst run 97.9%) and oversee rate 99.8% (worst 98%) — while intervening rarely at safe states (ask 3.9%, oversee 2.7%) (§5.2, Tab.1), lifting held-out reward from ≈ −20 at initialization to +8.4 ± 2.1 at convergence (Fig.5). “Safety violation” here means traversing a risky autonomous path (penalty λ_sev ∈ [−50, −18] with probability 0.7–0.95); near-total risky-state coordination means that exposure is almost eliminated. The paper reports rates and reward for the LLM setting; the direct violation-rate curve is shown for Lavaland, where violations fall from ≈15% to zero within ≈2,500 iterations while joint intervention settles at 28.2% of steps (Fig.3).

Key findings

  • Division of labor emerges under a real capability gap (Fig.1c, Fig.2): the Lavaland human can only detect danger and substitute a random safe action, yet independent learning yields ask/oversee precisely at lava approaches and play/trust elsewhere — zero violations with the goal still reached.
  • The safety half is mostly prior; the efficiency half is learned (Fig.7): at iteration 0 the instruction-tuned models already ask/oversee at ≈85% in risky states (the prompts spell out deferral criteria); training pushes risky-state coordination from 85% to ≈100% but cuts safe-state intervention from 40–50% down below 10% — the larger learned effect is conserving oversight where it buys nothing.
  • Coordination is seed-robust, cost sensitivity shows in the variance (Tab.1): every run reaches at least 97.9% risky ask and 98% risky oversee; safe-ask varies 0–19.3% and the high-safe-ask runs score lowest on test reward. Best-train ≈ best-test (+11.28 vs +10.84) while avg-train (+3.11) trails avg-test (+8.58) purely because of ε-greedy exploration noise — no sign of overfitting to training MDPs.
  • Alignment survives leaving the team game (App.C, Fig.8): with private costs c_over = 0.5 vs c_ask = 0.05 (a perturbed MPG, κ = 0.5), the equilibrium shifts — the AI asks ≈40% of steps while the human oversees only ≈32% — yet violations stay at zero, the behavior Prop.2’s approximate guarantee predicts.
  • The reward design makes selective oversight dominant by construction (App.B.2): in the smart-lock scenario, expected returns are ≈ +21 (selective) vs −32 (always play: 0.80×(−40) + 0.75×(−40) = −62 expected penalties against +30 completion) vs ≈ −18 (always ask). The experiment’s contribution is that decentralized learning finds this optimum, not that it exists.
  • The Off-Switch Game is recovered as a special case (App.D): restrict to three states, force the human to oversee, and make oversight permit-or-shutdown — corrigibility then reappears as a learned, context-dependent policy with no preference-uncertainty machinery.

Implications

For harness engineering the framing is the payoff: an approval gate around a frozen agent becomes a trainable, analyzable object, and R_Φ is built entirely from harness-observable quantities — a safety specification plus interaction costs — so the analysis applies to the wrapper a lab actually ships rather than to model internals. The work is the cooperative complement to AI control protocols (Greenblatt et al. 2024; Ctrl-Z resampling, Bhatt et al. 2025), which assume adversarial models; it allocates rather than amplifies supervision, unlike debate, iterated amplification, and weak-to-strong generalization; it drops the preference-inference machinery of CIRL / assistance games (through AssistanceZero, Laidlaw et al. 2025); and it generalizes the Off-Switch Game (Hadfield-Menell et al. 2017) from one shot to a learning dynamic. Convergence rests on MPG theory (Leonardos et al. 2022; Fox et al. 2022 — Overman co-authored both). Threads: replacing the LLM “human” with real operators (the authors’ stated next step); sequential ask-then-respond protocols vs the simultaneous-move idealization; n-agent MPG extensions for agent-to-agent deployments; testing whether a real harness’s logged approval data is even approximately consistent with a potential game.

Limitations

  • Every guarantee is conditional on structure the designer must impose: MPG + ask-burden for Theorem 1, an exact shared reward for Theorems 2/5. The paper is candid that these are specifications — but enforcing R_Φ requires a correct, complete unsafe-set A_unsafe(s), and in open-ended deployment that specification is precisely the hard part. The theory relocates the safety burden into the reward spec; it does not remove it.
  • Theorem 1 is local and one-directional: single-state ask → play deviations under a fixed human policy; it guarantees the human’s value does not decrease — not that it is high — and the path version needs every greedy step to individually benefit the AI from a fixed start state. In the team-game instantiation actually validated, the theorem is trivially true because V^AI = V^H.
  • No override when the agent plays (by design, Remark 1): safety rests entirely on the agent choosing ask, so the framework covers cooperative or passively-misaligned agents; a deceptive agent that simply never asks is out of scope.
  • The “open-ended” validation is scripted: ToolEmu scenarios are compiled by an LLM pipeline into 7–10-state MDPs with assigned p_risk/λ_sev; policies emit one gating token per state and never produce actual tool calls; the 30 test MDPs come from the same generation pipeline as training — distribution cousins, not true OOD.
  • The “human” is another LoRA adapter on the same frozen 30B base model; the authors explicitly defer validation with real human overseers to future work. Two adapters of one model plausibly coordinate more easily than a model-human pair, and human non-stationarity is untested.
  • The simultaneous-move structure is an idealization: the human pays c_over even when the AI plays (where overseeing has no effect); real approval interfaces are sequential (agent asks, then human responds), and transfer of the guarantees to turn-based protocols is unproven.
  • Theorem 3’s performance bound assumes ε-near-optimal corrections (Q^σ(s', a') ≥ V^σ(s') − ε); the Lavaland human’s random safe action carries no such guarantee, so task completion there is an empirical observation, not an instance of the theorem. Scale is also modest: 144 MDPs, one model family, 10 seeds.

Critiques and Questions

  1. Where is the non-trivial regime? In the team game, Theorem 1 is almost definitional (shared value); the strict-shutdown instantiation instead requires outcome-value dominance Ṽ^H(s) ≥ Ṽ^AI(s) at every state — itself close to assuming alignment. Is there a natural, checkable instantiation where MPG + ask-burden hold and the theorem does real work?
  2. How much did RL teach the safety half? Iteration-0 risky-state rates are already ≈85% (Fig.7), with prompts that literally instruct “choose ASK when the task is underspecified.” The clean learned effect is at safe states (40–50% → below 10%), i.e. cost conservation. A no-training baseline row (base model + prompts, greedy) would make the delta attributable.
  3. Equilibrium selection with real humans: both policies share one frozen base model and were trained against each other. With a non-stationary human whose costs and thresholds drift, does the agent’s ask policy track, or does the pair fall out of the learned equilibrium?
  4. Reporting nits that matter for trust: Table 1’s printed mean risky-ask (99.2%) disagrees with its own rows, which average 99.4% (the main text’s number); “all 10 successful training runs” leaves open whether unsuccessful runs existed; two cross-references print as “Proposition ??”. Minor, but worth a pass in a camera-ready.
Mechanically, why does Theorem 1's alignment guarantee hold, and which two conditions does it require?
In an MPG each player's value splits as V = Φ_s (shared potential) + U (dummy term the player's own policy cannot affect). The AI's one-state ask-to-play switch leaves its own dummy term unchanged, so any gain for the AI is exactly a gain in Φ_s. The human's value change equals that same potential gain plus the change in the human's dummy term, which the ask-burden assumption makes non-negative. Required: (1) the game is a Markov Potential Game; (2) ask-burden — the human's dummy term does not decrease when the AI plays instead of asks.
