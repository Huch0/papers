This ICML 2026 position paper gives AI reasoning an operational, method-agnostic definition — the iterated, exact application of learnable rules to beliefs and evidence, emitting an auditable state trace — and argues that a reasoning process’s validity comes from exact rule application alone, independent of how rules are selected and of external truth (soundness).

I evaluate agent “reasoning” constantly; this framework supplies vocabulary for separating harness-induced behavior (deterministic tool steps are auditable rule applications) from model reasoning (an unvalidated selection policy), and its “reasoning zombie” construct names why benchmark scores alone can never establish reasoning. Conceptual anchor of my ICML 2026 advisor-review picks.

Motivation

“Reasoning” in generative AI has no agreed operational definition — definitions are imprecise, overloaded, misaligned with historical treatments, or absent entirely (slides p.7). This breeds mismeasurement, an illusion of shared understanding, and unverifiable construct validity: claims of emergent reasoning are commonplace while a cited survey concedes “there is not a clear definition of what it entails”, goalposts shift, and strong benchmark performance gets conflated with reasoning itself (slides p.8). The deeper evaluation problem: LRMs’ black-box design and natural-language interface make it hard to differentiate true reasoning from reasoning-like speech — outputs may arise from memorization, guessing, or Clever Hans effects rather than reasoning (slides p.8). The field has drifted “from proofs to benchmarks”, and benchmarks suffer weak construct validity, contamination, gaming, and selective reporting — AI evaluation “has yet to mature into a proper ‘science’” (slides p.16). Stakes: reasoning is a necessary (not sufficient) precondition for AGI, and usership of LRMs outpaces evidence of trustworthiness (slides p.11).

Contribution (what’s new)

An operational, method-agnostic definition of reasoning as a process of exact rule application — not an output — over evolving states of beliefs, evidence, and rules, where the rules themselves are learnable, defeasible, and updatable by meta rules (poster; slides p.18-33). The definition must take this form because (a) product-based evaluation cannot separate reasoners from emulators — only process-level criteria can (slides p.13-16); and (b) restricting rules to fixed symbolic axioms would exclude neural and probabilistic systems, whereas the authors see no justification for “reinventing reasoning” for generative AI and explicitly reject the symbolic-vs-deep-learning dichotomy (slides p.9; project site). On top of the definition sit two decouplings: validity (internal: each transition arises from applying a rule in the current rule set) is separated from soundness (external: premises true under evaluation), and both are separated from rule selection — any properly-typed rule application is valid regardless of the algorithm that chose it (slides p.31-32). Deliverables: Algorithm 1 with an auditable trace (plus a runnable notebook in the repo), the r-zombie construct, and a 23-item community checklist for communicating AI reasoning research (checklist App. A).

Research questions

What should “reasoning” mean in AI, operationally, such that construct-valid measurement becomes possible — and can one definition span symbolic, neural, and neuro-symbolic methods? What makes a reasoning process valid, and how does validity relate to soundness, to how rules are selected, and to goal attainment?

Methodology

This is a definitional/argumentative paper, not an empirical one. The method: distill seven core ingredients of reasoning from historical treatments (Broome’s “rule-governed operation”, logic, bounded rationality) — process, goal, rules, evidence, prior beliefs, current beliefs, evolving states (slides p.21-28); formalize them as typed components plus an explicit algorithm (slides p.29-33); demonstrate method-agnosticism by instantiating six disparate domains in the same schema (slides p.34); and derive reporting norms as a checklist (App. A). A key design choice: the reasoner’s goal is encoded in the stopping rule, and validity is deliberately not tied to attaining it (slides p.23).

The formal machinery (slides p.29-33; poster)

State at step t is a triple of beliefs, evidence, and rules; reasoning is iterated rule application:

St:=⟨Bt,Et,Rt⟩,Bt=r(Bt−1,Et) for some rule r∈Rt−1S_t := \langle B_t, E_t, R_t \rangle, \qquad B_t = r(B_{t-1}, E_t) \ \text{for some rule } r \in R_{t-1}St​:=⟨Bt​,Et​,Rt​⟩,Bt​=r(Bt−1​,Et​) for some rule r∈Rt−1​

Rules are operators whose operands are information — evidence is extrinsic (a stream, possibly fixed at t=0), beliefs are intrinsic (intermediate conclusions of prior steps; defeasible, e.g. overwritten during backtracking). Two rule types: local rules typed B × E → B update beliefs; meta rules typed R × B × E → R update the rule set itself — rule learning and revision are first-class. The reasoner adds selectors (local s_L, meta s_M), a stopping rule s_stop : S → {0,1}, and a trace writer. Rules can be theorems, functions, policies, decision boundaries, premises — including rules for stochasticity, uncertainty, and approximation: “exact” means executing the rule as specified, not determinism (slides p.18, 24). In the paper’s own numbering, informal reasoning is Def. 2.1, reasoner Def. 2.2, formal reasoning Def. 2.4, validity Def. 2.7 (per README and checklist cross-references); other numbered definitions are Unclear.

  1. Ingest evidence — Advance the extrinsic evidence stream: E′ ← E_t+1. Evidence may come from interaction with an environment (as in RL) or be given retrospectively.
  2. Select, then apply, a local rule — Selector s_L picks a local rule from the current rule set; beliefs update by exact application: B′ ← r_L(B, E′). Selection is where agency, intelligence, or creativity enter — random selection is admissible; execution demands exactness and rigor (slides p.22).
  3. Select, then apply, a meta rule — Selector s_M picks a meta rule; the rule set updates: R′ ← r_M(R, B′, E′). This is where learning and rule revision live.
  4. Write the auditable trace — Append tr(S, r_L, r_M, S′) to the reasoning trace, advance the state, loop until s_stop fires; return final beliefs plus the trace. The repo notebook implements this loop for a toy logic domain to show validity can be audited in code (README).

Key findings

  • Validity, soundness, and rule selection are three independent axes (slides p.31-32): a transition is valid iff it arises from applying a rule in the current rule set; it is additionally sound iff all premises in beliefs/evidence/rules hold under external evaluation; and validity is invariant to the selection algorithm, “regardless of external ground truth”. A valid reasoner can be wrong — and validity is auditable without ground truth.
  • Agency lives in selection; validity lives in execution (slides p.22-23): rule selection is where intelligence or creativity enter (random selection is explicitly admissible); execution requires exactness. Validity is also “not necessarily tied to successful attainment of a goal” — valid ≠ successful.
  • “Learnable” is structural, not rhetorical (poster): rules are operators over information, split into local rules (update beliefs) and meta rules (update the rule set), so learned, defeasible, continuously-updated rules — and stochastic/approximate ones — are inside the definition, not exceptions to it.
  • One schema instantiates six domains (slides p.34), from logical deduction through RL to Turing machines and probabilistic next-token prediction — the demonstration of method-agnosticism (and, flip side, of near-universality; see Critiques).
  • The r-zombie makes behavioral evaluation insufficient in principle (project site): a system that “superficially behaves as an autonomous reasoner but lacks the internal mechanisms necessary for validity” is output-indistinguishable from a reasoner, so any purely output-level benchmark under-determines reasoning claims. Formal treatment in the paper: Unclear.
  • The 23-item checklist operationalizes the position as reporting norms (checklist App. A p.17): notable items — 2.3 (is each new belief provably obtained by exact rule application? hypotheses required absent proof), 2.4 (provenance of rules: learned vs axiomatic; how meta-rule updates are obtained), 3.1-3.2 (construct validity of evals justified against the stated definitions; metrics must measure both process quality and output quality), 4.3 (are reasoning traces theoretically guaranteed faithful to the internal process?), 4.6 (no excessive claims in titles/abstracts/marketing).

Implications

If adopted, the burden of proof for “model X reasons” inverts: authors must declare the rule set, beliefs, and evidence up front, then show transitions arise from exact rule application — benchmark scores alone can never establish reasoning because r-zombies exist. For my work the sharpest payoff is an agent-systems reading: in a tool-using agent, the harness’s deterministic tool executions are exact rule applications with a faithful-by-construction trace (tool logs are the trace of Algorithm 1), while the LLM plays rule/action selector — so per T3 the process can be valid regardless of the opaque, stochastic selection policy, and validity claims attach to the harness layer while “the model reasons” claims need separate mechanism-level evidence. Checklist items 3.2 (process metrics, not just output metrics) and 4.3 (trace faithfulness) transfer directly to agent evaluation, where CoT is unfaithful but tool logs are not. The paper sits in the construct-validity position lineage (Herrmann et al. ICML 2024; Wallach et al. ICML 2025; Alaa et al. on medical LLM benchmarks) and the evaluation-science program (Weidinger et al. 2025), against the benchmark-crisis literature (LiveBench; “Benchmarking is Broken”, NeurIPS 2025) and the Clever Hans line (Lapuschkin et al. 2019), with philosophical grounding in Broome’s Rationality Through Reasoning (2013), Chollet’s process-vs-product distinction, and Simon’s procedural rationality. Read the PhilArchive full text for the formal r-zombie definition and the Alternative Views section; run the repo notebook and try recasting one of my agent-eval traces as declared rules + audit; compare with Hendrycks et al.’s AGI definition (cited as R1) for how the two operationalizations compose.

Limitations

  • No empirical component in the available materials: no case study applying the definition or checklist to a real LRM/agent system end-to-end; the only implementation is a toy logic notebook. Whether the paper contains worked LLM case studies: Unclear.
  • Verification inside opaque models is deferred, not solved: for an end-to-end LRM, checking that a belief “arises from” exact rule application requires faithful traces or mechanism identification; the checklist concedes this by accepting hypotheses in lieu of proof (2.3) and treating trace faithfulness as an open reporting item (4.3).
  • Discriminative power depends on the declared rule level: the schema’s own Turing-machine and next-token rows show essentially any computational process is a valid reasoner over some rule set; exclusion is delegated to checklist 4.5’s contextual judgment (“useful, nontrivial reasoning beyond standard ML inference”), which is not formally defined.
  • Source coverage: the full paper (PhilArchive) was inaccessible; the abstract, Alternative Views, formal r-zombie definition, and Defs. 2.3/2.5/2.6 could not be recovered — marked Unclear throughout.

Critiques and Questions

  1. What does the definition exclude? By the authors’ own p.34 table, a Turing machine — hence every terminating program, including vanilla LM decoding — instantiates valid reasoning over machine-level rules. The operational content therefore lives less in the definition than in the declaration discipline the checklist imposes (which rule set a claim quantifies over, stated before evaluation). That is a real contribution, but it means “valid reasoning” is a property of a system under a description, not of a system simpliciter — the paper’s title thesis is weaker than it sounds without the checklist attached.
  2. “Arises from” must be causal-mechanistic or the r-zombie contrast collapses. Memorized replay of a proof produces transitions extensionally identical to rule applications; if consistency-with-a-rule sufficed, the replayer would be a valid reasoner and the r-zombie would be incoherent. The slides never state the causal reading explicitly (Unclear whether the paper does) — this is the load-bearing philosophical joint.
  3. Does it operationalize measurement or relocate it? Split verdict: for systems with explicit declared state (provers, Bayesian pipelines, agent harnesses), validity auditing is genuinely mechanical — Algorithm 1’s trace makes it a log-check needing no ground truth. For the LRMs that motivated the paper, it converts “does it reason?” into a mechanism-identification problem that interpretability cannot yet answer — a measurement contract, not a measurement procedure. The honest contribution is relocating the null hypothesis: absent process evidence, claim emulation, not reasoning.
  4. Validity is a floor, not a merit. A system looping a typed identity rule reasons validly forever while going nowhere; validity certifies procedural integrity, not competence or progress (those live in the goal/stopping rule, soundness, and unmodeled selection quality). Their own 4.6 (claim hygiene) cuts both ways: “provably valid reasoning” would make excellent misleading marketing.
  5. What would count as a counterexample? Either a consensus reasoner with no non-trivial rule description (intuitive/analogical leaps — the framework risks absorbing these post hoc as “learned rules”, which flirts with unfalsifiability), or a consensus non-reasoner satisfying domain-level validity (the replayer of #2). The authors anticipate the objections I can see in the slides — symbolic revanchism (p.9, learnable/defeasible rules), stochasticity (p.22, random selection admissible), success-conflation (p.23) — but the official Alternative Views rebuttals are Unclear from the available materials.
An agent selects rules with an opaque neural policy, applies each selected rule exactly, but one premise in its evidence stream is false. Is its reasoning process valid? Is it sound?
Valid: every transition arises from exact application of a rule in the current rule set, and validity is independent of the selection algorithm and of external ground truth (slides p.32). Not sound: soundness additionally requires all premises encoded by beliefs, evidence, and rules to be true under external evaluation (slides p.31).

Personal notes

The keeper idea for my agent-eval work: harness = rule-application layer (tool logs are a faithful trace; validity is auditable), model = selection policy (validity does not depend on it). That split turns “did the agent reason?” into two separable questions with different evidence standards. Use checklist 3.2/4.3 as review questions for agent-eval papers.
